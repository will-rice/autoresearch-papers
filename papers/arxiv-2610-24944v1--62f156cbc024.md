###### Abstract

We present Épi, an autonomous research agent that sustains
investigations across model sessions and evolves its methods through
accumulated experience. Épi combines persistent research history,
hierarchical memory, branching and independent review to maintain
research continuity, learn from experiments, and explore and revisit
alternative approaches. Its experiments run across session boundaries,
and a fixed verifier evaluates their outputs. Épi produced 120
record-setting results, comprising 20 numerical bound records across 12
families and 100 construction records across eight families. These
include an approximately 84-fold narrower interval for the $`n`$-queens
constant and a more than 3.6-million-fold narrower rigorous interval for
the quartic Feigenbaum attractor dimension. Exact dual certificates
establish that the Cohn–Elkies linear-programming bound cannot be sharp
in dimensions 10 and 11. Beyond numerical improvements, Épi gives a
computer-assisted proof of Tuza’s conjecture for split graphs with an
eight-vertex clique part and at most three triangle-active neighborhood
types, with arbitrary multiplicities. We extend this research process to
algorithmic coding with Épi-turbo, a lightweight configuration that
develops executable solvers for tasks including vehicle routing,
scheduling and production planning. On 30 FrontierOR tasks, Épi-turbo
achieves a 5.4% higher total benchmark score than Codex at 64% of its
model cost, using the same underlying model and reasoning effort. Épi is
designed to support human understanding by connecting computational
results to explicit methods and mathematical arguments that researchers
can examine and build upon.

## 1  Introduction

Autonomous research agents must conduct experiments, interpret results
and revise their methods over many interactions. FunSearch and
AlphaEvolve demonstrate how agents can combine code generation with
executable evaluation to discover new programs and constructions
([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib36); [Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)). The AI Scientist-v2 extends automation to an
experimental workflow ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)). When an
investigation requires more work than one model session can support,
however, the agent must manage three additional challenges.

#### First, maintaining research continuity

Experiments and research decisions span multiple model sessions. To
continue an investigation, the agent needs access to running jobs, code,
intermediate results and reasons for earlier decisions. Kosmos maintains
a shared research state, InternAgent-1.5 organizes long-horizon memory,
and ScienceFlow archives recoverable execution states ([Mitchener
et al., 2025](#bib.bib27); [Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Zhao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)). These mechanisms preserve information across
steps. As the history grows, the agent must also select what a bounded
model context needs: the current experimental state, relevant lessons
and reasons an earlier approach failed. This need motivates hierarchical
memory that separates immediate context from reusable experience.

#### Second, learning from experimental experience

An agent needs to turn experimental observations into knowledge that
guides subsequent decisions. This requires identifying which choices
produced a result, under what conditions the method applies and which
evidence supports the lesson. The agent must retain this knowledge and
retrieve it for relevant tasks. MemoHarness addresses experience reuse
by building a memory bank during training and retrieving execution
diagnoses and reusable patterns to adapt the harness at test time
([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)). In an ongoing research
investigation, the agent must also update its memory as new experiments
arrive, linking each lesson to the conditions and evidence that support
it.

#### Third, exploring alternatives and backtracking

A single research direction explores only part of the available methods.
The AI Scientist-v2 uses agentic tree search to develop experimental
alternatives, and ScienceFlow can resume from archived executable states
([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)).
These systems demonstrate the value of branching and backtracking. For
research with checkable outputs, the agent must preserve each
alternative’s implementation and evidence, compare candidates under a
common evaluation rule and recover earlier work when new findings make
it useful.

To address these challenges, we introduce Épi, a persistent and evolving
autonomous research agent. Épi uses hierarchical memory to resume
investigations and retrieve experience from earlier work. It maintains
versioned branches to develop alternative methods and backtrack to saved
implementations, and incorporates independent review when assessing
evidence and choosing its next experiment. The agent operates in
verifiable research environments that evolve between _campaigns_, where
a campaign is a complete investigation that may span multiple model
sessions. Within a campaign, every branch uses the same feasibility
checks, objective and comparison value. A verified finding can then
establish a new reference or motivate a new task for the next campaign.
Persistent execution and storage carry the research state across
sessions.

Mathematical discovery provides a concrete demonstration of this
research process: the agent develops methods whose outputs can be
checked as constructions, bounds or proofs. We also evaluate its
applicability to algorithmic coding, where the agent must design and
implement a solver that handles new problem instances efficiently. Our
lightweight configuration, Épi-turbo, achieves a 5.4% higher total
benchmark score than Codex on 30 FrontierOR tasks at 64% of its model
cost, using the same underlying model and reasoning effort
(Section [6](#S6 "6 Épi-turbo: Fast, Low-cost Algorithm Design ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research")).
These tasks include routing, scheduling, production planning and graph
optimization.

Our contributions are an autonomous research agent that integrates
hierarchical memory, branching and independent review to sustain and
refine its investigations; a verification protocol that evaluates
changing research methods under a fixed ruler and gain floor; and a
collection of 120 record-setting results, comprising 100 improved
constructions and 20 numerical bounds. Each record improves a documented
literature or registry reference for the same problem and normalization;
we count one final result per instance or parameter setting, as detailed
in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Experimental Results ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research").
These results include an approximately 84-fold narrower interval for the
$`n`$-queens constant and a more than 3.6-million-fold narrower rigorous
interval for the quartic Feigenbaum attractor dimension. Exact
certificates establish that the Cohn–Elkies bound is not sharp in
dimensions 10 and 11. Épi also proves Tuza’s conjecture for split graphs
with an eight-vertex clique part and at most three triangle-active
neighborhood types, with arbitrary multiplicities. We release the
constructions and supporting certificates ([Epi Team,
2026](#bib.bib12)).

Our goal is to connect self-improvement with human understanding:
researchers should be able to inspect how a method evolves, explain why
a result holds, and use that knowledge to guide further work. Épi
retains implementations, experimental artifacts, and research notes
across branches and sessions, allowing researchers to inspect the
development of a method and assess its supporting evidence. Its
constructions, certificates, and mathematical arguments further connect
computational outputs to findings that researchers can examine and build
upon
(Section [8](#S8 "8 Autonomous Research for Human Understanding ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research")).

## 2  Research Setting

### 2.1 Problems and Evidence

We study research tasks with a reference result, an explicit budget and
outputs that can be checked. A curator supplies the question and source
material; the agent chooses the methods and experiments. To compare the
agent’s output with the reference, we specify an evaluation rule, called
a _ruler_. The ruler defines the submission format, feasibility
conditions, objective, normalization and comparison value.¹¹ 1 A
reported benchmark score alone does not specify feasibility conventions,
normalization or input validation. These choices determine whether two
values measure the same quantity on valid objects. We introduce the
ruler to make them explicit before search.

The agent submits the information required to check its claim. For a
packing task, it supplies coordinates and orientations. For an
optimization bound, it may supply a dual vector or another certificate.
For a proof task, it supplies witnesses and an argument explaining why
the checked objects establish the stated claim.

For a minimization problem, we write $`V(x)\in\{0,1\}`$ for the
verifier’s feasibility decision, $`f(x)`$ for the objective and $`b`$
for the reference value. We define the improvement as
$`\Delta(x)=b-f(x)`$. We also specify a positive _gain floor_
$`\delta>0`$, the smallest improvement that the task counts as
meaningful.²² 2 Counting every positive score change as progress can
include differences due to reporting precision or routine
post-processing. We derive the floor from the objective’s granularity
and known transformations, and record its rationale with the task. For
an integer objective with unit granularity, a floor of one admits a
one-unit improvement. Épi accepts an improvement when

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       V(x)=1,\qquad\Delta(x)\geq\delta.
       ```                                |     | (1) |

For maximization, we instead define $`\Delta(x)=f(x)-b`$. The positive
floor excludes ties and admits an improvement exactly equal to the
threshold. If a task imposes no minimum gain ($`\delta=0`$), we instead
require $`\Delta(x)>0`$. We fix the feasibility tolerances and gain
floor before the investigation. The verifier uses the tolerances to
check constraints, while Épi uses the floor to decide whether the
improvement counts. For a rigorous numerical bound, we additionally
require exact arithmetic or outward rounding that controls the numerical
error.

We establish a new record by verifying feasibility and improvement over
the best established result for the same problem and normalization. Each
final artifact retains its comparison value, source and any rounding
convention. For a theorem, the proof connects the verified objects to
the full stated claim.

### 2.2 Campaigns and Sessions

We call a complete investigation a _campaign_. A campaign may contain
several agent sessions and concurrent experiments. Each session gives
the agent a bounded context in which to inspect evidence, modify code
and choose its next action. Épi stores the research state outside that
context and keeps experiments running across session boundaries. A later
session retrieves the saved state and resumes the investigation.

The curator may provide a published construction as the initial
reference. The agent can consult the literature, implement alternative
methods and select experiments within the campaign’s budget. We keep
model weights fixed in the campaigns reported here. The agent adapts its
methods through experimental feedback, retrieved memory and independent
review.

### 3.1 Evolving Research Environments

Comparing research methods requires a common objective and consistent
validity checks. We establish these before search by checking that the
scorer reproduces the reference result, testing input validation and
fixing the configuration, feasibility checks and gain criterion. The
verifier then evaluates every branch under this shared rule. This gives
Épi a consistent basis for comparing methods throughout the
investigation.

We also specify the evidence needed for each type of claim. The verifier
checks geometric constraints for a construction. For a rigorous bound,
we require a certificate with exact arithmetic or controlled numerical
error. For a computer-assisted theorem, we require both the finite
checks and a mathematical reduction showing why those checks establish
the full statement. The agent must develop this reduction or certificate
when the task requires one.

Épi’s research environments evolve with verified findings. Improved
results provide new reference values for subsequent investigations,
while new research questions motivate changes to task formulations,
representations and verification requirements. We incorporate these
changes when defining the next campaign. For example, a numerical
estimate can lead to a certification task whose environment includes
certificate representations and rigorous error control. Each campaign
uses a fixed evaluation rule to compare methods, and successive
campaigns build on the verified findings of earlier work.

### 3.2 Self-improving Research Agents

Épi improves its research methods through accumulated experience. The
agent develops and compares alternatives on versioned branches, retains
and retrieves experience through hierarchical memory, and uses
independent review to reassess its research decisions. Together, these
mechanisms allow Épi to refine its approach across experiments and
campaigns.

#### Branches and backtracking

A single approach restricts which formulations and search regions the
agent explores. Épi maintains branches to develop alternatives while
preserving each method’s code and evidence. Each branch stores an
implementation, tests and notes in a versioned repository. The agent
saves changes as commits and can create a new branch from an earlier
commit. It compares the resulting experiments under the same ruler.

The agent backtracks by returning to a saved implementation and trying a
different continuation. It can also revisit an existing branch and
extend the work already recorded there. When the agent abandons a
branch, it records the reason and retains the branch for later
inspection. For a packing problem, the agent might refine coordinates on
one branch and change the contact pattern on another. It can compare
both methods before allocating more compute.

#### Hierarchical memory

Drawing on work on human and agent memory ([Atkinson and Shiffrin,
1968](#bib.bib2); [Packer et al., 2023](#bib.bib32); [Park et al.,
2023](#bib.bib35)), Épi uses hierarchical memory to accumulate and reuse
research experience. Short-term memory supports the current
investigation, while long-term memory retains experience for subsequent
research. As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 The Épi Research Loop ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research"),
memory records include research context, lessons that capture reusable
findings, and dead ends that document unproductive approaches and why
they were abandoned. The agent retrieves these records to guide its next
experiment and updates them as new evidence becomes available.

Épi stores code, artifacts and detailed research notes in a persistent
filesystem and keeps retrievable lessons and dead ends in a memory store
outside the model context. At a session boundary, the agent records its
findings and a handover note identifying the current state and next
steps. A subsequent session receives relevant memory records and can
open the referenced files as needed. This supplies a bounded working
context without replaying the full history of earlier sessions.

#### Independent review

The agent that develops a method also chooses which evidence to examine,
giving its assessment a limited perspective. Prior work on
self-correction motivates adding feedback from outside the agent’s own
reasoning process ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)); research on reward
hacking further motivates checking whether a score increase reflects the
intended task ([Denison et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)). Épi combines a
separate review of research progress with artifact verification under
the fixed ruler.

Épi supplies a review model with the score trajectory, attempted
approaches, retrieved findings, comparison baseline and resource use.
The reviewer assesses what the evidence supports and whether the agent’s
interpretation of progress is justified. Épi records this assessment for
subsequent sessions, and the agent uses it when selecting further
experiments or revisiting a branch. We use a different model for an
independent review and record the reviewer’s identity. The reviewer
evaluates the research direction; the isolated verifier checks the
candidate’s validity and gain.

#### Research transparency

Épi records alternative implementations, their experimental outcomes,
and the assessments used to guide subsequent work. These records allow
researchers to compare branches, inspect the evidence associated with a
methodological change, and revisit the implementation on which a
conclusion depends. Lessons summarize reusable findings, while
references to code and experimental artifacts let researchers examine
the evidence behind those summaries.

#### Execution support

We use Breadboard as Épi’s execution infrastructure. Breadboard provides
isolated experimental environments, supports long-running jobs across
model sessions and stores research history, including code, branch
states and experimental outputs. It also runs the fixed verifier and
preserves the state needed to recover and continue an investigation. The
agent uses these services to execute experiments, inspect results and
resume its research across sessions.

## 5  Case Studies

### 5.1 Refining a Published Packing

The task is to place 34 non-overlapping regular pentagons of unit side
length inside an equilateral triangle of minimum side length. Each
pentagon has a center and orientation. The evaluator checks containment
and overlap and measures the required triangle size.

Épi starts from a published arrangement with triangle side length
approximately $`12.9814`$ ([Friedman, 2026](#bib.bib14)). It jointly
refines the pentagons’ centers and orientations, checking containment
and overlap after each candidate update. The final side length is
approximately $`12.9756`$, a reduction of $`0.0445\%`$. The released
coordinates specify the construction at full precision.

### 5.2 Separating the Cohn–Elkies Bound from Packing Density

The Cohn–Elkies method bounds sphere-packing density from above by
optimizing an auxiliary function. We ask whether its best possible bound
can equal the actual packing density. A strict separation would
establish a limitation of the method itself.

Let $`\rho_{d}`$ be the optimal sphere-packing center density in
dimension $`d`$, and let $`C_{d}`$ be the best upper bound attainable by
the Cohn–Elkies linear program in the same normalization. Then
$`\rho_{d}\leq C_{d}`$. To prove that the method is not sharp, it is
enough to find a packing upper bound $`U_{d}`$ and a lower bound
$`L_{d}`$ on the linear program’s optimum such that

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \rho_{d}\ \leq\ U_{d}\ <\ L_{d}\ \leq\ C_{d}.
       ```                                            |     | (2) |

The middle inequality creates a separation between the true packing
problem and the best answer available from this method.

[Li (2022)](#bib.bib24) obtains such lower bounds through a discrete
reduction of the infinite-dimensional linear program. The reduction
yields a finite optimization problem whose dual witnesses can be
checked. Épi sets new records in dimensions 9, 10, 11, and 13, retaining
one final dual vector for each.

reports the resulting lower bounds on $`C_{d}`$.

### 5.3 Narrowing the Interval for the $`n`$-Queens Constant

The number $`Q(n)`$ of placements of $`n`$ non-attacking queens on an
$`n\times n`$ board has an asymptotic form governed by a constant
$`\alpha`$,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       Q(n)=\left(ne^{-\alpha+o(1)}\right)^{n}.
       ```                                       |     | (3) |

Bounds on $`\alpha`$ can be obtained from finite convex optimization
problems. [Nobel et al. (2023)](#bib.bib29) develop a scalable Newton
method for these formulations and report the interval

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       [1.944000752,\;1.944001082].
       ```                           |     |

Épi refines the lower and upper bounds separately. Rational feasibility
checks and directed rounding give

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

### 5.4 Optimizing a Formulation: MAX-4-CUT

Épi starts from the MAX-4-CUT gadget framework of [Nagda et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib28) and optimizes its edge weights. With the gadget
structure and relevant completeness assignments fixed, the competing
colorings have objectives that are linear in those weights.

This allows the agent to normalize one side of the hardness ratio and
optimize the other through a linear program. Coloring constraints
enforce the required properties of the gadget.

The reference ratio follows from the gadget parameters in Lemma C.2 and
the reduction in Theorem C.2 of [Nagda et al. (2025)](#bib.bib28). The
final integer weighted gadget changes it from

### 5.5 A Finite Construction for the Spencer Constant

The square-matrix discrepancy problem asks how well signs can balance
the rows of a real matrix with entries in $`[-1,1]`$. Épi found an
$`8\times 8`$ matrix whose discrepancy is exactly five. Exhausting its
256 sign assignments gives row-maximum norms of five or seven, so the
minimum is five. The matrix therefore raises the record lower bound on
the universal constant from $`7/\sqrt{17}`$ to $`5/\sqrt{8}`$ ([Tao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib39)).

### 5.7 From Numerical Optimization to Proof: Tuza’s Conjecture

For a finite simple graph $`G`$, let $`\nu_{\triangle}(G)`$ be the
maximum number of pairwise edge-disjoint triangles and
$`\tau_{\triangle}(G)`$ the minimum size of a *triangle cover*: a set of
edges meeting every triangle in $`G`$. Equivalently, deleting a triangle
cover makes the entire graph triangle-free. Tuza’s conjecture, proposed
in 1981 ([Bonamy et al., 2022](#bib.bib3)), asserts

|     |                                              |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tau_{\triangle}(G)\leq 2\nu_{\triangle}(G).
       ```                                           |     | (6) |

Thus, if $`G`$ contains no $`k+1`$ pairwise edge-disjoint triangles,
deleting at most $`2k`$ edges suffices to eliminate every triangle in
$`G`$.

#### Packing and covering

Kőnig’s theorem equates the maximum matching size and minimum
vertex-cover size in a bipartite graph ([Kőnig, 1931](#bib.bib23)).
Tuza’s conjecture concerns a matching–cover relation in the *triangle
hypergraph*, whose vertices are the edges of $`G`$ and whose hyperedges
are its triangles. Taking all edges of a maximum triangle packing gives
the elementary bound $`\tau_{\triangle}\leq 3\nu_{\triangle}`$: any
uncovered triangle could be added to the packing. The conjectured factor
of two is attained by $`K_{4}`$, where $`\nu_{\triangle}=1`$ and
$`\tau_{\triangle}=2`$.

#### Split graphs and prior results

A split graph has a partition $`V(G)=C\sqcup I`$, where $`C`$ induces a
clique and $`I`$ is independent. Known cases include threshold graphs,
whose independent-side neighborhoods are nested by inclusion ([Bonamy
et al., 2022](#bib.bib3)), and $`K_{8}`$-free chordal graphs, which
include $`K_{8}`$-free split graphs ([Botler et al., 2021](#bib.bib4)).
Our eight-vertex clique part puts the family beyond the latter theorem’s
$`K_{8}`$-free hypothesis.

#### Neighborhood types and the extension

For $`x\in I`$, let $`S_{x}=N_{G}(x)\cap C`$. Since $`C`$ is a clique,
every pair $`u,v\in S_{x}`$ forms a triangle $`xuv`$. Thus $`x`$ is
*triangle-active* exactly when $`|S_{x}|\geq 2`$; vertices with
identical $`S_{x}`$ share one *neighborhood type*. [Zeng
(2026)](#bib.bib45) proved the conjecture for $`|C|=8`$ and at most two
types. Épi extends this to three types with unrestricted overlaps. Their
triangles can compete for the same clique edges, requiring joint covers
and packings.

###### Theorem 1 (Three neighborhood types).

Let $`G`$ be a finite simple graph with a specified partition
$`V(G)=C\sqcup I`$, where $`G[C]=K_{8}`$ and $`I`$ is independent.
Suppose that the sets $`N_{G}(x)\cap C`$, over vertices $`x\in I`$ with
at least two neighbors in $`C`$, take at most three distinct values.
Then $`\tau_{\triangle}(G)\leq 2\nu_{\triangle}(G)`$.

Up to clique relabeling and type permutation, there are 872 three-type
profiles: 35 have nested neighborhoods and 277 further profiles satisfy
Zeng’s maximum-cut criterion. Épi constructs exact cover and packing
certificates for the remaining 560 profiles, using 18,444 multiplicity
boxes. An independent checker verifies the witnesses and complete
profile enumeration. Zeng’s truncation lemma extends the checks to
arbitrary multiplicities, establishing the result for an infinite graph
family
(Appendix [A.13](#A1.SS13 "A.13 Tuza’s Conjecture: Proof for Three Neighborhood Types ‣ Appendix A Mathematical Results and Supporting Evidence ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research")).

The proof separates the finite certificate checks from the argument
extending them to arbitrary multiplicities, making explicit how the
computational evidence establishes the infinite-family statement.

## 6  Épi-turbo: Fast, Low-cost Algorithm Design

### 6.1 Purpose and Design

Algorithmic coding extends Épi’s research process to the development of
executable solvers. Given a problem specification and a fixed evaluator,
the agent designs, implements and tests an algorithm whose solution
quality and runtime are measured on new instances. Épi-turbo is a
lightweight configuration for these tasks, designed to develop effective
algorithms within a short campaign and a small model budget.

#### Shared foundation

Épi-turbo keeps the parts of Épi that make a result reliable. The
verifier checks each candidate under a fixed ruler, and Épi keeps the
research state across sessions. Branches keep each method with its code
and evidence, so the agent can return to an earlier version. Memory
records keep lessons and dead ends for later sessions. Épi-turbo changes
how the agent uses its model budget and when the campaign stops.

#### Instructions for algorithm tasks

The task instructions tell the agent how the benchmark measures a
program. They give the complete score formula, which includes the
quality term and the runtime term. They also tell the agent that the
final score comes from instances that the agent does not see. When a
program uses a large part of its time limit, Épi-turbo tells the agent.
Thus the agent can find a good balance between solution quality and
runtime. The instructions do not contain methods or hints for a specific
problem.

#### Model calls for algorithm design

Each model call has a cost, and a short task has a small number of
calls. Épi-turbo tells the agent that every call costs money, which
includes a check on an active job. The agent does short experiments with
a time limit in its own session. It sends an experiment to the execution
infrastructure only when the experiment needs more time than a session
gives. It examines active jobs only when a result can change its next
decision. As a result, most of the budget goes to the design and test of
algorithms.

#### Continuous sessions

Épi-turbo runs each campaign as one continuous session if possible. The
session continues the best method so far and does not restart from an
empty context. Before a session ends, the agent records its state, its
best candidate and its next steps. The next session reads this record
and continues the work. Épi-turbo stops the campaign when more sessions
do not improve the best verified score. Thus the agent does not use more
budget on a task where it finds no improvement.

#### Budget control

Épi-turbo reserves the maximum cost of a model call before the call
starts. It also keeps a small part of the budget for the end of the
campaign. Thus the cost of a campaign is never more than its budget, and
the final steps always have funds. The reservation uses the price limits
of the model, so the agent can use almost all of its budget.

#### Optional components

Independent review and extra validation are optional in Épi-turbo. On
short tasks, their cost was larger than their benefit in our tests. Both
components were off in the runs below.

### 6.2 Benchmark and Protocol

We use FrontierOR, a benchmark of 180 operations-research tasks from the
literature ([Kong et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). Each task asks for a
standalone program that solves new instances of one problem class. The
program must not call an external optimization solver. The baseline is
Codex ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib31)), a widely used coding agent, with
GPT-6-Astra as its model. Épi-turbo uses the same model with the same
reasoning effort.

We selected 30 tasks that were new to both systems. We took them in the
official hardness order of the benchmark and excluded tasks with
unreliable reference values. Of these tasks, 13 are in the hard split,
and 17 have hardness ranks 51 to 70. For each task, each system had a
model budget of \$20. We did one run for each task and system, so we do
not report statistical significance.

The evaluator first runs each program on two development instances. The
agent sees these results during the campaign. After the campaign, the
best development program runs once on four held-out instances. Each
held-out run has a time limit of one hour. The benchmark score has a
quality term and a speed term.

The quality term decreases as the gap to the reference value increases.
The speed term compares the runtime of the program with the runtime of
the reference solver. It counts only when the solution is within 1% of
the reference value. Outside this 1% band, the score is the quality term
alone. The reference values come from a commercial solver, and some of
them are weak. Thus we also compare the two systems on each instance
directly, with their objective values only.

## 7  Related work

Autonomous research systems differ in how they organize the search for a
useful next experiment. The AI Scientist-v2 explores experimental
directions through agentic tree search ([Yamada et al.,
2025](#bib.bib42)). Co-Scientist uses model-generated critique to refine
hypotheses around a research goal ([Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)),
and InternAgent-1.5 coordinates generation with verification across
computational and empirical tasks ([Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)).
These systems share Épi’s need to revise a research direction in
response to feedback. AIRA₂ addresses a related execution challenge
through asynchronous experiments and a consistent evaluation protocol
([Hambardzumyan et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). In Épi, the verifier stays
fixed within a campaign as the agent changes its approach.

Continuing research also depends on how previous work is represented.
Kosmos uses a shared world model to connect literature search and data
analysis ([Mitchener et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)). ScienceFlow preserves
executable research states that can be resumed or revisited ([Zhao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)). Knowledge can also pass between
investigations through reports, as in AgentRxiv ([Schmidgall and Moor,
2025](#bib.bib37)), or through AutoKD’s persistent graph of accepted
findings ([Ge et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Épi retains both the
experimental state needed to resume a campaign and lessons intended for
reuse on another problem. Branches preserve alternative histories within
that campaign.

For tasks with executable evaluation, FunSearch and AlphaEvolve guide
program generation using measured performance ([Romera-Paredes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib36); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)). SimpleTES scales
search through independent trajectories and reuse of evaluated histories
([Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)). Dream-RSI turns recorded discovery
trees into replay environments for evaluating and revising exploration
policies before deploying them in further searches ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)). TTT-Discover adapts model weights at test time
([Yuksekgonul et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)). Épi keeps model weights fixed
and uses stored lessons and branch histories to guide its next
experiment. Released Hyra constructions offer starting points for
further refinement ([Hyra Team, 2026](#bib.bib21)). Épi’s investigations
can also extend beyond refinement to finding a certificate or proving a
statement, as in the Tuza case study.

## 8  Autonomous Research for Human Understanding

Our goal with Épi is to make autonomous research contribute to human
understanding: researchers should be able to explain why a result holds,
identify what it reveals about the problem, and use that knowledge to
guide further work. Achieving this goal requires connecting
computational artifacts to explicit formulations, mathematical
arguments, and the scientific questions they resolve. Épi builds on
existing theory and retains the implementations, experiments, and
assessments through which its methods develop. The case studies
illustrate how this process can produce knowledge that researchers can
use: the Cohn–Elkies separation identifies a structural limit of an
established method; the MAX-4-CUT formulation provides a reusable
procedure for optimizing gadget weights; and the Tuza proof makes
explicit how finite certificates establish a statement about an infinite
graph family. These examples express our broader aim for
self-improvement: an agent should accumulate both the ability to obtain
better results and knowledge that helps people understand and extend
those results.

## 9  Conclusion

Épi is an autonomous research agent that sustains investigations across
model sessions and improves its methods through memory, branching and
independent review. Its campaigns produced 120 record-setting results
and a computer-assisted proof of a new case of Tuza’s conjecture. The
released constructions and certificates provide concrete, checkable
evidence for these advances. Épi-turbo extends this research process to
algorithmic coding across routing, scheduling, planning and graph
optimization. On 30 FrontierOR tasks, it achieves a 5.4% higher total
benchmark score than Codex with the same model while using 64% of its
model cost.

Épi couples method improvement with a persistent record of
implementations, experiments, and review. This record supports
inspection of the research process, while explicit constructions and
proof arguments make the resulting findings available for further
scientific work. Together, these evaluations demonstrate the
applicability of persistent, feedback-driven method development to
mathematical discovery and efficient algorithm design.

### A.12 Water-Network Design: Waterund36

For the original continuous waterund36 minimization problem ([MINLPLib,

It excludes objective values at or below the exact rational cutoff

|     |                                   |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math

       ```                                |     |

The displayed lower bound is rounded downward. Compared with MINLPLib’s

The proof rules out every network cheaper than the cutoff by deriving a

|     |                                                        |     |

evaluated componentwise. Rational support inequalities and bound
propagation eventually yield incompatible bounds under the cutoff
assumption. Containment of the original feasible set in the relaxation
then establishes the global lower bound. The released certificate
supplies the model, witnesses and exact comparisons needed for this
argument.

### A.13 Tuza’s Conjecture: Proof for Three Neighborhood Types

For the partition in
Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Three neighborhood types). ‣ Neighborhood types and the extension ‣ 5.7 From Numerical Optimization to Proof: Tuza’s Conjecture ‣ 5 Case Studies ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research"),
discard independent vertices with fewer than two clique neighbors: they
occur in no triangle. The remaining vertices have at most three distinct
neighborhoods in $`C`$. We call a triple of these neighborhoods,
considered up to relabeling $`C`$ and permuting the three types, a
neighborhood profile.

#### Finite reduction

Zeng’s multiplicity-truncation lemma ([Zeng, 2026](#bib.bib45))
preserves both $`\tau_{\triangle}`$ and $`\nu_{\triangle}`$ when each
type is truncated to 28 copies. Every triangle uses a clique edge, so a
packing uses at most 28 triangles and at most 28 independent vertices of
any type. For covers, all 28 clique edges suffice. If a minimum cover of
the truncated graph has fewer than 28 edges, some copy of every
saturated type has no incident cover edge. All clique edges within that
type’s neighborhood must then be covered, which also covers triangles
through any additional copies. Thus the finite multiplicity range
suffices for the infinite statement.

#### Completeness and certificates

An independent enumeration gives 872 profiles with three distinct active
types. Of these, 35 have nested neighborhoods and fall under the
threshold-graph theorem of [Bonamy et al. (2022)](#bib.bib3). Another
277 satisfy Zeng’s maximum-cut criterion ([Zeng, 2026](#bib.bib45)). The
cases with at most two types follow from Zeng’s theorem. The remaining
560 profiles are covered by exact certificates containing 18,444 integer
boxes in $`\{0,\ldots,28\}^{3}`$.

For a box with componentwise bounds $`\ell\leq m\leq u`$, the
certificate gives a triangle cover $`F_{u}`$ meeting every triangle in
the upper-multiplicity graph $`G_{u}`$ and an edge-disjoint triangle
packing $`P_{\ell}`$ in the lower-multiplicity graph $`G_{\ell}`$,
satisfying $`|F_{u}|\leq 2|P_{\ell}|`$. The cover meets both the
triangles inside $`C`$ and every triangle $`xuv`$ with $`x\in I`$ and
$`u,v\in N_{G}(x)\cap C`$. Adding copies preserves the smaller graph as
a subgraph. Hence

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau_{\triangle}(G_{m})\leq|F_{u}|\leq 2|P_{\ell}|\leq 2\nu_{\triangle}(G_{m}).
``` |  |

A separate checker reconstructs the graphs, enumerates every triangle in
each upper-multiplicity graph, checks that its stored cover meets every
such triangle, and verifies that the stored packing consists of
edge-disjoint triangles in the lower-multiplicity graph. It also checks
the cardinality inequality and coverage of every integer multiplicity
vector. All 560 profiles and 18,444 boxes passed. The certificates cover
the remaining profiles, and truncation extends the conclusion to
arbitrary multiplicities. This proves
Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Three neighborhood types). ‣ Neighborhood types and the extension ‣ 5.7 From Numerical Optimization to Proof: Tuza’s Conjecture ‣ 5 Case Studies ‣ Épi: Towards a Persistent, Self-Improving Agentfor Autonomous Research").
