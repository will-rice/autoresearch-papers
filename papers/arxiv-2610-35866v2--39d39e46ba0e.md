# Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling

###### Abstract

As autonomous research systems become more capable, benchmarks must both
evaluate their performance and support meaningful research progress. We
introduce Auto-Research Arena, a multi-target benchmark for evaluating
autonomous research systems and discovering ideas in language modeling.
Arena supports diverse research objectives within a shared experimental
environment by separating _admissibility_ from _improvement_: model
quality and resource constraints determine whether a result qualifies,
while the selected target measures progress among admissible results. We
instantiate Arena in language model pretraining with nine targets
spanning model, data, training, and inference efficiency, and evaluate
seven research harnesses using two foundation models. Arena clearly
differentiates existing harnesses across research objectives, with
several sophisticated methods outperformed by our simple sequential
search baseline, indicating substantial headroom in current
auto-research systems. Beyond evaluation, Arena reveals a substantially
broader frontier of model and system ideas aligned with active research
directions. Reusing these discoveries in a new quality-driven search
yields lower training loss under matched data and FLOPs and lower
held-out BPB at the same model size. Together, Arena provides a common
framework for benchmarking autonomous research and expanding its scope
in language modeling. The code is available at
[https://github.com/Auto-Research-Arena/auto-research-arena](https://github.com/Auto-Research-Arena/auto-research-arena).

## 1 Introduction

Advances in LLMs and research harnesses have enabled increasingly
capable autonomous research systems ([Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib2);
[Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). These systems iteratively propose ideas,
conduct experiments, and use the results to guide further research.
Auto-research benchmarks provide a common basis for comparing these
systems, but their role extends beyond evaluation. By defining clear
objectives and constraints, a well-designed benchmark helps researchers
identify, validate, and build on useful improvements. However, existing
benchmarks remain limited both in evaluating research systems and in
supporting broad research ideas ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Wijk
et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Nathani et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Karpathy,
2026](#bib.bib6); [Intology, 2026](#bib.bib7)).

To address this gap, we introduce Auto-Research Arena, a comprehensive
benchmark for evaluating auto-research systems and advancing idea
discovery in language modeling. Departing from prior benchmarks that
define progress primarily through a particular objective within each
research problem, Arena defines multiple research objectives within a
common experimental environment. These objectives address complementary
bottlenecks across the model lifecycle, including model size, data
consumption, training computation and memory, and inference memory and
latency. The shared environment provides a common experimental basis for
evaluating whether a harness remains effective across different research
objectives. Meanwhile, different objectives reward different types of
improvements. For example, reducing model size or inference memory can
count as progress even when prediction loss remains unchanged. By
recognizing these complementary forms of progress, Arena supports
exploration of a broader range of model designs and systems
optimizations.

To support this broad range of research objectives within a unified
framework, Arena specifies both what to improve and which requirements a
candidate must satisfy. That is, Arena separates the standard for
_admissibility_ from the direction of _improvement_. Each objective is
formulated as a constrained optimization problem. Constraints on model
quality and relevant non-target resources determine whether a candidate
is _admissible_. Among admissible candidates, a reduction in the
selected resource metric constitutes _improvement_. Changing the target
metric defines a different research objective within the same framework.
This formulation supports consistent evaluation across diverse
objectives, while excluding gains that violate the specified quality or
resource constraints.

Beyond evaluating objectives independently, Arena supports research
across objectives. Separate searches can pursue different objectives in
parallel, each exploring improvements relevant to its target. Compatible
improvements discovered in one search can then be incorporated into the
starting program for a subsequent search targeting a different
objective. This enables both _parallel exploration_ of distinct research
directions and _cumulative progress_ through the reuse of earlier
discoveries. By explicitly specifying objectives, constraints, budgets,
and starting programs, Arena makes it possible to measure how prior
discoveries affect subsequent research.

We evaluate six LLM-based auto-research methods and a Tree-structured
Parzen Estimator baseline ([Ferreira et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) across
Arena’s nine objectives. Arena provides a discriminative and meaningful
evaluation of research harnesses: methods exhibit clear differences
across objectives, with no single harness dominating the benchmark. Two
results further support the evaluation. TPE, specialized for the
original validation loss-oriented setting, performs worst in Arena’s
broader multi-target evaluation. Moreover, our simple Sequential Search
baseline ranks second overall with both foundation models, outperforming
several substantially more sophisticated harnesses and suggesting large
headroom in current auto-research system design. The recorded search
trajectories help explain how these final outcomes arise.
Higher-performing harnesses tend to sustain progress throughout the
search budget, whereas lower-performing methods often plateau earlier or
improve less consistently.

Multi-target research also exposes a substantially broader idea
frontier. We empirically find that different objectives lead to
qualitatively different discoveries, yielding 32 findings across nine
idea families spanning model architecture, optimization, memory, and
inference systems. Many of these discoveries align with active
directions in LLM research. For example, when optimizing parameter
efficiency, agents discover looped Transformers through weight sharing.
They further learn depth-specific residual gains to adapt the residual
updates across repeated applications of the shared block. This finding
closely parallels recent work identifying residual adaptation as a key
ingredient of looped Transformers ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)).
Similar connections appear across other discoveries, although alignment
with existing research does not imply identical solutions. Several
discoveries follow design principles similar to those of prior work but
implement them through distinct mechanisms, offering alternative
approaches to the same research problems. Together, these findings show
how Arena’s diverse objectives expand the range of ideas explored by
autonomous research systems.

Finally, we examine whether ideas discovered through Arena’s lightweight
searches remain useful beyond the search setting. The original searches
train each candidate for only 10 minutes. We provide the collected
discoveries to a new search focused on model quality, which can select,
combine, and modify these ideas. We then train the resulting model for
one hour from random initialization. Unlike the original searches, which
optimize individual efficiency targets, this experiment evaluates
improvements in model quality directly. Relative to the reference run,
training loss decreases from 2.675 to 2.544 at matched training data and
from 2.617 to 2.544 at matched cumulative FLOPs. Held-out BPB also
decreases from 0.9295 to 0.9112 at essentially the same model size.
These results show that discoveries from Arena’s small-scale,
multi-target searches can improve model quality through combination and
adaptation. Arena thus supports both the evaluation of autonomous
research systems and the discovery of reusable research ideas.

## 2 Related Work

Autonomous research benchmarks. PaperBench ([Starace et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)) evaluates replication of published research, while
EXP-Bench ([Kon et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)) assesses the design and
execution of paper-derived experiments. These benchmarks emphasize
implementing and validating specified research content. More open-ended
settings include MLE-bench ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) and
RE-Bench ([Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)), which evaluate ML engineering
and research, alongside MLGym ([Nathani et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)),
MLRC-Bench ([Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib11)), and AstaBench ([Bragg et
al., 2026](#bib.bib12)), which support standardized experimentation and
evaluation across ML and scientific tasks. Within language modeling,
Karpathy’s autoresearch ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib6)) minimizes
validation loss under a fixed training-time budget, whereas NanoGPT
Bench ([Intology, 2026](#bib.bib7)) targets faster training at a
required model quality. Related benchmarks evaluate reproduction of
NanoGPT improvements ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) and
correctness-constrained computational speedups ([Ouyang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Press et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Shetty et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)). However, existing benchmarks provide limited
evaluation of auto-research systems across complementary objectives.
Arena instead unifies diverse objectives within a shared experimental
setting, revealing how the research objective shapes both harness
performance and the resulting idea frontier.

Autonomous research systems. Complementing these benchmarks, research
harnesses determine how agents organize experiments and use accumulated
evidence. End-to-end systems connect ideation, experimentation, and
reporting ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Tang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)), while AIDE ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)) and
AIRA ([Toledo et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)) organize iterative code
improvement through search. AutoScientists ([Gao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)) coordinates decentralized teams, Arbor ([Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)) maintains a persistent hypothesis tree, and
GEAR ([Jeddi et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)) evolves populations of research
states. Heuresis ([Antoniades et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)) further
compares search strategies across quality, diversity, and novelty. Arena
evaluates how the relative strengths of research harnesses vary across
objectives.

## 3 Auto-Research Arena

Auto-Research Arena defines a family of research problems that optimize
different resource targets within a shared language-modeling
environment.
Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Auto-Research as Constrained Optimization ‣ 3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
illustrates the research loop, from selecting a target and evaluating
candidate programs to identifying each harness’s best admissible program
and comparing performance across targets. In the following, we present
the general formulation in
Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Auto-Research as Constrained Optimization ‣ 3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
and its language-modeling instantiation in
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Language-Modeling Instantiation ‣ 3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling").

### 3.1 Auto-Research as Constrained Optimization

Consider a testbed with a fixed data pool, evaluator, and execution
environment. An auto-research harness searches over executable programs
$`p\in\mathcal{P}`$, where candidate programs may modify the model
architecture, optimizer, training schedule, and inference
implementation.

For each objective $`j`$, Arena minimizes a resource metric
$`\operatorname{Target}_{j}(p)`$ subject to a quality threshold $`\tau`$
(shared by all targets $`j`$), target-specific resource limits
$`\mathbf{b}_{j}`$, and correctness checks:

|     |                                                                      |                                                                     |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\underset{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\operatorname{minimize}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Target}_{j}(p)`$                       |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{subject~to}`$                           | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(p)<\tau,`$                               |     |     |
|     |                                                                      | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Resources}_{j}(p)\leq\mathbf{b}_{j}.`$ |     |     |

Here, $`\mathcal{L}(p)`$ denotes validation loss, for which lower values
are better. The vector $`\operatorname{Resources}_{j}(p)`$ contains the
constrained resource measurements, and $`\mathbf{b}_{j}`$ specifies
their limits. Resource inequalities apply componentwise. This
formulation separates _admissibility_ from _improvement_: candidates
qualify by satisfying the quality, resource, and correctness
requirements, while lower target values indicate better outcomes among
qualifying candidates.

Efficiency targets. Arena instantiates this formulation with nine
objectives spanning model, training, and inference efficiency. These
objectives address complementary bottlenecks in model development and
deployment.
Tab. [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Language-Modeling Instantiation ‣ 3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
summarizes their measurements and practical motivations.

Each metric captures a distinct dimension of efficiency. Parameter
efficiency measures the model size needed to achieve the required
quality. Training efficiency measures the resources needed to reach that
quality, while inference efficiency measures the resources needed to use
the trained model. Optimizing these metrics separately defines distinct
research problems within the same experimental environment.

### 3.2 Language-Modeling Instantiation

We instantiate Arena using Karpathy’s autoresearch language-modeling
testbed ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib6)), in which candidate solutions are
compact LLM pretraining programs. We retain its fixed training-data pool
and lightweight experimental setup, replacing loss minimization with the
constrained formulation in
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Auto-Research as Constrained Optimization ‣ 3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling").
Each track optimizes one of nine efficiency metrics subject to
requirements on model quality and relevant non-target resources.

Model quality. Following the original testbed ([Karpathy,
2026](#bib.bib6)), we measure language-modeling quality using validation
bits per byte (BPB). A candidate is admissible only if its validation
BPB is below $`1.05`$. This threshold is slightly above the reference
program’s BPB, providing flexibility for structural changes while
limiting quality degradation.

Resource constraints. Each track limits relevant non-target resources to
control the trade-offs permitted during optimization. These constraints
cover quantities such as model size, training FLOPs, peak training
memory, and available training data. Inference tracks also constrain
other serving costs. For example, the live-state-memory track imposes a
latency bound, while the decode-latency track imposes a
live-state-memory bound. Each track therefore measures improvement in
its selected metric while requiring other resource costs to remain
within specified limits.

Measurement. The Arena evaluator measures all target metrics and
constrained resources directly. Depending on the target, measurements
are taken after a fixed 600-second training period or when the candidate
first meets the quality requirement. Training metrics include consumed
tokens, FLOPs per token, peak allocated GPU memory, elapsed training
time, and optimizer updates. Inference metrics are measured after
training using standardized prompt and generation workloads.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Efficiency Target Definitions ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
provides complete metric definitions and resource constraints for all
nine tracks.

|     |                                                        |                                                                               |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\underset{p}{\operatorname{minimize}}`$ | $`\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{param}}(p)`$                                       |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle\text{subject to}`$                     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{BPB}(p)<1.05,`$                                  |     |
|     |                                                        | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{FLOPs}(p)\leq 239.1\,\text{M/token},`$           |     |
|     |                                                        | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Mem}_{\mathrm{train}}(p)\leq 44.0\,\text{GiB}.`$ |     |

The BPB threshold of $`1.05`$ permits a modest quality decrease relative
to the reference program to accommodate structural changes. The compute
and memory limits match the reference program’s measured costs, ensuring
that parameter reductions do not exceed either resource budget.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate Arena along its two central roles: benchmarking autonomous
research systems and supporting broad idea discovery. We then use its
controlled multi-target setting to study how research evolves under
different objectives, constraints, budgets, and research histories.

Benchmark–harness interface. Arena provides the environment, task
specification, reference program, evaluator, and experiment budget,
while each harness determines how to conduct the search. Starting from
the reference program, a harness proposes code modifications, submits
candidates for evaluation, observes the measured outcomes, and decides
what to explore next. Arena records every experiment and independently
reports the best recorded candidate satisfying the quality, resource,
and correctness requirements, regardless of which program the harness
ultimately retains. Each search receives 100 charged experiments; an
experiment is charged once GPU execution begins, including runs that
subsequently fail or are abandoned.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Harness Integration and Reference Baselines ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
provides the full interface and protocol.

Reference baselines. We introduce two simple reference harnesses that
differ primarily in how many research directions they retain.
_Sequential Search_ maintains one program and evaluates one modification
at a time, adopting an admissible improvement before continuing. _Beam
Search_ retains up to four programs and evaluates two modifications of
each before selecting the next frontier. Both condition subsequent
proposals on the accumulated experimental history, including
unsuccessful attempts.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Harness Integration and Reference Baselines ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")
provides their detailed proposal, selection, and stopping policies.

Tasks and resources. We evaluate all nine Arena objectives defined in
Sec. [3](#S3 "3 Auto-Research Arena ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling"),
each with target-specific resource constraints and the shared quality
requirement BPB $`<1.05`$. Each candidate runs on one NVIDIA A100 80GB
GPU with seed 42 and up to 600 seconds of training, excluding
compilation, validation, and the first ten warmup updates. All searches
use the same approximately 631M-token ClimbMix ([Diao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34)) training pool and held-out BPB evaluator;
training-token consumption includes repeated exposure to the data.

Models and harnesses. We evaluate six LLM-based research harnesses with
Claude Opus 5 ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib25)) and GPT-6 Astra ([OpenAI,
2026](#bib.bib26)), both using medium reasoning effort. In addition to
Sequential Search and Beam Search, we evaluate AutoScientists ([Gao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib17)), Arbor ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)),
GEAR ([Jeddi et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)), and Heuresis ([Antoniades et
al., 2026](#bib.bib20)), which respectively use multi-agent
coordination, hypothesis trees, evolutionary search, and
diversity-preserving archives. We additionally include TPE ([Ferreira et
al., 2026](#bib.bib16)), a non-agentic baseline restricted to a
predefined hyperparameter space, in the Opus 5 experiments.

Scoring. Each objective is scored by the best recorded admissible target
value, with lower values better. For training-token consumption,
optimizer updates, and training time, we use the first 30-second
validation checkpoint satisfying BPB $`<1.05`$. We summarize performance
across objectives with different units and scales using Elo ratings from
pairwise harness comparisons within each objective. Appendix  gives the
scoring procedure.

Arena provides a discriminative evaluation. Research harnesses exhibit
substantial differences across Arena’s objectives, with no single method
dominating the benchmark. With Opus 5, AutoScientists achieves the
highest aggregate Elo, followed by Sequential Search and Arbor; with
GPT-6, GEAR ranks first, followed by Sequential Search. The variation
across objectives shows that Arena distinguishes complementary strengths
of research systems rather than reducing their performance to a single
research setting.

Simple search is surprisingly competitive. Despite its minimal design,
Sequential Search ranks second overall with both foundation LLMs,
outperforming several substantially more sophisticated harnesses. Its
strong performance suggests substantial headroom in current
auto-research system design: more elaborate orchestration does not yet
consistently translate into stronger performance.

Specialization does not necessarily transfer across research problems.
TPE provides a complementary sanity check. Designed for Karpathy’s
autoresearch setting ([Ferreira et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)), TPE performs
substantially worse than all LLM-based harnesses across Arena’s broader
set of objectives. Strong performance in a specialized research setting
therefore need not translate into broad research capability,
illustrating the value of evaluating research systems across
complementary objectives.

the same level of improvement.

### 4.2 A Broader Idea Frontier

Multi-target research substantially broadens the idea frontier. Beyond
routine hyperparameter tuning, Karpathy’s reported loss-driven search
identifies two architectural changes, _smear_ and _backout_  ([Karpathy,
2026](#bib.bib6)). Across Arena’s efficiency objectives, our search
catalogue contains 32 distinct findings, including 20 that were accepted
as improvements during search, which we consolidate into nine idea
families spanning model architecture, training, and inference. Different
objectives expose qualitatively different research directions, from
architectural changes under model-efficiency targets to memory and
systems optimizations under training and inference targets.

The discovered idea frontier mirrors active LLM research. The breadth of
Arena’s discoveries is not merely a larger collection of
benchmark-specific optimizations: many of them reach questions central
to current LLM research. A representative example emerges from parameter
efficiency
(Tab. [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 A Broader Idea Frontier ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")).
Agents first discover recurrent weight sharing, effectively turning the
model into a looped Transformer, and further identify the need to adapt
residual updates across repeated depth, learning separate attention and
MLP residual gains at each depth. This closely mirrors recent work
studying residual adaptation as a key component of looped
Transformers ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)). Similar correspondences
appear across attention-state reduction through cross-layer K/V
sharing ([Brandon et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)), MQA/MLA ([Shazeer,
2019](#bib.bib24); [DeepSeek-AI, 2024](#bib.bib35)), conditional
memory ([Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)), optimizer-state
reduction ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib39)), activation-memory
efficiency ([Korthikanti et al., 2022](#bib.bib40)), and inference
optimization ([Aminabadi et al., 2022](#bib.bib41)). Importantly, these
discoveries are not simply reproductions of existing methods: they often
share the same underlying principles while arriving at different
technical mechanisms, offering alternative perspectives on active
research questions. The resulting idea frontier therefore demonstrates a
broader value of Arena: its diverse objectives expand not only what
research systems are evaluated on, but also the space of meaningful
research directions they explore.

The broader idea frontier accompanies substantial measured progress. The
searches producing these discoveries also achieve large admissible gains
across distinct resource dimensions. In the Opus 5 group, the best
programs reduce model size from 50.33M to 13.20M parameters, training
computation from 239.08M to 52.15M FLOPs per token, and peak training
memory from 43.96 to 0.373 GiB, while satisfying BPB $`<1.05`$ and the
corresponding resource constraints. However, these endpoint programs
combine multiple changes accumulated during search, so their gains do
not establish whether the discovered ideas themselves provide reusable
knowledge beyond the searches that found them. We test this directly in
Sec. [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Validating Discoveries Beyond the Search Setting ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling").

### 4.3 Validating Discoveries Beyond the Search Setting

Arena discovers mechanisms under specific objectives and short
experimental budgets. To assess their usefulness beyond these
conditions, we first test whether a library of discovered ideas can
guide a new search for better model quality. We then ask a stricter
question: can winning programs from the original Arena searches retain
their advantages when retrained for longer without further search?

Combining discovered ideas improves model quality. We collect
discoveries across Arena’s objectives and harnesses into an idea library
to guide a quality-driven search. The agent can select, modify, and
combine mechanisms from different research trajectories rather than
continue from a single winning program. We extend the training time
budget to 1 hour in this search.

The resulting design improves model quality under both matched-budget
evaluations
(Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")).
At matched training data, it reduces training loss from 2.675 to 2.544
(4.9%); at matched training FLOPs, it reduces training loss from 2.617
to 2.544 (2.8%). Final held-out BPB also decreases from 0.9295 to 0.9112
at the same model size (50.33M parameters). The resulting design
selectively adapts and simplifies ideas rather than merely combining
their original implementations. These results demonstrate that
efficiency-targeted discoveries can serve as reusable model-design
knowledge for improving quality. Appendix  details how these ideas are
selected and transformed.

Discovered programs can transfer far beyond the search horizon. We next
ask a stricter question: can a program found under a short training
budget be scaled directly, without further search? Arena normally
evaluates candidate programs after 10 minutes of training. We select two
programs from each of the training-time, token, and update objectives
and retrain them from random initialization for 60 minutes alongside the
reference program.

Several discoveries retain their advantages far beyond the horizon at
which they were found. A token-optimized GEAR program reaches 0.9593 BPB
after 30 minutes using 1.004B tokens, while the reference requires
approximately 47.6 minutes and 1.771B tokens to reach the same quality,
corresponding to 43.3% fewer tokens. Its quality advantage persists
until approximately 51 minutes, extending to five times the original
search horizon. Similarly, a training-time program discovered by
AutoScientists outperforms the reference over the 15–46-minute interval
and reaches 0.9647 BPB after 30 minutes, a quality level that the
reference reaches only after approximately 45.8 minutes, corresponding
to 34.5% less training time
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 A Broader Idea Frontier ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling");
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.4 The Science of Auto-Research ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")).
Reference costs at matched quality are estimated by linear
interpolation.

The transfer is substantial but not indefinite. The GEAR program’s
learning rate reaches its 2% floor around 27 minutes, while the
AutoScientists schedule reaches zero around 24 minutes. Their early
advantages consequently diminish as training continues; by 60 minutes,
for example, the reference reaches 0.9295 BPB compared with 0.9478 for
the GEAR program. Thus, short-horizon search can discover programs whose
advantages persist for four to five times the original search horizon,
while horizon-dependent training policies can ultimately limit direct
extrapolation to longer training.

### 4.4 The Science of Auto-Research

Arena also enables controlled study of what shapes the auto-research
process. We examine quality constraints, budget allocation between
evaluation depth and search breadth, and the role of research history in
cross-target search.

Constraints shape the research space. We vary the BPB threshold across
$`1.02`$, $`1.05`$, and $`2.0`$ for parameter- and memory-efficiency
searches with Sequential Search and Beam Search
(Tab. [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.4 The Science of Auto-Research ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling")).
Relaxing the threshold from $`1.05`$ to $`2.0`$ allows Sequential Search
to reduce parameters from 18.94M to 0.69M, while its separate
peak-memory search reduces memory from 0.83 GiB to 0.035 GiB. The
additional quality slack permits substantially more aggressive
efficiency improvements. Conversely, tighter thresholds restrict which
structural changes remain admissible. Quality constraints thus shape not
only the final results, but also which regions of the design space can
be developed.

Budget allocation creates a depth–breadth trade-off. For parameter
minimization under BPB $`<2.0`$, we vary candidate training time from
1,200 to 6,000 seconds under a shared 20 GPU-hour training budget,
trading fewer, longer evaluations for more, shorter ones (Tab. ). The
shortest setting evaluates 60 candidates and finds the smallest model;
longer settings yield better BPB but more parameters. These outcomes
illustrate the trade-off between evaluation depth and search breadth,
with different allocations favoring different quality–efficiency
balances.

Cross-target research builds on prior discoveries. We initialize
searches under new objectives from programs discovered under earlier
objectives, transferring code while reinitializing model weights

Subsequent searches can accumulate compatible gains: parameter savings
are fully preserved while training FLOPs decrease by a further 33.7%,
and a FLOP-optimized program preserves its FLOP count while uncached
decode latency decreases by 87.5%. Cross-target continuation can also
introduce trade-offs: optimizing training time from the
parameter-optimized program reduces time to quality by 21.1% but
increases parameters by 24.6%. Prior discoveries therefore provide
foundations for both cumulative gains and new trade-offs across
objectives.

Together, these studies show how constraints define admissible designs,
budgets balance evaluation depth against search breadth, and research
history supports cumulative gains or trade-offs across targets.
Therefore, Arena enables systematic study of how autonomous research
develops over time.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced Auto-Research Arena, a multi-target benchmark for
autonomous research across nine quality-constrained language-model
efficiency objectives. Arena reveals complementary harness strengths and
a broad frontier of architectural and systems ideas. Discoveries can
seed searches under new objectives, while a program combining selected
ideas can improve model quality. Controlled studies further show how
constraints, budgets, and research history shape progress. Together,
these results support evaluating autonomous research across
complementary objectives and studying how useful discoveries accumulate.

Limitations. Our scope is explicitly limited to auto-research in
language modeling, rather than the full end-to-end research process or
scientific novelty. Idea-family groupings are interpretive, and noisy
system measurements require care when interpreting small differences.

### AI use statement

LLM-based research agents are the subjects of evaluation in this work;
their roles in proposing ideas, implementing programs, and conducting
experiments are described in the paper.

Separately, we used AI tools to assist with literature search,
compilation of experimental records, text polishing for readability and
figure preparation.

We have not used generative AI tools for helping develop theoretical
models or conceptual frameworks, proposing or refining hypotheses,
designing or providing feedback on research methodology or experiments,
implementing methods, assisting with translation, supporting qualitative
and thematic data analysis, and interpreting results.

Generating synthetic data sets, formulating mathematical claims,
providing critical ingredients for proving mathematical claims,
assisting in the writing of proofs, cleaning and reformating dataset,
are not applicable to this work.

We have reviewed AI-assisted content against the relevant literature,
source code, and experimental records. We take responsibility for the
final content of this work, including text, claims or artifacts produced
with the aid of generative AI.

### Ethics statement

This work studies autonomous research systems in a controlled
language-modeling environment. The experiments use existing
language-model training data and do not involve human subjects, personal
data collection, or deployment to users.

The evaluated agents can autonomously propose code changes and execute
experiments, which may raise broader concerns about increasingly capable
autonomous research systems. In our setting, their actions are
restricted to a sandboxed experimental environment, a fixed program
interface, predefined compute budgets, and standardized evaluation. We
do not evaluate or enable autonomous interaction with external systems
beyond the research environment.

We report both successful and unsuccessful research outcomes and
distinguish measured improvements from interpretations of scientific
novelty. We hope that the benchmark can support more systematic study of
the capabilities and limitations of autonomous research systems.

The following specifications use the objective names in

Displayed resource limits are rounded; each data-pool constraint fixes
the available pool rather than the number of tokens consumed.

Total parameters

Why it matters. Fewer parameters shrink checkpoints and reduce model
storage and transfer costs.

How we measure it. The model’s total parameter count after the fixed
600-second training schedule.

supply ground-truth tokens at each decoding step and compare the

KV-cache objective, with capacities of 513 and 2,048 for the short- and
long-prompt settings, respectively.

## Appendix B Harness Integration and Reference Baselines

This section specifies the integration interface and the two reference
search policies used in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling").
Arena provides execution, measurement, and scoring; the baselines
determine proposals and retention. Their selection rules are harness
choices, not requirements imposed on other integrations.

### B.1 Interface and Recorded Artifacts

Minimal interface. A harness must inspect the provided workspace and
task definition, modify only the permitted program, submit experiments
through Arena, read standardized results, and choose subsequent
candidates. Arena defines the research problem and evaluation
environment while leaving the research strategy open: an integration may
retain an incumbent, beam, population, hypothesis tree, or another
search state.

Recorded artifacts. Each run records the reference program, proposed
ideas, candidate implementations, execution outcomes, measured metrics,
admissibility decisions, and search lineage. The terminal score is
computed from measured admissible outcomes; the accompanying record
supports audits and post-hoc trace analysis. Charged-experiment
accounting follows
Section [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Execution and Budget Accounting ‣ Appendix B Harness Integration and Reference Baselines ‣ Auto-Research Arena: Benchmarking and Advancing Auto-Research in Language Modeling"),
independently of harness-side bookkeeping.

### B.2 Initialization

Arena prepares the environment and evaluates the reference program. The
harness receives its source and measurements, the task’s objective, BPB
threshold, resource constraints, measurement procedures, experiment
budget, and a workspace for candidate programs and research records.

Sequential Search starts from one incumbent and proposes one candidate
per round. Beam Search retains up to four programs and proposes two
modifications per retained program in each generation. A full generation
therefore contains eight candidate experiments; selection occurs after
all outcomes are available.

A persistent controller coordinates an ideator, executor, and reviewer.
The implementation calls the reviewer an evaluator, distinct from
Arena’s evaluator. Agent sessions are fresh for each round or
generation. Instructions, source code, task rules, and saved research
history are supplied to each session, so continuation does not rely on
conversational memory.

### B.3 Proposal Preparation

The ideator inspects the current code and prior experimental history,
then records a proposal, rationale, timestamp, and position in that
history. Edits may change architecture, attention, optimization, data
processing, or training within the permitted boundary. A proposal can
coordinate several edits implementing one mechanism; Sequential Search
also requests follow-up experiments that could separate their
contributions.

The executor implements each proposal in an isolated copy of its parent
program and checks the edits before Arena’s benchmark evaluation.

### B.4 Execution and Budget Accounting

Arena checks the submitted source, protected files, available launch
budget, and hardware, then runs training and task-specific evaluation.
It records source, measurements, execution status, and logs. Successful
execution alone does not establish admissibility.

Sequential Search uses one worker; Beam Search uses four. Parallel
submission does not alter accounting: each candidate is one experiment.
A full eight-candidate generation needs eight available slots and
consumes eight launches if all reach chargeable GPU work, although at
most four run simultaneously. Local CPU checks do not replace candidate
training through Arena.

The budget is 100 charged launches, including the reference evaluation.
A recorded GPU-work-start marker determines charging. Once GPU work
begins, crashes, out-of-memory errors, and task failures still consume
budget. A resolved launch without the marker is not charged; an
unresolved launch reserves a slot. A normally charged reference
therefore leaves at most 99 candidate experiments. The harness reads
Arena’s remaining budget rather than counting its own rounds or
generations. Failed experiments remain recorded.

### B.5 Review and Selection

The reviewer compares the proposal, parent and candidate code,
measurements, and logs, then records feedback. Both baselines exclude
failed or constraint-violating candidates from further development.
Other harnesses may continue refining them, but only admissible results
contribute to Arena’s score.

Unchanged-target attempts. An efficiency improvement may increase BPB
while remaining below the quality threshold. Both baselines also permit
limited attempts to improve quality without changing the target. Target
equality means exact equality of recorded values, regardless of the
proposal’s description. Such attempts consume the allowance even when
rejected.

Sequential Search. An admissible candidate replaces the incumbent when
it strictly improves the target. At exactly equal target value, it may
replace the incumbent while the allowance remains if it improves either
the task-defined tie-break metric or BPB. Otherwise the incumbent is
retained. The allowance starts at four unchanged-target attempts and
resets only after an accepted strict target improvement; accepting a
quality improvement alone does not reset it.

Beam Search. Parents remain fixed throughout a generation. After every
outcome is resolved, the reviewer pools parents and new candidates,
removes exact program duplicates, and excludes failures, constraint
violations, and missing required ranking measurements. Parents need not
be replaced when their modifications fail.

Up to two unchanged-target attempts are permitted per generation only if
the best retained target strictly improved during the preceding two
completed generations. This allowance is determined before proposal
generation. When permitted, a parent and modification with equal target
are reduced to the admissible member with lower BPB. The remaining
programs are ranked by target, ordered task tie-break metrics, and
finally BPB, retaining up to four. Remaining ties favor lower
complexity, lower resource use, and then an older program where evidence
is available. A read-only helper computes identities, admissibility
checks, and ranking values; the reviewer applies the selection policy.

### B.6 Persistence, Interruption, and Stopping

Saved state retains programs, measurements, decisions, feedback, and the
remaining unchanged-target allowance, together with Beam generation
membership and outcomes. Rejected and failed experiments remain in the
history. Continuation reconciles outstanding requests with Arena and
completes pending review before new proposals; recovery itself is not a
new experiment.

Sequential Search normally stops when the budget is exhausted. Beam
Search also stops when the remaining budget cannot cover two candidates
per retained program; it does not shrink the generation or select from a
partially evaluated one. Both record the stopping reason and unused
budget.

Arena independently ranks all admissible recorded results and reports
the best program, which need not be the final harness-selected endpoint.
Computing this result requires no additional training.
