###### Abstract

AI agents can now carry out data-driven scientific analyses end to end,
and benchmarks assess them by giving an agent a question and a dataset
and scoring its final answer against a fixed key. These benchmarks
assume that a correct answer was derived from the supplied data, a
property we call _evidence grounding_. However, an agent can also reach
the key from prior knowledge or by ruling out the other options, and a
score based on a single run cannot tell these cases apart. We show how
to test this assumption and find that it often fails. For each question,
we build versions of its data files in which the evidence for the answer
is left intact, withdrawn or reversed, check each edit with a
pre-registered reference statistic, and run the same agent on every
version. We then measure _evidence-grounded accuracy_, which credits a
correct answer only if the agent also responds when the evidence is
withdrawn and follows it when it is reversed. We evaluate three agent
scaffolds and five models on 18 single-cell questions from BAISBench and
four synthetic problems from GeneBench-Pro. On the single-cell
questions, the Claude agents are 95% accurate and answer 83% correctly
without any data, but their evidence-grounded accuracy is only 41%.
Hiding gene names raises the share of runs that follow reversed evidence
from 58% to 93% on ten gene tasks, suggesting that prior knowledge
competes with the supplied data. The benchmark score and LLM judges can
also reward answers that ignore the changed evidence. Measuring
scientific intelligence rather than recall therefore requires checking
whether answers follow the evidence and whether scores reward them for
it.

## 1 Introduction

Benchmarks orient AI. They encode what the community treats as progress
and set the direction of future work \[[1](#bib.bib20),
[2](#bib.bib21)\]. This is now true for AI scientists: agents that plan
an analysis, write and run code and interpret the
output \[[3](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib2)\]. Biology is one of the domains
where this shift is most visible. Agents load single-cell atlases, fit
genetic models and report discoveries end to end, and teams of agents
have designed nanobodies that were then validated
experimentally \[[5](#bib.bib25)\]. A growing family of benchmarks
scores these agents on such tasks, pairing real or synthetic data with a
verifiable answer \[[6](#bib.bib22), [7](#bib.bib15), [8](#bib.bib23),
[9](#bib.bib17)\]. Their scores are used to compare systems, to track
progress and to argue that agents can do science. When a score carries
this much weight, what it certifies deserves the same scrutiny as the
systems it ranks.

The value of a benchmark depends on whether its score supports the claim
it is used to make, a property known as construct
validity \[[2](#bib.bib21)\]. For a data-driven benchmark the claim is
specific: a correct answer shows that the agent derived its conclusion
from the data supplied in that run. We call this property _evidence
grounding_. To our knowledge it has not been tested for the score
itself, and
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
illustrates why it should be. In one of our runs, we asked an agent
which gene identifies endothelial cells in a single-cell dataset from
which we had deleted that gene. The agent noticed (“PECAM1 is not in the
dataset’s gene list”) and answered PECAM1 anyway, “the only option
left”. The benchmark scored it fully correct. The answer was right, but
it did not come from the supplied data
(App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B A worked example of the definitions ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

What should a score for an AI scientist satisfy to serve as evidence of
data use? Drawing on construct validity \[[2](#bib.bib21)\] and
counterfactual tests \[[10](#bib.bib26), [11](#bib.bib27)\], we propose
three necessary properties that existing practice addresses only in
part. ❶ Dependence on the supplied data. Partial-input baselines show
that many benchmarks can be solved without their intended
evidence \[[12](#bib.bib28), [13](#bib.bib29), [14](#bib.bib30)\]. In
our tasks, agents answer most questions correctly before opening a
single file. ❷ Separation of analysis from recall. Outcome scores credit
a derived and a recalled answer alike. Reading the trajectory helps only
in part, since stated reasoning need not reflect what drove the
answer \[[15](#bib.bib31), [16](#bib.bib32)\] and process judges still
grade against the same key \[[17](#bib.bib36), [18](#bib.bib16)\]. ❸
Credit for the appropriate response to changed evidence. Withdrawn
evidence calls for a changed or qualified answer, and reversed evidence
for a reversed one \[[11](#bib.bib27), [19](#bib.bib33)\], yet end-point
scores for agents rarely test whether an answer responds in this way.

The community has recognised parts of this problem. Benchmarks filter
out questions answerable from pretraining \[[20](#bib.bib24)\], report a
no-data baseline \[[21](#bib.bib8)\], compare against a shuffled
dataset \[[8](#bib.bib23)\] or test agents on a hand-made label
swap \[[22](#bib.bib19)\], and process judges grade the analysis, not
just the answer \[[17](#bib.bib36), [18](#bib.bib16)\]. These controls
are valuable, but they share two limits. (i) They are aggregate or
one-off, so they cannot say whether a given correct answer came from the
data. (ii) They audit the agent rather than the score, so none asks
whether the score rewards responding to new evidence.

To address these gaps, we rethink what a correct answer should certify
and ask a direct question: when the evidence changes, does the agent
follow it, and does the evaluator reward that response? We make this
question testable with verified data counterfactuals, versions of each
item’s data files in which the evidence is preserved, withdrawn or
reversed and every edit is confirmed by a pre-registered reference
statistic. Running the same agent on every version shows whether it
follows the evidence and whether the benchmark score and LLM judges
reward it. We apply this protocol to BAISBench \[[8](#bib.bib23)\] and
GeneBench-Pro \[[9](#bib.bib17)\] with three agent scaffolds and five
models from two vendors.

Most of the accuracy is available without the data. The Claude agents
answer 95% of questions correctly with the data and 83% without. When
the evidence is withdrawn or reversed, agents often notice the change
but keep the original answer, and the benchmark still awards 77% of its
credit after withdrawal. Their evidence-grounded accuracy is only 41%,
and it orders the agents very differently from accuracy. Hiding gene
names raises flip-following from 58% to 93%, supporting competition from
prior knowledge as an explanation. The benchmark score and LLM judges
share this blind spot.

To summarize, our contributions are as follows.

- •
  Evaluation target. We formalise evidence grounding through invariance
  and responsiveness, introduce _evidence-grounded accuracy_ to measure
  correctness together with the response to withdrawal and flip, and
  separate agent grounding from evaluator validity.
- •
  Verified counterfactual protocol. We build paired versions of raw data
  files that preserve, withdraw or reverse the evidence, verify every
  edit with a pre-registered statistic and control leaks through file
  metadata that could reveal an edit.
- •
  Empirical evidence. On single-cell tasks, we show that high accuracy
  can coexist with weak responsiveness to evidence and a different
  ranking of agents, and use gene-name anonymisation to show that prior
  knowledge can compete with the data.
- •
  Evaluator blind spots and targeted mitigations. The official score and
  LLM judges can reward answers that ignore changed evidence. An
  abstention option, a grounding-focused judge and transform consistency
  each mitigate a distinct failure, but none suffices alone.

## 2 Related work

Benchmarks for AI scientists and scientific discovery. Science is judged
not only by its conclusions but also by how they were reached. AI
scientists now reach conclusions end to end \[[3](#bib.bib1),
[4](#bib.bib2), [23](#bib.bib3)\], and as these systems proliferate,
their evaluation shapes what capabilities are developed. Benchmarks have
expanded from scientific knowledge \[[24](#bib.bib4), [25](#bib.bib5)\]
and question answering \[[26](#bib.bib6), [27](#bib.bib7)\] to
data-driven analysis \[[21](#bib.bib8), [28](#bib.bib9)\], code
execution \[[29](#bib.bib11), [30](#bib.bib10)\], and research
replication \[[31](#bib.bib12), [32](#bib.bib13)\]. In computational
biology, recent benchmarks pair real or synthetic data with verifiable
answers, covering analysis decisions \[[33](#bib.bib14)\],
bioinformatics and study-scale analyses \[[7](#bib.bib15),
[18](#bib.bib16)\], single-cell annotation and
discovery \[[8](#bib.bib23), [34](#bib.bib18), [22](#bib.bib19)\], and
synthetic multi-stage genetics \[[9](#bib.bib17)\]. These designs bring
evaluation closer to how research is done. At the same time, stronger
models have more ways to reach the reference answer without the data, by
recall, by eliminating options \[[35](#bib.bib47)\] or by exploiting the
grader \[[36](#bib.bib46), [37](#bib.bib45)\]. This raises a natural
question: does a high score show that the agent’s answer was determined
by the data supplied in that run?

Validity of benchmark scores. Whether a benchmark measures what it was
built to measure is a question of benchmark validity. Prior work finds
recurring problems in how LLM benchmarks define target abilities, choose
tasks and score answers \[[2](#bib.bib21)\], and leaderboard rankings
can shift under small choices such as answer-option order or answer
extraction \[[38](#bib.bib34)\]. Process evaluation grades intermediate
steps rather than final answers alone, through step-level
supervision \[[39](#bib.bib35)\], rubrics \[[32](#bib.bib13)\], agent
judges \[[17](#bib.bib36)\] and LLM judges of analysis
decisions \[[33](#bib.bib14), [18](#bib.bib16)\]. Yet a judge sees only
one run and relies on the agent’s account, which may not be reliable.
LLM judges can be misled by agents’ claims of task
completion \[[40](#bib.bib37)\], and written reasoning need not reflect
what produced an answer \[[15](#bib.bib31), [16](#bib.bib32)\]. An agent
can thus describe a valid analysis of the data while its final answer is
not grounded in them.

Counterfactual tests. A natural way to test whether an answer depends on
the data is to change the evidence and see whether the answer changes.
Early work in NLP and vision showed that models can often answer without
key inputs, such as the premise \[[41](#bib.bib38), [12](#bib.bib28)\],
the passage \[[42](#bib.bib39)\], the question \[[13](#bib.bib29)\] or
the image \[[14](#bib.bib30)\]. Later work paired inputs with edited
versions, testing whether predictions remain stable under irrelevant
edits and change when relevant evidence is altered, in
NLP \[[10](#bib.bib26), [11](#bib.bib27)\], software
testing \[[43](#bib.bib40)\] and medical AI \[[44](#bib.bib41),
[45](#bib.bib42)\]. Studies of knowledge conflicts find that models can
hold on to what they learned in pretraining when the context contradicts
it \[[46](#bib.bib43), [47](#bib.bib44)\], and ImpossibleBench applies a
similar idea to coding agents \[[37](#bib.bib45)\]. Scientific
benchmarks have begun to adopt such tests. DiscoveryBench compares
performance with and without data \[[21](#bib.bib8)\], BAISBench
compares against shuffled data \[[8](#bib.bib23)\], and scBench-Long
swaps labels in one test \[[22](#bib.bib19)\]. These tests show that
performance depends on the data on average, but not whether a particular
credited answer follows the evidence, nor whether the benchmark score
would still reward an agent that ignores a change. We differ in editing
the raw data files of each item, verifying every edit with a reference
statistic so that the correct response is known, and testing the
benchmark’s score as well as the agent.

## 3 Preliminaries: a taxonomy of current evaluation

Setting. A data-driven benchmark item consists of a task $`x`$, which
may be a question or an analysis instruction, data $`E`$ and a reference
answer, or key, $`c^{\star}`$. An agent works on the task in a loop. At
each step it writes code or calls a tool, $`u_{i}`$, which is executed
against the data and returns an observation $`o_{i}`$. After $`n`$ steps
it returns a final answer,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\tau=(u_{1},o_{1},\dots,u_{n},o_{n}),\qquad o_{i}=\mathrm{Exec}(u_{i},E),\qquad a=\pi_{\theta}(x,\tau),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is the agent with model parameters $`\theta`$,
and an evaluator $`M`$ assigns a score. The data reach the answer only
through the observations, while $`\theta`$ carries what the model
learned in pretraining, so a correct answer alone does not reveal how
much the supplied data contributed.

Current practice. Existing evaluations fall into four families that
differ in what the evaluator compares and at what level
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Preliminaries: a taxonomy of current evaluation ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
Outcome scoring compares the answer with the key, $`M(a,c^{\star})`$,
and says whether one run was right. Process scoring also reads the
trajectory, $`M(\tau,a,c^{\star})`$, but still judges one run against
the same key. Input ablation compares accuracy with and without the
data, but only on average over items. Ad hoc perturbation edits one
item’s data and checks whether the answer changes, but assumes the
correct response to the edit and leaves the score unexamined. Single-run
scores and aggregate ablations cannot establish whether a particular
correct answer tracks the supplied data, and an unverified perturbation
cannot establish whether a changed answer is appropriate. None of these
procedures tests whether the evaluator rewards responses to verified
changes in evidence.

| family | compares | level | correct response | evaluator |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  | to the change known | tested |
| outcome scoring | answer vs. key | run | – | no |
| process scoring | trajectory vs. key | run | – | no |
| input ablation | with vs. without data | benchmark | – | no |
| ad hoc perturbation | before vs. after an edit | item | assumed | no |
| verified counterfactual (ours) | paired data versions | run | verified | yes |

Table 1: Families of evaluation for data-driven AI scientists. Only a
verified counterfactual tests single answers against a known correct
response and also tests the evaluator.

## 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals

We propose to evaluate AI scientists by whether their answers follow the
evidence, and to test evaluators in the same way. Our approach rests on
one observation and one design principle. The observation is that
whether an answer came from the data is a counterfactual property, which
no evaluation of a single run can establish
(Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Problem formulation ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
The principle is one set of pairs, two audits. We pair each item with
versions of its data whose correct response is verified
(Sec. [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Constructing verified counterfactuals ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")),
and use the same paired runs to audit both the agent and the evaluator
(Sec. [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Scoring agents and evaluators ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

### 4.1 Problem formulation

We start from what the data themselves support. We define
$`s^{\star}(E)`$ as the answer supported by the data, computed by a
reference statistic that the task implies, for example the cell type
with the highest mean expression of the queried gene. On an item whose
key is supported by its data, $`s^{\star}(E)=c^{\star}`$. An
intervention $`T`$ edits the data while leaving the task and prompt
unchanged, and we classify interventions by their effect on
$`s^{\star}`$: ❶ a null intervention $`T_{0}`$ preserves the supported
answer, $`s^{\star}(T_{0}E)=s^{\star}(E)`$, ❷ a withdrawal $`T_{B}`$
removes sufficient support for any answer, so $`s^{\star}(T_{B}E)`$ is
undefined, and ❸ a flip $`T_{C}`$ changes the supported answer to
another option, $`s^{\star}(T_{C}E)=c^{\prime}\neq c^{\star}`$.
App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B A worked example of the definitions ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
works through every definition of this section on one item.

We call an answer evidence-grounded when it tracks $`s^{\star}`$ across
these interventions: it remains unchanged under $`T_{0}`$, changes or is
flagged under $`T_{B}`$, and changes to $`c^{\prime}`$ under $`T_{C}`$.
The definition concerns only how the answer responds to the evidence in
each run and places no constraint on the analysis, so our object of
evaluation is the relation between answer and evidence rather than the
analysis path, model or scaffold. In terms of
Eq. ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3 Preliminaries: a taxonomy of current evaluation ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")),
editing $`E`$ changes the observations $`o_{i}`$, and grounding asks
whether $`a`$ changes accordingly. An agent may therefore detect an
intervention without being grounded in it, when some $`o_{i}`$ reflects
the edit but $`a`$ does not follow. We term this noticing without
acting.

Why observational evaluation cannot certify grounding.

*Proposition 1 (observational non-identifiability).* For any observed
trajectory–answer pair $`(\tau,a)`$ on evidence $`E`$, there exist two
agents $`\pi`$ and $`\tilde{\pi}`$ that produce exactly $`(\tau,a)`$ on
$`E`$ but respond differently to some intervention $`T`$ on $`E`$.
Consequently, no evaluator of the form $`M(\tau,a,c^{\star})`$ computed
only on the original evidence can determine whether the answer is
evidence-grounded.

*Proof sketch.* Construct $`\pi`$ and $`\tilde{\pi}`$ to behave
identically on $`E`$, yielding the same actions, observations,
trajectory $`\tau`$, and final answer $`a`$. Let them differ only on
some intervened evidence $`T(E)`$. For example, under a flip $`T_{C}`$,
let $`\pi`$ change its answer to the new data-supported option
$`c^{\prime}=s^{\star}(T_{C}E)`$, while $`\tilde{\pi}`$ retains its
original answer. Since an evaluator computed only from the run on $`E`$
receives identical inputs for the two agents, it must assign them the
same score, although their grounding behavior differs under
intervention. $`\square`$

The ambiguity is not hypothetical. An agent may run a correct analysis,
report that the evidence is missing and still return the key
(App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B A worked example of the definitions ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")),
a run that looks strong under both outcome and process scoring.
Separating grounded from ungrounded answers therefore requires an
intervention on $`E`$ whose effect on $`s^{\star}`$ is known, which is
what a verified counterfactual provides.

### 4.2 Constructing verified counterfactuals

![Refer to caption](2610.04915v1/figs/framework.png)

Figure 2: Protocol. (1) Each item is paired with null, withdrawal and
flip versions of its data, each verified with a pre-registered reference
statistic. (2) The same agent runs on every version in a fresh sandbox.
(3) The paired outputs audit both the agent, which should track the
evidence, and the evaluator, which should score an evidence-following
answer above one that keeps the old key.

For each item we pre-register the reference statistic and build one
version of the data for each intervention class
(Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Constructing verified counterfactuals ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
Null versions permute cells and genes and rename barcodes and donors.
Withdrawal versions delete the queried gene, remove the contrast group
or equalise the candidate groups. Flip versions move a gene’s expression
to another cell type, swap two genes or down-sample one population in
one condition. Each edit touches only the quantity the statistic reads
and is applied to the expression matrix, all layers and the raw counts.
A version counts as verified only when the statistic, recomputed on
every file and on the raw counts, behaves as its class requires. The
correct response of a grounded agent is therefore known for every
version.

Keeping the counterfactuals clean. A counterfactual is informative only
if the versions differ in evidence alone. Agents read metadata. In
development one agent recovered a swap from a file name stored in a gzip
header, and the h5ad files ship precomputed summaries that would still
show the original evidence. Every file of every version, the original
included, therefore passes through one canonical writer
(App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Agent protocol ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

### 4.3 Scoring agents and evaluators

Agents. Writing $`a(E)`$ for the answer of a run on data $`E`$ and
$`\mathrm{flag}(E)`$ for an explicit statement that the data do not
support an answer, we define invariance and responsiveness over paired
runs,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}I=\Pr\big[a(T_{0}E)=a(E)\big],\qquad R_{\text{flip}}=\Pr\big[a(T_{C}E)=c^{\prime}\big],\\
R_{\text{rm}}=\Pr\big[a(T_{B}E)\neq a(E)\ \vee\ \mathrm{flag}(T_{B}E)\big],\qquad R^{\text{flag}}_{\text{rm}}=\Pr\big[\mathrm{flag}(T_{B}E)\big].\end{gathered}
``` |  | (2) |

We compare $`I`$ with the agreement between two runs on the unmodified
data, which separates sensitivity to the intervention from sampling
variation. $`R_{\text{rm}}`$ measures whether the answer depends on the
withdrawn evidence at all, the counterfactual dependence of
Proposition 1, while $`R^{\text{flag}}_{\text{rm}}`$ measures whether
the response is appropriate, and we report both.

We introduce *evidence-grounded accuracy* (EGA), which credits a correct
answer only when it also reacts to withdrawal and follows the flip,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{EGA}=\Pr\big[\,a(E)=c^{\star}\ \wedge\ \big(a(T_{B}E)\neq a(E)\vee\mathrm{flag}(T_{B}E)\big)\ \wedge\ a(T_{C}E)=c^{\prime}\,\big].
``` |  | (3) |

The strict variant $`\mathrm{EGA}^{\text{strict}}`$ replaces the
withdrawal condition by $`\mathrm{flag}(T_{B}E)`$. We assess null
invariance separately with $`I`$. EGA combines correctness with
responsiveness to the two interventions that change the evidence. EGA
does not penalise prior knowledge. It asks only whether the answer
remains responsive to the evidence supplied in the current run.

Paired runs also make the route to each correct answer observable.
Classifying each correct run by whether its withdrawal run *reacts*, by
changing the answer or flagging that the data cannot decide, and whether
its flip run follows $`s^{\star}`$ to $`c^{\prime}`$, we decompose
accuracy as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Acc}=\underbrace{P_{\text{derived}}}_{\text{reacts, follows}}+\underbrace{P_{\text{elim}}}_{\text{persists, follows}}+\underbrace{P_{\text{rm-only}}}_{\text{reacts, keeps }c^{\star}}+\underbrace{P_{\text{recalled}}}_{\text{persists, keeps }c^{\star}}+P_{\text{other}},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`P_{\text{other}}`$ collects flip runs that move to a third
option or give no answer. Only the derived route is evidence-grounded,
so $`P_{\text{derived}}=\mathrm{EGA}`$ and the other components are the
part of the score that the data do not certify. An answer *selected by
elimination* persists under withdrawal because a withdrawal removes the
evidence for the key but not the evidence against the other options, so
in a closed option set the key can still be reached by ruling the others
out. A *recalled* answer persists under withdrawal and keeps
$`c^{\star}`$ after a flip, consistent with knowledge outside the
supplied data, and $`P_{\text{rm-only}}`$ reacts when the evidence
disappears but not when it contradicts the key. We also report the
*evidence gain*, accuracy with data minus accuracy without, and, for
synthetic problems whose ground truth transforms exactly under a flip,
*transform consistency*, which compares the flipped answer with the same
transform of the agent’s own original answer and so measures
responsiveness even when the original answer misses the tolerance.

Evaluators. Throughout, the evaluator is given the original key
$`c^{\star}`$, as a benchmark would be, since it is not told that the
data were edited. We call an evaluator *counterfactually valid* if, over
paired runs, it is stable under null interventions up to run-to-run
variation, does not credit $`c^{\star}`$ once the edited data no longer
support it, and scores a run that follows a verified flip above one that
does not. Writing $`M(\cdot)`$ for its score of a run against the
original key, we measure the three conditions by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}S_{\text{null}}=\mathbb{E}\big|M(E)-M(T_{0}E)\big|,\qquad B_{c}=\mathbb{E}\big[M(T_{c}E)\big]\ \ (c\in\{\text{rm},\text{flip}\}),\\
\mathrm{AUC}_{\text{flip}}=\Pr\big[M(r^{+})>M(r^{-})\big]+\tfrac{1}{2}\Pr\big[M(r^{+})=M(r^{-})\big].\end{gathered}
``` |  | (5) |

The null sensitivity $`S_{\text{null}}`$ should not exceed the change
between two runs on the original data, the blind spot $`B_{c}`$ should
be near zero, and the flip AUC, which compares a flip run $`r^{+}`$
answering $`c^{\prime}`$ with one $`r^{-}`$ that does not, should be
near 1. Evidence grounding is thus a property of the agent and
counterfactual validity a property of the evaluator, and both are
measured on the same pairs.

## 5 Experimental setup

Tasks. We use BAISBench’s scientific-discovery split
(BAIS-SD) \[[8](#bib.bib23)\] and GeneBench-Pro \[[9](#bib.bib17)\]. Of
193 BAIS-SD questions, 22 can be decided from the supplied single-cell
data by a reference statistic. Four of these were excluded because the
original data do not support the benchmark key, leaving 18 questions
from 13 studies. The official score is an exact set match with half
credit for a strict subset of the key. GeneBench-Pro contributes four
synthetic statistical-genetics problems whose ground truth transforms
exactly under specified data edits, and its grader requires every
reported quantity to fall within tolerance. More details on the tasks
and their interventions are provided in
App. [C](#A3 "Appendix C Benchmarks and tasks ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists").

Agents. We evaluate three scaffolds and five models. Claude Code runs
Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5, and Codex CLI runs GPT-5.6-Sol
and GPT-5.6-Terra, each with three replicates.
Biomni \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] runs Claude Sonnet 5 with one replicate.
Each run starts in a fresh sandbox with only its task files and no web
or retrieval access, receives the same prompt on every data version, and
writes its answer, a confidence and whether the data support the answer.
A no-data condition provides only the question and background. More
details on the agent protocol are provided in
App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Agent protocol ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists").

Evaluators. We stress-test three kinds of evaluator on the same paired
runs: the benchmark’s official score against the original key, an LLM
process judge that reads the question, the key and a condensed
trajectory (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6-Sol), and the agent’s own report
of its confidence and of whether the data support its answer.
Separately, an audit of all 2,944 included runs (16,091 tool calls) for
access outside the sandbox or to benchmark answer material flagged none.
More details on the judges and the audit are provided in
App. [E](#A5 "Appendix E Evaluators ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
and
App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Agent protocol ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists").

Research questions. We organise the results around four questions. *RQ1*
asks whether agents track the evidence when it is withdrawn or reversed
(Sec. [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 RQ1: Do agents track the evidence? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
*RQ2* asks how much of their accuracy is evidence-grounded
(Sec. [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 RQ2: How much of the accuracy is evidence-grounded? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
*RQ3* asks why agents fail to follow the evidence, and separates prior
knowledge from analytical skill
(Sec. [6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 RQ3: Why do agents not follow the evidence? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
*RQ4* asks whether the benchmark score and LLM judges notice these
failures, and whether they can be repaired
(Sec. [6.4](#S6.SS4 "6.4 RQ4: Do evaluators notice, and can they be repaired? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

Null edits, which shuffle cells and genes and rename identifiers, change

#### ➠Withdrawn evidence is noticed but not acted on.

When the evidence behind the key is withdrawn, the benchmark still
awards 77% of its credit to the Claude agents
(Table [2](#S6.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 6.1 RQ1: Do agents track the evidence? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
The agents do register the loss
(App. [F](#A6 "Appendix F Additional analyses ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")),
yet only 35% of withdrawal runs state that the data cannot decide, and
84% of those give the key regardless. As
Fig. [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ ➠Answers are stable when the evidence is intact. ‣ 6.1 RQ1: Do agents track the evidence? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")a
shows, the forced choice turns this uncertainty into a committed answer,
which the trajectories show is often reached by ruling out the other
options or from memory
(App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B A worked example of the definitions ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists"),
App. [I](#A9 "Appendix I Case studies ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
The benchmark score cannot tell such an answer from analysis.

#### ➠Even reversed evidence often fails to move the answer.

A reversal is a sharper test, because the data now point to a different
option. Only 61% of runs follow it, and in 30% the agent keeps the key
and is still credited
(Fig. [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ ➠Answers are stable when the evidence is intact. ‣ 6.1 RQ1: Do agents track the evidence? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")b).
A scientist might reasonably distrust data that contradict known
biology, but most runs that keep the key never mention a conflict, and
reversals that look biologically ordinary, such as a shift in cell-type
proportions, are followed only slightly more often (67%). The effect
holds in every scaffold and without the two tasks that admit a second
valid analysis
(App. [C](#A3 "Appendix C Benchmarks and tasks ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists"),
App. [F](#A6 "Appendix F Additional analyses ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
The original answer remains sticky even when the supplied evidence
supports another option.

Accuracy credits every correct answer alike. Using the paired runs, we
ask how much of it remains once a correct answer must also respond to

### 6.3 RQ3: Why do agents not follow the evidence?

An agent may fail to follow the evidence because it cannot analyse the
data well, or because what it already knows overrides the data. We
separate them by removing the prior but not the evidence.

#### ➠No-data accuracy and the recall pattern covary.

Given only the question and background, the Claude models answer 83%
correctly and the GPT models about as many, so the data add only 12
percentage points. Sonnet answers the most questions without data and
shows the recall pattern most often, and Haiku does both least
(Fig. [4](#S6.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 6.2 RQ2: How much of the accuracy is evidence-grounded? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")),
suggesting that the prior competes with the data.

#### ➠Hiding gene names makes agents follow reversed evidence.

We replaced every gene symbol with an opaque identifier in the 10 gene
tasks, consistently in the data, question, options and background
(App. [G](#A7 "Appendix G Gene-name anonymisation ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
This preserves the underlying evidence while removing gene identity as a
usable prior. As
Fig. [5](#S6.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ ➠Most correct answers do not meet both response conditions. ‣ 6.2 RQ2: How much of the accuracy is evidence-grounded? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
shows, accuracy without data falls from 87% to 27%, near chance, while
accuracy with data barely changes, so the tasks remain solvable from the
data alone. Under reversal, agents now follow the flipped evidence in
93% of runs instead of 58%, and EGA rises from 39% to 69%. The GPT
agents change the same way, and the effect remains without the one task
whose study the models still recognise. Anonymisation also removes
semantic cues, so this supports prior competition as an important
explanation rather than proving it the only one. Withdrawal improves
less (key credit 79% to 52%), consistent with the forced choice still
allowing answers by elimination.

#### ➠Without a competing prior, analysis quality becomes the limit.

GeneBench-Pro offers little task-specific prior to fall back on. There,
the agents’ answers on flipped data transform with the ground truth
about as often as repeated runs on unchanged data agree, although
accuracy stays low
(App. [F](#A6 "Appendix F Additional analyses ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
Without a competing prior, responsiveness appears limited mainly by
analytical precision.

### 6.4 RQ4: Do evaluators notice, and can they be repaired?

If agents often ignore changed evidence, a valid evaluator should
notice. We applied the stress test of
Sec. [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Scoring agents and evaluators ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
to the benchmark score, LLM judges and the agents’ self-reports, and
tested targeted repairs.

Figure 6: What the score hides, and what repairs it. (a) GeneBench-Pro
(Claude models). The grader passes almost no run on either version, yet
transform-consistent answers are as frequent as run-to-run agreement.
(b) Offering “the provided data are insufficient” cuts key credit on
withdrawn evidence for Claude Code agents and leaves intact-data
accuracy unchanged. (c) Flip AUC, the probability that a run following
the flip outscores one that does not. Under the standard prompt both
judges reward ignoring the flip, like the benchmark score. The grounding
prompt reverses this.

#### ➠Neither the score nor LLM judges reward following the evidence.

The benchmark score is stable under null edits, but falls under flips
whenever the answer leaves the key, so a run that follows the flipped
data scores below one that ignores them nearly nine times in ten (flip
AUC 0.11). An LLM judge sees the condensed analysis, including
statements that evidence is missing, yet grades against the same key and
behaves alike (flip AUC 0.12). A GPT judge does the same (0.10), so the
blind spot is not family-specific
(App. [E](#A5 "Appendix E Evaluators ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
Self-reports separate the two cases better, since runs that keep the key
after a flip rarely claim full support (13%), unlike runs that follow it
(84%). Synthetic data reverse the failure. As
Fig. [6](#S6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 6.4 RQ4: Do evaluators notice, and can they be repaired? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")a
shows, the grader scores most runs zero on every version, so a run
tracking the transformation but missing the tolerance looks like one
that ignores the edit.

#### ➠Targeted repairs fix one failure at a time.

An explicit “data are insufficient” option leaves intact-data accuracy
unchanged and more than halves withdrawal credit, but fails under flips,
where the data support an answer
(Fig. [6](#S6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 6.4 RQ4: Do evaluators notice, and can they be repaired? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")b).
Asking judges to grade grounding rather than agreement lifts their flip
AUC above 0.7 at no cost on null edits, but leaves part of the
withdrawal blind spot
(Fig. [6](#S6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 6.4 RQ4: Do evaluators notice, and can they be repaired? ‣ 6 Results and analysis ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")c).
Transform consistency credits synthetic answers that move with the data
despite missing the tolerance. No repair covers every failure, and none
can be validated without paired counterfactuals
(App. [H](#A8 "Appendix H Repairs ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

## 7 Conclusion

Science advances when evidence can change a conclusion, yet high
accuracy on a data-driven benchmark does not establish that an AI
scientist used the supplied data. We tested this assumption with
verified, item-level counterfactuals that preserve, withdraw or reverse
the evidence. On our tasks, agents often retained the original answer
after the evidence changed, while benchmark scores and LLM judges
continued to reward it: Claude agents averaged 95% accuracy but only 41%
evidence-grounded accuracy. Hiding gene names increased responsiveness
to reversed evidence, suggesting that prior knowledge can override
evidence the agents are able to analyse. As models learn more, accuracy
may increasingly measure what an agent knows rather than what it can
infer. Evaluations of data-driven AI scientists should therefore test
whether both answers and scores respond to verified changes in evidence,
and report evidence-grounded accuracy alongside conventional accuracy.

## Appendix A Discussion

What the score measures. Benchmark accuracy measures whether an answer
meets the scoring criteria, but does not establish whether it depends on
the supplied data. EGA measures behaviour under our interventions rather
than the reasoning inside a run, so the routes in
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.3 Scoring agents and evaluators ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists"))
are behavioural patterns consistent with recall or elimination, not
observations of them. For withdrawal, the plain variant accepts either a
changed answer or an explicit statement that the data cannot decide, and
the strict variant requires the latter.

What the evaluator results show. All runs were scored against the
original key, as in normal use. The exact-match score still uses the
original key after a flip, so it penalises answers that follow the
changed data. It therefore measures agreement with the key, not whether
the answer follows the supplied evidence. LLM judges could in principle
credit an analysis that contradicts the key, yet under the standard
prompt they followed it, and only a grounding prompt changed this
(App. [H](#A8 "Appendix H Repairs ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).

Recommendations. We recommend four practices. Data-driven benchmarks
should ship each item with a verified withdrawal and flip and report
both EGA variants and the no-data prior next to accuracy. They should
offer an explicit “insufficient data” option. They should validate
process judges on counterfactual pairs and credit transform consistency
where the ground truth transforms exactly. Last, they should pass every
file version, the original included, through one canonical writer.

Limitations. Our study was limited mainly by compute and budget. Each
counterfactual is built and verified by hand and every version requires
a full agent run, so we covered 18 BAIS-SD questions and four
GeneBench-Pro problems, ran three replicates per model, a single
replicate for Biomni, and tested gene-name anonymisation on ten tasks
only. Larger task sets, more replicates, open-weight models and a human
validation of the LLM judges would tighten our estimates. Finally,
responsiveness to evidence is necessary for data-driven reasoning but
not sufficient for scientific quality.

## Appendix B A worked example of the definitions

This section follows one BAIS-SD item through every definition of
Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists").
It is the item described in the introduction, and all answers below are
from our runs.

The item. The task $`x`$ asks “Which of the following gene expression
markers was used to identify endothelial cells in the human hamstring
tendon?” with options A) PDGFRB, B) TRDN, C) PECAM1 and D) PAX7. The
data $`E`$ are the single-nucleus RNA-seq file of the
study \[[49](#bib.bib49)\] and the key is $`c^{\star}=\text{C}`$.

The reference statistic and $`s^{\star}`$. The task asks for a marker of
one cell type, so the statistic is, for each option gene, its mean
log-normalised expression in endothelial cells minus its mean in all
other cells. The data-supported answer $`s^{\star}(E)`$ is the option
with the largest value. On the original file this is PECAM1, so
$`s^{\star}(E)=c^{\star}=\text{C}`$ and the key is supported by the
data.

The three interventions. The null version $`T_{0}E`$ permutes the order
of cells and genes and renames cell barcodes and donors. The statistic
is unchanged, so $`s^{\star}(T_{0}E)=\text{C}`$. The withdrawal
$`T_{B}E`$ deletes PECAM1 from the expression matrix, every layer and
the raw counts. The statistic can no longer be computed for the key, so
the data no longer decide the task and $`s^{\star}(T_{B}E)`$ is
undefined. The flip $`T_{C}E`$ swaps the values of PECAM1 and PAX7 in
every cell. PAX7 now has the endothelial profile, so
$`s^{\star}(T_{C}E)=\text{D}=c^{\prime}`$. We confirm these outcomes by
recomputing the reference statistic on every file and on the raw counts.

The loop and noticing without acting. On $`T_{B}E`$, one observation
$`o_{i}`$ in the trajectory of Opus 5.5 leads it to state that “PECAM1
is not in the dataset’s gene list”. Its final answer is nevertheless C,
“the only option left”. The edit reached the observations but not the
answer. This is noticing without acting in the sense of
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Problem formulation ‣ 4 Method: evidence grounding and verified counterfactuals ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists").
On $`E`$ its trajectory and answer are those of a careful analyst.

Agent metrics and routes.
Table [3](#A2.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix B A worked example of the definitions ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
lists the answers of every run on this item. All agents answer C on
$`E`$ and on $`T_{0}E`$, so accuracy and invariance are both 100%. The
runs then separate by route. Haiku flags the withdrawn evidence and
follows the flip to D in all three replicates, which is the *derived*
pattern. Two GPT-5.6-Terra runs give C without a flag under withdrawal
but follow the flip, which is the pattern of *elimination*. The other
options are well-known markers of pericytes, muscle and satellite cells,
so C can be chosen without PECAM1 being measured. The remaining eleven
runs keep C under both edits, which is the *recall* pattern. No run on
this item reacts to withdrawal alone. Accuracy on the item is therefore
16/16, $`R_{\text{rm}}=3/16`$, $`R_{\text{flip}}=5/16`$ and EGA

## Appendix C Benchmarks and tasks

BAISBench. BAISBench \[[8](#bib.bib23)\] evaluates AI scientists on
omics data-driven biological discovery with two tasks, cell type
annotation and scientific discovery. Its scientific-discovery split
(BAIS-SD) poses 193 multiple-choice questions derived from conclusions
reported in 41 published single-cell studies. Each question comes with
the study’s h5ad files and a short background, and one or more options
form the key. The official score is an exact set match with half credit
for a strict subset of the key. Of the 193 questions, 22 can be decided
from the supplied data by a single reference statistic, and 18 remain
after the exclusions below.

GeneBench-Pro. GeneBench-Pro \[[9](#bib.bib17)\] evaluates multistage
statistical reasoning in genomics, quantitative biology and
translational biomedicine with 129 evaluations. Each problem provides a
synthetic dataset generated with known ground truth, brief context and a
target quantity, and an answer passes only if every reported quantity
falls within a problem-specific tolerance. We use the four of its ten
public problems for which a data edit has an exactly known effect on the
ground truth
(Table [5](#A3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix C Benchmarks and tasks ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")).
For the other six, no such edit was available.

The 18 BAIS-SD tasks fall into five evidence families: cell-type
abundance, composition change between conditions, expression
localisation, marker gene and differential expression between
conditions. The four GeneBench-Pro problems cover Hi-C loop strength,
multi-parent QTL mapping, single-cell eQTL and Wright–Fisher selection,
and their ground truth transforms exactly under a case and control swap,
a founder relabelling, an allele recoding or a locus swap.

## Appendix D Agent protocol

Data. Before any intervention, every version, the original included,
passes through one canonical writer. It drops precomputed per-gene
statistics, varm loadings and analysis results in uns, and keeps the
matrices, .raw, layers, cell annotations and embeddings. Gene edits are
applied to X, .raw and all layers. Tables are re-encoded with canonical
gzip headers (the file’s own name and a zero timestamp). Files are
hard-linked into the sandbox under the benchmark’s original names.

Common to all agents. Each run starts in a fresh sandbox with an opaque
random path under a non-listable root and sees only its task files. It
has no web, search or retrieval tools and no user settings, skills,
memories or MCP servers. The prompt is identical across the versions of
a task. Each run has a 40-minute limit. The agent writes answer.json
with the answer, a confidence from 0 to 100, a data-support judgement
(supports, partially supports, does not support, cannot determine) and a
short statement of evidence.

Claude Code runs headless (claude -p) with only the Bash, Read, Write,
Edit, Glob and Grep tools, a private temporary directory and background
tasks disabled.

Codex CLI runs codex exec with web search off and every stateful or
web-capable feature disabled explicitly (memories, browser and computer
use, in-app browser, apps, skill search and goals). A check of all Codex
transcripts found only shell commands, file edits and messages.

Biomni (A1 agent, version 0.0.8) runs with its own loop, system prompt,
know-how documents, tool registry and tool retriever, the model’s API
defaults and its own output limit, and without the data lake, MCP
servers or self-critique. We made two changes. Biomni appends
code-execution results as assistant turns, which current Claude models
reject, so these results are sent in the user role with unchanged
content. And network access is disabled, since name resolution is
refused for every host except the model API and child processes receive
a dead HTTP proxy. Biomni runs in its own Python environment with every
analysis library pinned to the version used by the other agents.

Audit. Every transcript is checked for paths outside the sandbox (other
runs’ workspaces, the stored data versions and our project files, which
contain the answer keys), parent-directory traversal, filesystem-wide
searches, benchmark answer material and network use (URLs, HTTP
libraries and package installation). No run in the analysis was flagged.

## Appendix E Evaluators

Process judge. The judge mimics current process
judges \[[18](#bib.bib16)\]. It receives the task, the benchmark’s
reference answer, the agent’s condensed trajectory (tool calls with
truncated outputs, in order) and the final answer, and it is not told
that the data may have been edited. Each agent message, tool call and
tool output is cut to its first 1,200 characters, and a trajectory
longer than 40,000 characters keeps its first and last 20,000. It
returns whether the answer matches the reference, the quality of the
executed analysis (0 to 10), whether the results the agent obtained
support its answer (yes, partially or no) and an overall score (0 to
10). We analyse the overall score, rescaled to $`[0,1]`$, and the
support rating. The main judge is Claude Opus 5.5.

Cross-family judge. A GPT-5.6-Sol judge graded the first replicate of
every Claude and GPT-5.6-Sol run under both prompts. Its agreement with
the Opus judge on the overall score is 0.91 (Spearman) and 0.89
(Krippendorff’s $`\alpha`$, $`n=306`$). On whether the answer is
supported, weighted $`\kappa`$ is 0.74, and on whether it is correct,
$`\kappa`$ is 1.00. Under the grounding prompt the values are 0.89, 0.87
and 0.85 ($`n=303`$). The agreement sets contain the 288 first-replicate
runs (18 tasks, four agents, four data versions) and 18 later-replicate
runs that both judges also graded, and three runs lack a
grounding-prompt grade.

## Appendix G Gene-name anonymisation

Construction. The follow-up uses the 10 verified BAIS-SD tasks whose
question or options name genes. They come from the marker, expression
and condition differential-expression families. S38_Q3 is excluded
because its question names the gene only by function (“the rod opsin
gene”), which becomes unanswerable once names are hidden. For each task,
every gene symbol is mapped to an opaque identifier by a fixed random
permutation, in var_names and .raw of every file of the original,
withdrawal and flip versions. All other gene annotation columns, such as
symbols, Ensembl identifiers and biotypes, are dropped because they map
back to symbols. The same mapping replaces gene names in marker-style
cell labels (“CD14+ monocytes”) and in the question, options and
background. Gene symbols that double as study vocabulary are kept. These
are cluster identifiers (C1, C2 and so on), “CTRL” and “RPE”, and in
S16_Q1 the mutated genes that name the experimental groups in the data
(TET2 and DNMT3A). The reference statistic, recomputed on every
anonymised file with the mapped names, supports exactly the same option
as before on all 10 tasks, and the option genes no longer appear in the
text. Because the anonymised files are derived from the verified
versions, the interventions are identical.

Runs. The original, withdrawal, flip and no-data versions were run with
the three Claude Code models and both GPT-5.6 models, three replicates
each. The named results in
Table [11](#A7.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Appendix G Gene-name anonymisation ‣ Are We Measuring Scientific Intelligence? Rethinking the Evaluation of AI Scientists")
use the same tasks and replicates from the main runs.

Residual prior. S03_Q3 stays answerable without data after anonymisation
(80%). The models recognise the study from its background and recall its
finding that age-related macular degeneration genes are expressed in
Müller glia, which hiding gene names cannot remove. Without this task,
no-data accuracy falls from 85% to 22% for the Claude models and from
85% to 20% for the GPT models. Flips followed rise from 54% to 93% and
from 48% to 89%, and evidence-grounded accuracy rises from 35% to 65%
and from 13% to 50%.

Withdrawal without names. When the queried gene is deleted, agents check
the three remaining option genes in the data, find that none fits, and
choose the fourth, which they cannot find, by elimination. Their
explanations say so explicitly (for example “G21386 is not in the
dataset, so C was chosen by elimination”). This is why part of the
withdrawal credit survives anonymisation.

Answer from memory, rewarded. After TMPRSS2 is deleted, Sonnet reports

The same failure occurs across scaffolds. When the deep-caries cells are
removed, Claude Code (Opus), Codex (GPT-5.6-Sol) and Biomni (Sonnet 5)

gives 3 of 10.

Elimination and overruling the data. After a flip, Opus finds that HES1
is barely expressed in matrix fibroblasts and chooses it anyway “by
ruling those out, not because the data show it”. Sonnet finds RHO
highest in cones, concludes that “the RHO column looks mis-annotated”,
and answers rods “from known biology, not from the RHO data”. Both are
graded fully correct. Treating a surprising measurement as an error can
be good practice, but here it is applied selectively, to exactly the
evidence that contradicts the expected answer, and the benchmark rewards
it.

Following the evidence, scored zero. On the Hi-C problem, Opus’s case
and control loop strengths on the flipped data are its original
estimates exchanged to the third decimal, and its $`\Delta`$ changes
sign exactly as the ground truth does. Its estimate is outside the
grader’s tolerance on both versions, so it scores 0 on both, the same as
an agent that ignored the swap.
````
