# EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search

Binjie Guo Affiliation: Zhejiang University    Aisheng Mo
Affiliation: Zhejiang University    Ruitong Li Affiliation: The
University of Hong Kong    Xinle Deng Affiliation: Zhejiang University

###### Abstract

AI research agents are increasingly used to search over programs,
mathematical constructions, and proofs. However, existing systems
typically optimize evaluator feedback without adequately governing how
that feedback is interpreted, challenged, and reused. As a result,
promising but fragile candidates can be promoted as discoveries, while
benchmark improvements, finite certificates, and theorem-level claims
are too easily conflated. We introduce EPOCH, an evidence-governed
architecture designed to close this gap. EPOCH implements an
evidence-governed discovery loop by combining explicit task contracts,
typed memory, active falsification, admission checks, and independent
replay, so that each candidate is evaluated against the strength and
scope of the claim it supports. EPOCH achieves state-of-the-art
aggregate performance on AlgoTune, substantially exceeding the strongest
baseline in mean normalized score (0.65 vs. 0.53), and attains the
highest mean score on the internal Math14 suite (0.57). It further shows
favorable held-out behavior under official-test replay and leads the
descriptive aggregate on AgentHPO. Across ten discovery problems, EPOCH
delivers substantial task-specific advances, including improved
executable constructions, optimized algorithms, counterexamples, and
proof-supported results. These advances demonstrate its ability to
convert search into concrete progress across mathematical and
computational domains. Together, the results suggest that evidence
governance is a necessary step toward AI research agents that produce
not only stronger solutions, but also more trustworthy scientific
discoveries.

## 1 Introduction

AI research agents are increasingly being developed not only to solve
well-specified tasks, but also to generate candidate discoveries in
code, algorithms, mathematics, and science. Recent systems have
demonstrated this potential across games, program synthesis, tensor
decompositions, geometric constructions, formal proofs, and scientific
screening ([Silver et al., 2018](#bib.bib25); [Schrittwieser et al.,
2020](#bib.bib24); [Li et al., 2022](#bib.bib16); [Fawzi et al.,
2022](#bib.bib7); [Trinh et al., 2024](#bib.bib27); [Hubert et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Merchant et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)). FunSearch and
AlphaEvolve further show that evaluator-guided evolution can transform
large language models into effective engines for open-ended search
([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)). Despite this progress, current systems are primarily
designed to generate and optimize candidates, rather than to construct
the evidence needed to support a discovery. A promising program,
construction, or conjecture is only a starting point: establishing its
value may require identifying a mechanism, producing a counterexample to
a competing explanation, checking a finite certificate, or
distinguishing an experimentally supported observation from a
proof-backed claim. These supporting objects are often generated,
tested, and recorded only opportunistically. As a result, an agent may
return an improved candidate without a clear account of why it works,
what evidence supports it, or how that evidence can be independently
revisited. The central challenge is therefore to couple search with an
explicit process for constructing, challenging, and retaining the
evidence that turns promising candidates into supported discoveries.

Existing agents combine generation with execution, testing, or
verification, but they do not provide a common protocol for this
evidence-construction process across domains. We ask: _what protocol
should govern the promotion of a candidate into a retained discovery
when the required supporting evidence differs across tasks?_ For code,
this may involve semantic checks, resource measurements, and held-out
replay; for algebra, exact identities, constructions, or obstruction
witnesses; and for theorem discovery, computational evidence and formal
proof with distinct scopes. A scalar reward can guide a search procedure
toward better candidates, but it cannot by itself organize the
generation of supporting evidence, specify the scope of a claim, or
preserve the failed attempts that may explain or challenge it.

We propose EPOCH (Evidence-conditioned Proposal Optimization through
Cumulative Hypothesis-governance,
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")),
an evidence-governed architecture that frames discovery as constrained
search over an explicit evidence ledger. Each task begins with a
contract specifying permissible edits, applicable checks, resource
limits, and the scope of admissible claims. Typed memory retains both
successful and failed trials, allowing later search to reuse supporting
evidence and avoid known failure modes. Candidates are actively
challenged against their declared mechanisms, admitted only when their
support satisfies the task contract, and independently replayed after
the search endpoint is frozen. Rather than making a verifier complete or
elevating finite evidence into a general theorem, EPOCH provides a
domain-general protocol that aligns each retained result with its
supporting evidence and makes the limits of that support explicit.

Figure 1: EPOCH couples discovery search to an explicit evidence ledger.
Successful and failed trials enter typed memory; failures are fed back
into search for repair and refinement (red loop). Challenge, admission,
and independent replay determine whether a candidate is retained, while
the task contract fixes the checks, budget, and claim scope governing
that decision.

Our contributions are:

- •
  Evidence-governed discovery. We introduce a domain-general
  architecture in which task contracts, typed memory, active
  falsification, claim-aware admission, and independent replay jointly
  govern how candidate solutions become retained results.
- •
  Strong exploration and exploitation performance. EPOCH achieves
  state-of-the-art aggregate performance on AlgoTune, the highest mean
  score on internal Math14, favourable official-test replay behaviour,
  and the leading descriptive aggregate on AgentHPO.
- •
  Major discoveries with scoped evidence. Across ten discovery problems,
  EPOCH delivers eleven improved tensor border-rank upper bounds,
  including an explicit 30-path construction for $`T_{\rm ss444}`$; a
  reusable uniform Big-centroid construction; a record-setting
  54-comparison executable procedure for median-of-23 selection; a
  1,512-gate ($`14.10\%`$) matched-PyZX T-count reduction for AES; a
  Lean-checked all-dimensional hypercube determinant inequality with
  complete equality classification; and a Lean-checked nine-element
  counterexample to the proposed $`H_{\beta}`$ coefficient ordering,
  extended to an infinite family.

## 2 Related Work

##### Evaluator-Guided Search and Discovery.

AlphaCode generates programs at scale and filters, clusters, and reranks
them according to their behavior on generated test inputs, whereas
AlphaTensor formulates matrix-multiplication algorithm discovery as a
search for low-rank decompositions of the matrix-multiplication tensor
([Li et al., 2022](#bib.bib16); [Fawzi et al., 2022](#bib.bib7); [Google
DeepMind, 2022](#bib.bib8)). FunSearch couples language-model proposals
to evolutionary selection, and AlphaEvolve extends evaluator-guided
evolution to mathematical and computational tasks ([Romera-Paredes
et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)).
OpenEvolve, AdaEvolve, and AI-Scientist further broaden the landscape of
evolutionary and end-to-end research workflows ([OpenEvolve
contributors, 2026](#bib.bib21); [Cemri et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Lu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib17); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). These
systems establish the effectiveness of evaluator-guided search; EPOCH
complements them by introducing an evidence contract that governs which
candidates may be retained as discoveries, what support their claims
require, and how rejected paths remain available for subsequent search.

##### Domain-Specific Evidence for Discovery.

AlphaGeometry combines learned construction with symbolic deduction,
AlphaProof searches formally checkable Lean states, and GNoME couples
learned screening to first-principles calculations ([Trinh et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27); [Google DeepMind, 2024](#bib.bib10); [Hubert et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Merchant et al., 2023](#bib.bib18); [Google
DeepMind, 2023](#bib.bib9)). Their domain-specific validators are
essential rather than interchangeable. Recent AI-assisted mathematical
studies likewise combine model-guided exploration with expert scrutiny
and, where applicable, formal verification, while distinguishing
suggestive evidence from established mathematical claims ([Anthropic,
2026](#bib.bib3); [OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib20)). EPOCH therefore does not
seek to replace specialist validation with a universal truth score;
instead, it standardizes how domain-specific outputs support discovery.
Typed receipts bind a retained result to the checks that produced its
evidence, three-valued gates distinguish supported, refuted, and
unresolved candidates, and independent replay tests frozen artifacts
outside the search loop. The result is a replayable admission record
that specifies what was checked, what outcome was obtained, and which
claim the available evidence permits.

## 3 Evidence-Governed Discovery

We organize the protocol around three decisions: what counts as an
admissible endpoint, how evidence changes subsequent proposals, and what
may be claimed after independent replay.

### 3.1 Task contract and admissible endpoints

A task contract is $`\mathcal{C}=(D_{\rm pub},D_{\rm term},E,B,A,S)`$:
public and terminal data views, evaluators, resource budget, editable
artifact space, and allowed claim scope. A candidate $`c`$ has objective
$`q_{\mathcal{C}}(c)`$ and evidence bundle $`\xi`$; a claim $`\sigma`$
states what the result asserts. Endpoint selection is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{gathered}(c^{*},\xi^{*},\sigma^{*})\in\arg\max_{c,\xi,\sigma}\ q_{\mathcal{C}}(c)\\
\begin{aligned} \text{s.t.}\quad&c\in A,\quad u_{j}(c,\xi)\leq B_{j},\\
&\operatorname{Correct}_{E}(c;\xi)=1,\quad\operatorname{Challenge}(c;\xi)=1,\\
&\operatorname{Replay}_{E}(c;\xi)=1,\quad\operatorname{Supports}(\xi,\sigma)=1,\quad\operatorname{Scope}(\sigma)\subseteq S.\end{aligned}\end{gathered}
``` |  | (1) |

The maximization ranges only over proposals generated within the
registered run, including a verified fallback when supplied. It is not a
global optimum over all programs or proofs. Gates take values in
$`\{1,0,\bot\}`$, where $`\bot`$ denotes unresolved evidence; only 1
permits admission.

### 3.2 Evidence memory, falsification, and phase control

At step $`t`$, the state is $`s_{t}=(c_{t}^{*},M_{t},z_{t},b_{t})`$:
incumbent, append-only memory, phase, and remaining resources. The
proposal policy emits

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}=(c_{t},H_{t},\widehat{\delta}_{t},R_{t},F_{t})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C},\mathcal{V}(M_{t}),z_{t},b_{t}),
``` |  | (2) |

containing a candidate, hypothesis, predicted effect, evidence
references, and intended falsifier. The bounded view
$`\mathcal{V}(M_{t})`$ exposes relevant prior evidence without rewriting
the ledger. Each observation records artifact and evaluator hashes,
score, correctness, resource use, failure attribution, and rejection
reason. Scientific failure can therefore constrain a later proposal,
whereas an infrastructure incident can trigger bounded recovery without
masquerading as evidence against the method.

A challenge targets the stated mechanism rather than merely repeating
the objective. Examples include semantic boundary inputs, an algebraic
coefficient mismatch, a cokernel witness, counterexample search, or a
proof obligation. Passing a finite challenge supports only its declared
coverage. The controller chooses among explore, refine, recover, and
finalize; it does not imply an unreported learned optimal schedule. If
$`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ contains candidates whose applicable gates pass, the
retained endpoint satisfies
$`c_{t}^{*}\in\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{\mathcal{C}}(c)`$. With a
verified fallback, this gives a protocol-level safety invariant, not
correctness beyond evaluator coverage.

### 3.3 Replay and claim sealing

Terminal replay evaluates a frozen selected endpoint and cannot feed
back into selection. A sealed result binds the contract, endpoint,
ledger, evidence, claim, and scope:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Seal}=h(\mathcal{C},c^{*},M_{T},\xi^{*},\sigma^{*},S).
``` |  | (3) |

Independent implementations may reconstruct semantics rather than rerun
the same checker. For mathematical outputs, the bundle distinguishes
finite computation, general argument, formal kernel, and missing bridge.

## 4 Evaluation

We first compare endpoint quality and held-out behavior, then examine
component removals and the distribution of performance across tasks. The
discovery audit in
Section [5](#S5 "5 From Search to Discovery: Case Studies ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")
asks a separate question: what reusable objects and bounded claims
emerge from this process?

### 4.1 Benchmarks and Estimands

We evaluate EPOCH in three complementary settings: AlgoTune for
constrained algorithmic search, Math14 for diverse mathematical
objectives with exact endpoint checkers, and six AgentHPOBench tasks for
component ablations ([AlgoTune contributors, 2026](#bib.bib1);
[OpenMOSS, 2026](#bib.bib22)). On a frozen 12-task AlgoTune subset, five
methods receive the same budget of 48 semantic calls and are run with
three seeds; we report mean task scores and their macro-average. To test
whether search-time gains transfer beyond development data, we freeze
each eligible final program and replay it, without further search or
adaptation, on 32 previously unused official test inputs. This replay
reruns correctness checks and fresh-process timing. Math14 evaluates
performance across 14 mathematical tasks, whereas AgentHPO isolates the
contribution of EPOCH’s individual components. Full task-level results,
seeds, measurements, and statistical procedures are provided in the
Supplement.

### 5.1 Constructive Discoveries and Algorithmic Optimization

##### Characteristic-two tensor transfer.

Low-border-rank decompositions are fundamental building blocks for fast
bilinear algorithms: each explicit construction provides a concrete
certificate of reduced algebraic complexity. A central difficulty is
that rational decompositions can collapse after reduction to
characteristic two, preventing their direct use over the target field.
EPOCH turns this obstruction into a constructive search signal: it
extracts the incompatible coefficient constraints, uses their witnesses
to select a new degeneration chart, and searches for a valid
characteristic-two replacement. Across the audit, this procedure
transfers all 43 directly applicable formulas and covers 24
coordinate-distinct targets, yielding eleven improved border bounds. In
particular, it discovers a 30-path construction for $`T_{\rm ss444}`$,
establishing $`\brank(T_{\rm ss444})\leq 30`$. The result contributes
explicit low-complexity decompositions in a regime where naïve transfer
fails, expanding the constructive toolkit available for
algebraic-complexity and fast-algorithm design.

##### Uniform Big-centroid construction.

Big-centroid constructions are particularly valuable when they extend
across a family of dimensions: unlike an isolated decomposition, a
uniform family can serve as a reusable object for asymptotic border-rank
and algebraic-complexity analysis. EPOCH discovers such a family,
deriving an explicit $`q+u_{1}`$-term construction, where
$`q=\prod_{s=2}^{d}u_{s}`$, from a reusable left-inverse identity rather
than repeated ambient linear solves. The resulting formula applies
throughout the stated parameter and field regimes, replacing a sequence
of instance-specific searches with a single dimension-general
construction. This shift from individual examples to an explicit
scalable family provides a concrete foundation for studying the
associated complexity bounds and for developing subsequent
centroid-based tensor constructions.

##### Improved median-of-23 selection.

Median selection is a fundamental problem in comparison complexity, and
the classical Hadian–Sobel construction gives a 55-comparison upper
bound for selecting the median of 23 elements ([Hadian & Sobel,
1969](#bib.bib11)). EPOCH improves on this benchmark by treating
over-budget execution paths as reusable counterexamples: it organizes
1,454 difficult cases into sixteen repair policies that eliminate the
remaining excess comparisons. The resulting executable decision
procedure has worst-case cost at most 54 comparisons, establishing
$`V_{12}(23)\leq 54`$ and improving the classical bound by one
comparison. This result demonstrates that failure-guided search can
produce a concrete improvement to a long-standing constructive benchmark
in exact selection.

##### Reversible circuit factor sharing.

T gates are a dominant resource in fault-tolerant quantum computation,
making T-count reduction a central objective in reversible-circuit
optimization ([Kissinger & van de Wetering, 2020](#bib.bib15)). In the
evaluated AES construction, common intermediate factors are computed
independently for multiple outputs, introducing substantial redundant
non-Clifford cost. EPOCH identifies these factors and restructures the
circuit so that each is computed once, shared across the relevant
outputs, and subsequently uncomputed.
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Reversible circuit factor sharing. ‣ 5.1 Constructive Discoveries and Algorithmic Optimization ‣ 5 From Search to Discovery: Case Studies ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")
illustrates this mechanism and shows how the relative saving increases
with the number of factor uses. Under matched PyZX synthesis, the
resulting circuit reduces the T-count from 10,746 to 9,234, eliminating
1,512 T gates and lowering the non-Clifford cost by $`14.10\%`$, while
preserving all 256 input-output cases and restoring every ancilla. This
result provides a substantial resource reduction for a cryptographically
relevant reversible circuit under the stated library and synthesis
model.

pseudocode preserves the input and restores the ancilla.

### 5.2 Mathematical Discoveries

The remaining cases distinguish a checked general statement, an explicit
counterexample, and theorem candidates with open proof bridges.

##### A sharp hypercube inequality with equality characterization.

Let $`w_{v}\geq 0`$ assign weights to the vertices $`v\in\{0,1\}^{d}`$,
let $`W=\sum_{v}w_{v}`$, and let $`m_{i,b}=\sum_{v_{i}=b}w_{v}`$ denote
the mass on the $`b`$-facet in coordinate $`i`$. For the unordered
weighted sum $`\operatorname{SD}(w)`$ of absolute affine simplex
determinants, EPOCH establishes

|     |                                                               |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{SD}(w)W^{d-1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}m_{i,0}m_{i,1}.
       ```                                                            |     | (5) |

For $`d\geq 3`$, equality holds exactly when all mass is supported on a
facet. Thus coordinate marginals sharply bound the global determinant
quantity and characterize its degeneracies. Relating this
finite-polynomial theorem to the source geometric statement still
requires the stated normalization bridge ([Averkov et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)).

##### A stable counterexample to $`H_{\beta}`$ front-loading.

The proposed rank-five $`H_{\beta}`$ front-loading property requires
$`h_{2}^{\beta}\geq h_{3}^{\beta}`$. EPOCH discovers a nine-element
matroid with

|     |                                                         |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H_{0}=(1,9,27,27,9,1),\qquad H_{\beta}=(1,9,31,32,9,1),
       ```                                                      |     | (6) |

and hence $`h_{2}^{\beta}-h_{3}^{\beta}=-1`$, directly refuting the
ordering. A two-line principal-extension mechanism preserves the defect
for every nonnegative extension count, producing an infinite family
while leaving neighboring conjectures unresolved ([Sinclair,
2026](#bib.bib26)).

##### Additional Mathematical Results and Verification Boundaries.

Four further candidates concern a three-state kernel, Full Perazzo
ranks, a Heisenberg limit ([Kammeyer, 2026](#bib.bib13)), and
online-embedding asymptotics ([Altschuler et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)).
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5 From Search to Discovery: Case Studies ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")
summarizes their verified cores and the remaining bridges: boundary
certificates, interpolation and rank arguments, representation limits,
and recurrence asymptotics. Full details appear in the supplement.

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

The benchmarks and portfolio support complementary conclusions.
Aggregate gains show that contracts, typed memory, falsification,
admission, and replay can guide search, while the cases show how
failures become repair strategies and verification turns candidates into
scoped research artifacts. This evidence discipline is particularly
relevant to AI4Math and AI4Science workflows combining code, symbolic
reasoning, simulation, and formal methods.

Two limitations define the next frontier. First, larger paired
evaluations with fixed budgets and comprehensive resource accounting are
needed. Second, although EPOCH makes evidential gaps explicit, it does
not resolve them all; specialist claims still require external review,
statement alignment, and priority assessment. Extending the protocol to
simulations, experiments, databases, scientific instruments, and expert
interaction remains an important direction.

## 7 Conclusion

EPOCH reframes AI research agents from candidate generators into systems
for evidence-guided discovery. By combining typed memory, active
falsification, admission, and independent replay, EPOCH turns
successful, failed, and repaired search trajectories into retained
results with explicit supporting evidence. Across ten discovery
problems, this process yields concrete advances: eleven improved tensor
border bounds, a 1,512-T-gate reduction in an AES reversible circuit, a
54-comparison median-of-23 procedure that improves the classical
55-comparison Hadian–Sobel bound ([Hadian & Sobel, 1969](#bib.bib11)), a
sharp all-dimensional hypercube inequality with an equality
characterization, and an infinite family refuting the proposed
$`H_{\beta}`$ front-loading property. Together with state-of-the-art
aggregate results on AlgoTune and leading performance on Math14 and
AgentHPO, these outcomes show that evidence-governed search can do more
than optimize benchmark scores: it can generate substantive algorithmic
and mathematical discoveries.

### B.4 How discovery-case evidence is constructed

The mathematical and algorithmic cases do not use a statistical
train/test split. Each begins with a frozen problem statement,
comparator, editable object, and claim boundary. Search may inspect
public algebraic identities, source constructions, or finite instances;
admission then binds the selected object to exact verification
obligations. Evidence is constructed in four noninterchangeable layers:

1.  1.
    Semantic reconstruction: rebuild the target independently from its
    definition (for example, tensor coordinates, AES truth tables, or
    selection ranks).
2.  2.
    Exact or exhaustive replay: check every coordinate or every member
    of the declared finite universe when the claim is finite.
3.  3.
    Mutation controls: corrupt a factor, coefficient, gate, recurrence
    term, or certificate field and require the verifier to reject it.
4.  4.
    Formal or analytic bridge: prove the reusable implication connecting
    the checked finite object to the stated theorem, while listing any
    remaining source-specific or representation-specific bridge.

Figure S1: Evidence construction from claim definition to the final
theorem bridge. Exact replay establishes the declared finite object;
mutation controls test sensitivity to selected errors; only the final
bridge transfers those facts to the stated mathematical claim. The
diagram is logical, not a count of experiments.

Discovery examples may guide the search, but the admitted statement is
determined by the replay domain, not by how many exploratory examples
happened to succeed. A finite exhaustive certificate and a sampled
numerical check therefore receive different labels throughout
Sections [I](#A9 "Appendix I Four Algorithmic Discovery Cases ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")–[K](#A11 "Appendix K Auxiliary Results: Exact Classifications, Reductions, and Obstructions ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search").

##### Finite-certificate sufficiency.

Let $`U`$ be the finite universe named by a claim, let $`C`$ be the
admitted candidate, and let $`P(C,u)`$ be the semantic property to be
established for every $`u\in U`$. Suppose an independent constructor
enumerates $`U`$ without omission or duplication, and a sound exact
checker returns true exactly when $`P(C,u)`$ holds. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(|\widehat{U}|=|U|\ \wedge\ \widehat{U}=U\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{u\in\widehat{U}}P(C,u)\right)\Longrightarrow\forall u\in U,\ P(C,u).
``` |  | (S3) |

###### Proof.

Take arbitrary $`u\in U`$. Completeness of the independent enumeration
gives $`u\in\widehat{U}`$, and the accepted checker result for that
element gives $`P(C,u)`$. Since $`u`$ was arbitrary, the universal
finite claim follows. Soundness of the checker and equality
$`\widehat{U}=U`$ are premises, not consequences of a large pass count;
mutation controls can expose violations of soundness but cannot prove
those premises by themselves. ∎

### B.5 Exclusions, deduplication, and contamination controls

A task–method–seed key is counted once in a benchmark matrix. Retries
caused by transport failure retain the same key and provenance; endpoint
replays do not become new observations. Correct but non-improving
proposals retain the endpoint selected by the frozen rule. Incorrect
proposals are recorded as rejected trials, and an available verified
fallback remains the method outcome when the contract provides one. In
case studies, coordinate symmetries and isomorphism reductions may
compress a search, but reported coverage is expanded back to the full
declared universe before admission.

### C.1 Common execution lifecycle

Every registered cell begins from the method-specific frozen revision
and the same task contract. The runner records the initial artifact and
verifier result before model interaction. At intervention $`t`$, the
method receives only the public task view and the information permitted
by its variant. A proposal must parse into the expected schema and stay
within the editable surface. Candidate execution then records
correctness, objective value, resource use, and any task-specific
challenge. EPOCH additionally records evidence references, falsifier
outcome, phase, and admission status. The event is appended even when
rejected.

Endpoint replacement is separate from proposal evaluation. A higher
public score cannot replace a verified incumbent if correctness or
another required gate fails. At the budget boundary, the runner
serializes the selected endpoint and its evidence root. Scheduled replay
reconstructs precisely this serialization in a clean process. The replay
result is linked to, but does not rewrite, the search record. This
lifecycle also explains the ablations: each removal changes a declared
part of the proposal or admission interface, rather than merely hiding a
column during analysis.

### C.2 Evaluation Details for AlgoTune and Math14

For each public AlgoTune instance, correctness is evaluated before
timing. Timing is measured in a fresh process after one warmup run and
aggregated across three measured repetitions according to the
benchmark’s frozen evaluator. Task-level means are computed by first
averaging the three registered seeds, after which all tasks receive
equal weight.

Math14 reports the project-normalized endpoint score returned by each
objective-specific evaluator. Verification status is recorded separately
from method failure: a failed method may still retain a task-defined
fallback score.

### C.3 AgentHPO evaluation details

AgentHPO preserves raw task units: win rate and accuracy are maximized,
whereas MAE, MSE, and RMSE are minimized. The registered paired report
orients each full-versus-removal difference so that positive favors full
EPOCH, collapses to a task direction under its frozen tie rule, and
applies an exact two-sided sign test across non-tied tasks.

Behavior diagnostics are computed from trace events rather than endpoint
scores. Evidence-reference rate asks whether a decision cites a
nonbaseline prior event; duplicate-configuration rate asks whether the
emitted configuration exactly matches the baseline or an earlier
configuration in the same cell; rejection rate records admitted=false.
The alternative macro report aggregates within each task and then
averages six tasks equally.

## Appendix D Detailed Architecture and Evidence Protocol

This section translates the main-text contract into protocol-level
operations. The mathematical notation defines the quantities to preserve
and verify, while the implementation details specify the concrete
settings through which the architecture enforces its invariants.

### D.1 The research task as an executable contract

A task is more than an objective function. It specifies an initial
artifact, editable scope, allowed observations, correctness conditions,
resource limits, and an endpoint rule. For a code task, the artifact may
be a Python implementation of a function with a fixed input/output
interface. For a tensor task, it may be a list of rank-one paths and a
target-coordinate generator. For a mathematical statement, it includes
the hypotheses and the meaning of any finite or formal evidence used to
justify the conclusion. Without this surrounding contract, a high score
is compatible with changing the problem rather than solving it.

The public view and terminal view have different roles. Public instances
can influence proposal generation. A terminal view can evaluate the
selected endpoint only under the declared protocol. If official test
feedback is used to generate a new candidate, that candidate belongs to
a new search experiment; it is not the original endpoint’s replay.
Similarly, a certificate verifier may expose a precise failed coordinate
during search, whereas a held-out benchmark evaluation must not silently
become an additional optimization loop.

A resource envelope has both accounting and attribution semantics.
Candidate execution consumes task resources; proposal generation
consumes semantic calls and associated model resources. A candidate that
exceeds a valid registered time limit has failed within the method
contract. An evaluator that cannot launch because a required device is
unavailable creates a different state. The experiment must identify
which boundary failed before deciding whether the cell has a score, or a
fallback. This distinction is particularly consequential when comparing
methods with different orchestration patterns.

### D.2 Evidence records and information exposed to generation

An event should be interpretable independently of the model’s narrative.
It identifies the exact executable or symbolic candidate, evaluator
version, and input version; metric and correctness fields record
different outcomes; and attribution explains why the result can or
cannot be used. An evidence reference points to this immutable event,
not to a mutable natural-language statement such as “the previous
attempt worked.” A subsequent claim can therefore be traced back to a
specific observation without printing storage-level identifiers in the
paper.

The bounded memory view is a projection of a longer evidence history. A
projection can contain successful trials, rejected proposals, baseline
observations, and relevant counterexamples without reproducing an entire
transcript. This distinction matters because a context-window summary
may omit details while the underlying record still retains them. The
architecture’s auditability claim concerns preservation of the full
record and stable identifiers, not an assertion that the model always
receives every historical event.

A useful proposal has at least four interpretable parts. Its hypothesis
describes the mechanism intended to improve the objective. Its expected
effect states the direction or observable change. Its evidence
references identify earlier observations supporting the intervention.
Its falsifier specifies a condition under which the mechanism should be
rejected. For example, a faster convolution implementation may predict
identical zero-padded linear convolution on rectangular shapes; a
wraparound mismatch is a falsifier even when timing improves. An
algebraic lift may predict a specified coefficient identity; a single
mismatching semantic coordinate refutes that proposal.

Evidence can be useful without being positive. A tensor-chart
obstruction excludes an entire local representation. A failure at a
boundary input shows which invariant an optimization omitted. A repaired
parser incident explains why a method appeared to produce no candidates.
These observations should be stored with different types because they
imply different next actions. Treating them all as low rewards loses
both scientific information and the ability to diagnose the evaluation
itself.

### D.3 Admission as a three-valued decision

Figure S2: EPOCH’s three-valued admission gate. Passing proposals add
verified gains; failed proposals retain the correct incumbent and
contribute a falsifier; unresolved checks preserve the claim as pending.
This separation lets EPOCH learn from adverse evidence while protecting
an already verified endpoint.

Figure [S2](#A4.F2 "Figure S2 ‣ D.3 Admission as a three-valued decision ‣ Appendix D Detailed Architecture and Evidence Protocol ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")
makes the three outcomes explicit. Retention depends on the conjunction
of the applicable gates, not a numerical average of their statuses.

For each applicable gate $`g`$, write
$`g(c)\in\{\mathsf{pass},\mathsf{fail},\mathsf{unresolved}\}`$.
Conjunction passes only if every required gate passes. Any demonstrated
contract violation fails admission. An infrastructure incident that
prevents a required check leaves admission unresolved; it does not
establish either candidate correctness or candidate incorrectness. This
gives an operational form to the main-text admission predicate.

The gates are logically heterogeneous. Parsing checks that the proposed
artifact can be interpreted. Scope checks that changes remain within the
task. Budget checks enforce the candidate’s resource contract.
Correctness checks the task semantics. Challenges test declared weak
points or adverse cases. Replay checks reproducibility of the selected
object.

A benchmark search endpoint and a theorem statement may require
different final gates. A code endpoint can be admitted as correct on the
registered validation instances without being admitted as universally
correct on all inputs. A polynomial identity checked symbolically can
support all values covered by its algebraic hypotheses. A finite
certificate for 1,600 coordinates can support the whole finite tensor
identity because those coordinates span the target object. The same
number of sampled random matrices would have a different evidential
meaning.

The word “proof” in proof admission therefore refers to the required
justification, not to one proof technology. A Lean receipt can be a very
strong terminal object, but only if the formal statement has been
connected to the concrete candidate and task. Conversely, an exhaustive
executable verifier can completely establish a finite property without a
Lean translation.

### D.4 Protocol-level discovery loop

The following pseudocode exposes the sequence of decisions without
adding an unreported search heuristic.

> Input: frozen contract, initial verified fallback when supplied,
> resource ledger, and empty append-only trial history.
> Initialize: record the baseline, its verification receipt, and its
> resource use.
> While an intervention remains allowed:
>  Choose the current phase using the registered controller.
>  Construct the bounded evidence view and request a proposal.
>  Validate its schema, editable scope, and required evidence fields.
>  Evaluate correctness, objective, and the applicable challenge.
>  Attribute failures before deciding score eligibility.
>  Append the candidate, observations, and gate reasons to history.
>  Replace the endpoint only if admission and the comparison rule
> permit.
> Finalize: reconstruct and replay the exact selected artifact.
> Seal: bind the final claim, scope, contract, history, and replay
> receipts.

The pseudocode deliberately separates evaluator observations from
endpoint replacement. A candidate can be informative and still be
ineligible for replacement.

### D.5 Endpoint safety and provenance invariants

Suppose the baseline is verified under contract $`\mathcal{C}`$ and
every endpoint replacement requires all applicable gates to pass. By
induction on the number of interventions, the retained endpoint always
has a passing receipt under that contract. At the base step this is the
registered baseline. At a later step, a failed or unresolved candidate
does not replace it, while an admitted replacement has its own passing
receipt. This invariant explains why a misleading public score need not
corrupt the endpoint.

The invariant is intentionally relative. If the correctness checker
misses a class of inputs, a passing endpoint may still fail outside its
registered coverage. If the checker itself is wrong, the invariant
establishes protocol compliance rather than external truth. This is why
the artifact must preserve the evaluator identity and why independent
semantic reconstruction provides information beyond repeated execution
of the same checker.

Append-only history gives a second invariant. A new event extends the
history from $`M_{t}`$ to $`(M_{t},e_{t+1})`$; it does not change the
candidate or attribution of earlier events.

Claim sealing adds a binding invariant rather than a theorem of
correctness. If the candidate, evaluator, inputs, or claim text changes,
the archival record must create a distinct version. This versioning rule
does not validate a theorem; it only ensures that a receipt cannot
silently migrate to a different claim or artifact.

### D.6 Falsification, duplicates, and phase changes

A repeated configuration can indicate useful replication, accidental
repetition, or a failure to incorporate evidence. The AgentHPO trace
metric counts equality with an earlier emitted configuration; it does
not by itself distinguish those motivations. Consequently, a lower
repetition rate is an operational signal, not a standalone measure of
scientific efficiency. In the deterministic traps, the intended repeated
behavior is specified by construction; in external tasks, endpoint
outcomes remain necessary to interpret it.

A falsifier differs from an ordinary correctness test by being directed
at the proposal’s mechanism. A factor-sharing proposal must preserve ANF
coverage and return ancillas; a certificate compression proposal must
preserve all represented cases; a conjectured inequality must survive
degenerate and equality regimes. The same challenge can discover a
scientific failure and produce a useful input for the next proposal.

Phase changes organize how these observations affect search. Exploration
seeks materially different hypotheses. Refinement invests in supported
mechanisms. Finalization stops search and concentrates on replay and
claim scope.

### D.7 Independent replay and trusted boundaries

Replay independence has several levels. Process independence
reconstructs the same candidate in a clean process. Implementation
independence reconstructs the mathematical or executable target using
another program. Representation independence uses a different
computational model, such as multiplication-matrix norms versus
resultants, or one irreducible finite-field model versus another. Formal
reflection moves selected finite checks into a proof assistant. These
levels can be complementary rather than interchangeable.

A strong replay package makes the interfaces explicit: exact input
object, output statement, invariant checked, failure code, and resource
boundary. For example, a QASM truth-table replay checks an emitted
circuit; it does not verify the optimizer’s claim of global minimality.
A Lean determinant kernel proves an implication from a
characteristic-polynomial hypothesis; it does not supply the
representation-theoretic hypothesis. An exhaustive graph-catalogue pass
verifies every listed record; it does not prove the catalogue contains
every possible graph.

This layered view is also how the supplementary catalogue should be
read. Each entry identifies the mathematical object and the strongest
retained statement, then distinguishes construction, exhaustive finite
checks, analytic arguments, and formal kernels. Results that are locally
complete but globally unresolved remain useful evidence when their
boundary is preserved.

##### Problem and mechanism.

A rational rank-$`r`$ tensor decomposition can fail to reduce directly

|     |                                                   |     |      |

$`p=((a_{q},b_{q},c_{q}))`$ of the Segre-sum parametrization. Its
differential is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d\Phi_{p}(\dot{a},\dot{b},\dot{c})=\sum_{q}(\dot{a}_{q}\otimes b_{q}\otimes c_{q}+a_{q}\otimes\dot{b}_{q}\otimes c_{q}+a_{q}\otimes b_{q}\otimes\dot{c}_{q}).

a reusable algebraic obstruction.

> Clear denominators and assign each term its activation height. For
> each coefficient order, subtract contributions already fixed by lower
> jets. Solve the exact linear residual equation when feasible;
> otherwise retain a separating cokernel witness and revise the chart.
> After completing the jets, replay every coefficient and tensor
> coordinate before admission.

#### I.1.1 Coefficient-level derivation and obstruction certificates

For clarity, write a trilinear factor path as
$`a(\varepsilon)=a_{0}+\varepsilon a_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}a_{2}+\cdots`$,
and similarly for $`b,c`$. The coefficient of order $`k`$ in a rank-one
path is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
[\varepsilon^{k}](a\otimes b\otimes c)=\sum_{i+j+\ell=k}a_{i}\otimes b_{j}\otimes c_{\ell}.
``` |  | (S9) |

At order one, exactly three terms occur, giving the Segre differential.
At order two, the terms involving a new second jet are linear in that
jet, while products of first jets are already known. This is why each
lifting stage separates into a known nonlinear contribution and an exact
linear residual problem. The decomposition is structural, not an
approximation to the coefficient equations.

Suppose a stage asks for $`Lx=r`$ over a finite field. If no solution
exists, row reduction can produce a functional $`\omega`$ satisfying
$`\omega L=0`$ but $`\omega r\neq 0`$; rescale to $`\omega r=1`$ when
applicable. Anyone can verify this witness by multiplication. It proves
that the current residual is outside the active tangent image. It does
not prove that the tensor has no decomposition of the desired rank,
since a new base point, chart, support, or higher-order ansatz changes
$`L`$ and possibly $`r`$.

This distinction is central to the search loop. A witness excludes a
precise local proposal family and can constrain the next representation.
It cannot be admitted as a global tensor-rank lower bound. Repeatedly
increasing a solver timeout would not resolve an exact cokernel
obstruction within the same chart; changing the chart is a
mathematically different intervention.

#### I.1.2 From a truncated identity to a border upper bound

Let the full polynomial rank-one sum be $`F(\varepsilon)`$ and suppose
coefficient verification establishes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{h}T+\varepsilon^{h+1}E_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon^{h+e}E_{e}.
``` |  | (S10) |

For nonzero $`\varepsilon`$, the rescaled tensor
$`\varepsilon^{-h}F(\varepsilon)`$ has rank at most the number of paths,
because the scalar can be absorbed into one factor of each term. As
$`\varepsilon`$ tends formally or algebraically to zero, its limit is
$`T`$. This supplies the border upper bound over the stated closure. It
says nothing about whether $`T`$ itself has a rank decomposition of that
same length without a limiting parameter.

For a tensor power $`N`$, the error degree is at most $`eN`$.
Interpolating at $`eN+1`$ suitable field points recovers the constant
coefficient of the powered rescaled expression, giving the retained
$`(eN+1)r^{N}`$ exactification pattern. Availability of interpolation
points and extension-field descent costs must be accounted for
separately. The ss444 result uses $`e=4`$, yielding $`(4N+1)30^{N}`$;
the later tail-elimination cases improve $`e`$ without necessarily
changing $`r`$.

#### I.1.3 Semantic reconstruction and negative controls

A certificate must be checked against the intended matrix operation, not
only against a tensor imported from the same optimizer. The independent
route reconstructs the operation tensor from input/output matrix
entries, decodes the official rational factors, and checks every
coefficient against those semantics. This separates factor parsing,
target construction, and polynomial expansion as possible error sources.

The ss444 coefficient weights are useful compact diagnostics, but
weights alone cannot prove identity: two different tensors can have the
same number of nonzero coordinates. Admission therefore uses the full
1,600-coordinate comparison, with the weights serving only as a
support-count summary. The one-bit corruption test demonstrates
sensitivity to a chosen factor error. It complements, rather than
replaces, the complete coordinate proof.

#### I.2.1 Why arbitrary-arity sharing differs from pair matching

A pairwise optimizer considers sharing between two residual expressions
at a time. A factor that benefits three or more groups can be
unprofitable in every locally selected pair and still profitable when
its opening cost is paid only once for the whole group. The binary
variable $`y_{f}`$ represents this global activation decision, while the
$`x_{gf}`$ variables assign residual use. Their coupling captures a cost
that cannot be represented by independent residual choices alone.

For a factor used in $`k\geq 2`$ outputs, let $`c>0`$ be its registered
compute–uncompute cost and $`u>0`$ the per-output use cost. Separate
realization costs $`k(c+u)`$, whereas a legal shared schedule costs
$`c+ku`$. Hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
k(c+u)-(c+ku)=(k-1)c>0,\qquad\frac{k(c+u)-(c+ku)}{k(c+u)}=\frac{(k-1)c}{k(c+u)}.
``` |  | (S12) |

This establishes the saving claimed by the schematic whenever the same
factor remains live across all uses and the final inverse computation
restores the ancilla. It does not establish that a factor is
semantically common to those outputs or that routing preserves the same
cost; those are separate certificate obligations.

The optimization model also distinguishes logical sharing from
reversible execution. A classical expression DAG may reuse a value
without specifying how temporary storage is restored. A reversible
circuit must include the computation of the factor, the uses that leave
it available, and the inverse computation returning the temporary
register to its initial value. The opening cost $`c_{f}`$ therefore
includes the registered compute/uncompute expense. A model that omits
the inverse computation would optimize the wrong cost contract.

#### I.2.2 Constructing a circuit from a selected assignment

For each opened factor, construct its value on a designated ancilla
using the registered factor implementation. Apply the residual-specific
controlled operations to the assigned outputs in the scheduled order.
Because the factor register is used as a control and its input data
remain available under the contract, run the inverse factor computation
to reset that ancilla. The emitted schedule is then translated to QASM
and checked as a complete circuit.

This procedure requires more than an optimal integer-program objective.
The assignment must correspond to the intended ANF occurrences; every
occurrence must be accounted for under the representation’s cancellation
convention; control dependencies must be legal; and uncomputation must
preserve outputs. A solver can be correct while the schedule generator
is wrong. The independent ANF and QASM layers address these different
interfaces.

The Boolean identity follows by distributivity in the two-element
Boolean ring. For bits, XOR is addition and AND is multiplication, so
$`(a+b)h=ah+bh`$. Applying the identity at each output index justifies
shared algebraic factors. The formal identity does not encode the entire
ancilla schedule or a decomposition of every CCX into T gates, which is
why the full-circuit and cost checks remain separate.

#### I.2.3 Interpreting the cost comparisons

The first comparison, 25,886 to 10,696 T gates, measures the combined
effect of moving from the frozen pair baseline to the shared
construction. The intermediate 12,362 value reveals an additional
reduction of 1,666 T gates from cross-output merging after
independent-output Hyperfactor optimization. The matched PyZX comparison
isolates a shared-versus-independent contrast under the same
post-processing pass: 10,746 versus 9,234, a difference of 1,512.

These numbers should not be combined as if they were measurements of the
same single intervention. The matched post-processing comparison
provides the cleanest evidence that sharing remains advantageous after
that specific optimizer. The larger pair-baseline difference reflects a
broader change of construction. Neither establishes minimal T count
among all AES circuits, because the factor library, ancilla budget,
synthesis basis, and permitted schedules delimit the search space.

Finally, enumerating all 256 inputs is exhaustive for the registered
eight-bit input contract. It checks the implemented truth table and
ancilla restoration under that contract. It does not directly measure
routing overhead, depth under a physical architecture, or behavior under
noise. The contribution is a verifiable circuit construction and a cost
comparison in a fixed model.

### I.3 All-parameter Big-centroid constructor

For $`d\geq 3`$ and ordered dimensions $`u_{1}\leq\cdots\leq u_{d}`$,
set $`q=\prod_{s=2}^{d}u_{s}`$. Choose full-column-rank matrices
$`V^{(s)}\in\mathbb{F}^{u_{s}\times u_{1}}`$ with nonzero entries and
left inverses $`L^{(s)}V^{(s)}=I`$. This existence condition is part of
the construction contract; a finite set of field tests does not prove
existence over every small field.

For $`J=(j_{2},\ldots,j_{d})`$, define first-mode coordinates
$`a_{J}(t)=\prod_{s=2}^{d}V^{(s)}_{j_{s},t}`$. The perturbation for mode
$`i\geq 2`$ uses

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{L^{(i)}_{t,j_{i}}}{\prod_{s\geq 2,\,s\neq i}V^{(s)}_{j_{s},t}}.
``` |  | (S13) |

Summing over $`j_{i}`$ yields
$`\sum_{j}L^{(i)}_{t,j}V^{(i)}_{j,t^{\prime}}=\delta_{tt^{\prime}}`$.
Thus target linear coordinates appear with the required coefficients and
unwanted coordinates cancel. Subtracting $`u_{1}`$ constant correction
tensors yields a $`q+u_{1}`$-term first-order degeneration. The retained
flattening lower bound matches $`q+u_{1}`$ in the stated Big-centroid
family.

The generic mechanized first-order kernel proves constant cancellation
and the tangent coefficient identity. The constructor separately checks
matrix inverses, denominators, and coordinates. The supplied audit
records 390 parameter/field controls and 278,904 exact identities,
including odd prime fields and $`\mathrm{GF}(2^{k})`$ for tested
$`k\geq 2`$. The $`(2,2,2,3)`$ example over $`\mathbb{F}_{5}`$ has 14
terms, 30,184 ambient coordinates, and degree-0–4 support weights
$`(0,44,288,240,64)`$.

The result is a symbolic constructor with an independent exact verifier
under the stated assumptions, not an improvement in the
matrix-multiplication exponent. Related tensor-centroid theory supplies
context ([Kassabov et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)).

#### I.3.1 The left-inverse identity as a certificate generator

The constructor’s core operation is local cancellation. Fix mode $`i`$
and all indices except $`j_{i}`$. Multiplying the perturbation
coefficient by the base-coordinate factors cancels every denominator
from the modes held fixed. The remaining sum over $`j_{i}`$ is exactly
an entry of $`L^{(i)}V^{(i)}`$. It equals one when the two first-mode
labels coincide and zero otherwise. Thus the same small-matrix identity
both selects desired coordinates and annihilates undesired ones.

This is more informative than solving the full ambient tangent system
independently for each dimension tuple. The latter can certify one
instance but hides why the instance works. The left-inverse construction
exposes a dimension-parametric algebraic mechanism and emits witnesses
whose validation reduces to matrix products, nonzero-denominator checks,
and coordinate expansion. Its usefulness is not tied to one unusually
favorable numerical seed.

The $`q`$ base paths contribute a constant term. The retained
construction expresses that constant as $`u_{1}`$ correction tensors, so
adding their negatives produces zero constant coefficient. The
prescribed first-order perturbations then yield the target. This is the
reason for the $`q+u_{1}`$ term count; it is not inferred from a
numerical fit. The matching flattening argument is needed to call the
bound minimal within the stated family.

Concretely, let $`\mathrm{Flat}(T)`$ be the registered linear flattening
of the Big-centroid tensor. A rank-one tensor maps to a matrix of rank
at most one, so subadditivity gives
$`\operatorname{rank}\mathrm{Flat}(T^{\prime})\leq r`$ for every tensor
$`T^{\prime}`$ of rank at most $`r`$. Matrix rank is upper
semicontinuous under specialization; hence the same inequality holds for
every tensor of border rank at most $`r`$. Once the displayed block
structure is shown to have

|     |                                              |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{rank}\mathrm{Flat}(T)=q+u_{1},
       ```                                           |     | (S14) |

it follows that $`\brank(T)\geq q+u_{1}`$. The first-order construction
gives the reverse inequality, and therefore equality. The remaining
obligation is to prove that block-rank identity uniformly under the
stated field hypotheses; checking finitely many parameter tuples alone
would not suffice.

#### I.3.2 Field assumptions and small-field controls

A formula containing division is meaningful only when its denominators
are nonzero. The constructor therefore supplies the actual coefficient
matrices and their left inverses rather than merely stating that
suitable matrices should exist. In finite fields, the matrix-existence
step needs a field-aware construction. A characteristic-zero
interpolation formula cannot automatically be reduced modulo a prime
that collapses distinct parameters or makes denominators vanish.

The supplied source claims a uniform field construction over odd prime
fields and binary extension fields of degree at least two. Its 390
controls and 278,904 identities provide a finite audit of the
implementation. The generic first-order Lean kernel does not formalize
this full existence theorem. In this manuscript the explicit
left-inverse hypotheses are retained in the displayed construction so
that the general algebraic implication is not confused with the separate
existence and instantiation layer.

The example’s 30,184 ambient coordinates illustrate another distinction:
a sparse target can require checking many zero coordinates. Support
weights $`(0,44,288,240,64)`$ summarize the polynomial path by degree,
but complete verification must check where those coefficients occur. A
correct target weight with an incorrect coordinate assignment would not
establish the intended tensor degeneration.

#### I.3.3 Algorithmic and mathematical roles of the result

As an algorithmic result, Big-centroid is a deterministic generator
producing factor paths and a checkable payload from a dimension tuple
and an admitted field. As a mathematical result, its correctness is a
symbolic implication from the matrix identities, constant cancellation,
and flattening lower bound. These roles are related but not identical: a
general proof without an implementation does not provide an executable
artifact, while finite implementation tests without the general argument
do not establish all parameters.

The symmetric Full Perazzo case developed later uses a different
interpolation organization and has additional Waring-rank and geometric
conclusions. We keep the two case identities distinct to avoid counting
one formula twice as unrelated evidence. They demonstrate a shared
transition from pattern observation to explicit construction, but their
targets, assumptions, and lower-bound arguments differ.

#### I.4.1 Decision-tree correctness versus path-count arithmetic

A path count is not a decision-tree correctness proof. Every comparison
branch must represent the corresponding order relation, every terminal
return must have the requested rank in every total order extending that
relation, and every dispatch must preserve the target rank. The global
count becomes meaningful only after these semantic conditions are
attached to the enumerated states.

Write a residual state as $`(P,r,b)`$, where $`P`$ is the current
comparison poset, $`r`$ the requested zero-based rank, and $`b`$ the
remaining comparison budget. Define $`\mathcal{C}(P,r,b)`$ recursively:
a leaf is valid when its returned element has rank $`r`$ in every linear
extension of $`P`$; an internal comparison of incomparable $`x,y`$ is
valid when both child states extend $`P`$ by the corresponding relation
and satisfy $`\mathcal{C}`$ with budget at most $`b-1`$. Induction on
$`b`$ then gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}(P,r,b)\Longrightarrow\text{every total order extending $P$ reaches a correct leaf in at most $b$ comparisons}.
``` |  | (S16) |

The base case is the universal-rank condition at a leaf. For the
inductive step, every total order satisfies exactly one of $`x<y`$ or
$`y<x`$, so execution enters the matching child; its budget is smaller,
and the induction hypothesis supplies correctness and termination. Thus
the semantic theorem depends on child-state validity and leaf ranks, not
on the number of enumerated paths.

A residual problem can be represented by a finite poset and a target
rank. Removing an element certified below the target decrements the
target rank; removing an element certified above it leaves the rank
unchanged. Canonical relabeling is valid only when it is a bijection
preserving order constraints and transporting the selected item and
target correctly. These simple rules explain why rank-decrement and
relabel corruptions are important negative controls: either mistake can
preserve graph sizes and node counts while changing the selection
problem.

For a policy node with remaining budget $`b`$, each comparison child
must have a smaller budget, and its state must extend the parent’s
comparison outcome. A solved leaf must satisfy the registered rank
criterion. Induction on the budget then verifies termination and
correctness of the residual policy. The supplied Lean stack formalizes
these semantic ingredients and a large generated policy library; it does
not allow the remaining concrete census-binding obligations to be
omitted.

#### I.4.2 Why classification compresses the difficult frontier

The 11,208,192 repaired occurrences do not require that many separately
synthesized strategies. Many residual states are equivalent after valid
reduction and relabeling. Compressing them into 1,454 classes exposes a
much smaller policy-design problem. The dispatcher certificate must
nevertheless account for every original occurrence and verify the
transport into its assigned class.

The three base-depth groups show how the resource bound composes. Depth
32 allows a 22-comparison continuation; depth 33 allows 21; depth 34
allows 20. Each composition is bounded by 54. The 15, 422, and 1,017
class counts sum to 1,454, and their occurrences sum to the repaired
frontier. These equalities audit coverage and arithmetic; they do not
establish the semantics of a single class transfer, which is checked
separately.

Sixteen reusable policies are a further level of compression. They are
not equivalent to sixteen original task instances, and their generated
DAG nodes are not independent experiments. The contribution is the
combination of a census, equivalence-preserving reduction, a small
policy library, and a verified dispatch map. Each layer removes a
different obstacle to independent replay.

### J.1 Hypercube determinant inequality and equality classification

Figure [S6](#A10.F6 "Figure S6 ‣ J.1 Hypercube determinant inequality and equality classification ‣ Appendix J Six Mathematical Discovery Cases ‣ EPOCH: Reliable Discovery through Evidence-Governed Search")
provides a geometric reading of the polynomial statement and its

an exact expansion over the $`\binom{8}{4}=70`$ unordered vertex subsets

$`m_{i,b}=\sum_{v:v_{i}=b}w_{v}`$. Define the unordered determinant
polynomial explicitly by

|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  | (S17) |

where the absolute determinant makes the ordering of
$`U=\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{d}\}`$ immaterial. The retained finite-polynomial
theorem is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{SD}(w)W^{d-1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}m_{i,0}m_{i,1}\qquad(d\geq 1).
``` |  | (S18) |

For $`d\geq 3`$, equality holds exactly when at least one facet mass is
zero. For $`d=1`$, $`W^{0}`$ is interpreted as the polynomial constant
1, including $`W=0`$.

##### Proof mechanism.

If $`W>0`$, normalize to a cube-valued random vector $`X`$. Let
$`\Delta`$ be the affine determinant of $`d+1`$ independent draws. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{SD}(w)=\frac{W^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}\mathbb{E}|\Delta|,\qquad\mathbb{E}\Delta^{2}=(d+1)!\det\Cov(X).
``` |  | (S19) |

Integrality gives $`|\Delta|\leq\Delta^{2}`$, while positive
semidefiniteness and Hadamard’s inequality give

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\det\Cov(X)\leq\prod_{i}\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})=W^{-2d}\prod_{i}m_{i,0}m_{i,1}.
``` |  | (S20) |

Combining the identities and restoring homogeneity proves the
inequality. The $`W=0`$ case is immediate. Equality requires
determinants in $`\{0,\pm 1\}`$ on positive-probability tuples and
diagonal covariance. The supplied formal proof excludes simultaneous
equality with all facet masses positive for $`d\geq 3`$, using rational
pivots, integer clearing, and a two-level separating construction. A
zero facet confines the support to a hyperplane and gives equality
directly.

The reported Lean 4.19 chain proves the finite-polynomial inequality and
equality characterization, including rejection of an incorrect equality
mutation. The relation to the geometric conjecture of [Averkov et al.

bridge and external statement-alignment review; we do not silently
promote the polynomial result to every geometric formulation.

#### J.1.1 Deriving the determinant second-moment identity

Let $`Z=(1,X^{\top})^{\top}`$ and let $`Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{d}`$ be
independent copies. Write the affine determinant as the determinant of
the matrix with these columns. Expanding its square produces a double
sum over permutations. Independence lets each term factor into entries
of the second-moment matrix $`G=\mathbb{E}[ZZ^{\top}]`$. Regrouping the
permutation sums yields

|     |                                                      |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}\det(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{d})^{2}=(d+1)!\det G.
       ```                                                   |     | (S21) |

The block matrix has the form

|     |                                                               |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       G=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu^{\top}\\
       \mu&\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mu=\mathbb{E}X.
       ```                                                            |     | (S22) |

Subtracting the mean contribution through the Schur complement gives
$`\det G=\det(\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]-\mu\mu^{\top})=\det\Cov(X)`$. This
establishes the stated identity without requiring a dimension-specific
polynomial expansion.

The conversion from the ordered random sample to the unordered simplex
polynomial also has a precise multiplicity. Tuples with repeated
vertices contribute zero determinant. Each $`(d+1)`$-element vertex set
appears in $`(d+1)!`$ orders with equal absolute determinant and product
weight. Dividing by this factorial is therefore required; omitting it
would alter the theorem’s normalization. The powers of $`W`$ then
restore the degree-$`2d`$ homogeneous inequality.

#### J.1.2 Equality requires two simultaneous rigidities

The integrality step is sharp exactly when every positive-probability
determinant has absolute value zero or one. The covariance step is sharp
when the relevant covariance matrix satisfies the equality condition of
Hadamard’s inequality. If a facet mass is zero, the support lies in a
coordinate hyperplane, so all full-dimensional determinants vanish and
both sides are zero.

The difficult direction is excluding equality when all facet masses are
positive in dimension at least three. The first inequality constrains
affine determinants of the support, while the second constrains its
mixed moments. Neither condition alone excludes many nondegenerate
distributions. The formal equality argument connects them through the
supplied rational-pivot and separator construction. This is a separate
proof component, not an automatic corollary of writing down the
covariance inequality.

The low-dimensional cases are deliberately outside the stated
$`d\geq 3`$ iff classification. The inequality holds for all positive
dimensions, but equality can have a different structure in dimensions
one and two. Preserving the dimension qualifier prevents a true general
inequality from carrying an overgeneralized equality statement.

#### J.1.3 Formal statement alignment and geometric interpretation

The Lean result checks a finite polynomial built from cube vertices,
weights, and determinant coefficients. The source geometric question
involves zonoids and mixed-volume formulations. Even if the finite
inequality is the central algebraic obstruction, transferring the formal
theorem to an external geometric claim requires the exact reduction, its
normalization, and its hypotheses. A proof assistant cannot fill that
bridge merely because both discussions use the word “Hypercube.”

This case therefore has two distinct strengths: a short reusable proof
mechanism for the inequality, and a reported complete formal chain for
the finite statement including equality. Its remaining external gate is
alignment and priority, rather than an unproved finite polynomial
calculation. That status differs substantially from the kernel-only
formalizations in the later analytic cases.

The counterexample begins with a matroid, not with an arbitrary proposed

#### J.3.1 Reversibility and spectral nonnegativity

Let $`D=\operatorname{diag}(\pi)`$. Reversibility gives
$`DP=P^{\top}D`$, so $`D^{1/2}PD^{-1/2}`$ is symmetric. The centered
matrix $`A=P-\mathbf{1}\pi^{\top}`$ is similar to a symmetric matrix as
well. Its eigenvalues are real, and for every positive integer $`r`$,
$`\tr(A^{2r})`$ is the sum of nonnegative even powers. This is the
spectral input used by the mixing and tail arguments.

The normalization $`W_{ij}=J_{ij}/(\pi_{i}\pi_{j})`$ satisfies
$`\sum_{j}\pi_{j}W_{ij}=1`$. Hence the minimum entry $`m`$ cannot exceed
one. Subtracting $`m`$ from every entry and dividing by $`1-m`$ when
$`m<1`$ gives another nonnegative normalized kernel with a zero entry.
When $`m=1`$, weighted row normalization forces the constant kernel,
which is handled as the endpoint rather than through division by zero.

Under the mixture $`W=(1-\lambda)+\lambda W_{0}`$, the centered operator
scales as $`A=\lambda A_{0}`$. Most terms in $`K`$ therefore scale by
their matrix degree. The term containing $`J_{ij}`$ separates into a
constant-kernel contribution and a $`W_{0}`$ contribution, producing the
degree-eight and degree-nine pieces in the exact formula. Tracking these
degrees explains why an incorrect coefficient or exponent would
invalidate the closure argument.

#### J.3.2 Strictness and the limited graph-density bridge

For a fully positive nonconstant kernel, $`0<\lambda<1`$ and
$`A_{0}\neq 0`$. Similarity to a symmetric matrix implies
$`\tr(A_{0}^{6})>0`$. The last mixing term is then strictly positive
because $`\lambda^{6}(1-\lambda^{3})>0`$. Once boundary nonnegativity is
established, this yields strict interior positivity. This is a precise
sufficient chain, not an appeal to numerical positivity at sampled
interior points.

The supplied source relates $`K`$ to a particular coefficient in a
graph-density expansion for $`K_{5,5}-C_{10}`$. Nonnegativity of that
coefficient is not automatically nonnegativity of the whole
graph-density difference on every kernel. The sharp-10 results use
additional structure on centered rank-one or lumpable families. The
manuscript preserves this boundary to avoid promoting a complete
three-state coefficient theorem into general Sidorenko.

#### J.3.3 What an exact positivity certificate must establish

A rational certificate has three independent obligations. First,
reconstruct the correct numerator from the original functional after
clearing only denominators known to be positive in the chart. Second,
prove the chart and its inequalities cover the assigned boundary region.
Third, expand the proposed nonnegative blocks exactly and show that
their sum equals that numerator. Positive coefficients in a polynomial
produced by an incorrect substitution would not establish the theorem.

The supplied boundary proof separates zero-off-diagonal and
zero-diagonal faces, then treats bounded algebraic regions and an
unbounded spectral tail. The detailed sizes quoted earlier concern the
zero-diagonal branch, not every face. The lower threshold $`L`$ and
upper threshold $`U`$ satisfy $`U-L=u(1-u)r^{2}\geq 0`$ in the
registered nonnegative chart, so the middle interval is well ordered.
Coverage at $`u=0,1`$ and other degenerate denominators must be handled
by the branch contract rather than silently removed.

Weighted pairings and Bernstein transformations are certificates only
after exact reconstruction. Floating optimization may find promising
nonnegative block coefficients; rationalization can change identities or
positivity. The independent expansion and deleted-pairing control check
precisely this transition from a numerical proposal to an exact object.
Full Lean reflection of these large branch certificates is still
outstanding.

##### Cauchy-star interpolation.

##### Ordinary Waring rank.

The retained Schur–Kneser argument for $`F=\sum_{i}X_{i}Q_{i}(Y)+G(Y)`$
gives $`R_{\mathrm{W}}(F)\geq d\rho`$, where
$`\rho=\dim\operatorname{span}\{Q_{i}\}`$. It uses vanishing second
$`X`$ derivatives, orthogonality of coordinatewise product spaces,
simultaneous stabilizer blocks, and a Kneser dimension bound in each
block. For Full Perazzo, $`\rho=q`$. The matching upper bound decomposes
each binary tangent form as

|     |                                              |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       WL^{d-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{j}(L+t_{j}W)^{d}
       ```                                           |     | (S39) |

under the corresponding moment equations. A mechanized binary-tangent
kernel proves that algebraic identity over a characteristic-zero field;
it does not formalize the full Schur–Kneser lower-bound chain or
guarantee every interpolation hypothesis automatically. The resulting
ratio $`dq/(q+n)\to d`$ for fixed $`d`$ gives the stated obstruction to
a smaller universal linear debordering coefficient on this family.
External algebraic-geometric and priority review remains necessary; full
border-VSP classification is not claimed.

#### J.4.1 Why the interpolation family has exactly the required size

There are $`\binom{n+d-2}{n-1}=q`$ subsets $`I`$ of size $`n-1`$,
matching the dimension of homogeneous degree-$`d-1`$ forms in $`n`$
variables. Each $`H_{I}`$ also has degree $`d-1`$, because its product
contains $`d-1`$ omitted $`t`$ indices. The diagonal evaluation matrix
$`H_{I}(v_{J})`$ therefore witnesses interpolation rather than merely
supplying many candidate linear forms.

If $`I\neq J`$ have equal size, some index in $`J`$ is absent from
$`I`$. That index occurs in the product defining $`H_{I}`$, and the
partial-fraction identity makes the corresponding factor vanish at
$`v_{J}`$. On the diagonal, the pairwise-distinct parameter condition
prevents the necessary factors from vanishing. This explains the
off-diagonal zero pattern and the nonzero diagonal without relying on a
finite symbolic example.

The basis property of $`\ell_{I}^{d-1}`$ lets the
$`X_{\alpha}Y^{\alpha}`$ target be expressed in the tangent span of the
$`d`$th powers. The constant identity then cancels the degree-zero part.
The field must allow the scalar operations and degree factors used in
the tangent construction; the displayed Waring statement is therefore
made in characteristic zero rather than casually transferred to small
characteristic.

#### J.4.2 Flattening lower bound and ordinary rank are different arguments

The first derivative space of $`B_{n,d}`$ has dimension $`q+n`$.
Derivatives with respect to the $`X`$ variables span all degree-$`d-1`$
monomials in $`Y`$. Derivatives with respect to the $`Y`$ variables have
positive $`X`$ degree and are independent through the distinguished
terms specified above. The two subspaces have different $`X`$ degrees
and intersect trivially. A rank-one symmetric tensor contributes at most
one dimension to this flattening, so the dimension gives a border-rank
lower bound.

That lower bound does not produce the larger ordinary Waring rank
$`dq`$. Ordinary rank needs the stronger product-space argument. In any
proposed decomposition, vanishing second derivatives in the $`X`$
variables creates algebraic relations among coordinatewise products of
coefficient vectors. The retained Schur–Kneser proof organizes these
relations by stabilizer blocks and sums the resulting block lower
bounds. Its dependencies and equality hypotheses require specialist
verification; the binary tangent identity alone cannot establish the
lower bound.

The upper ordinary-rank construction uses a different count: each of the
$`q`$ tangent monomials is expressed using $`d`$ powers, yielding $`dq`$
terms. Matching this explicit upper bound with the independent lower
bound yields equality. Distinguishing the two proofs prevents the border
construction from being mistaken for a finite exact decomposition of
length $`q+n`$.

#### J.4.3 Binary tangent identity and debordering interpretation

Expanding a proposed block gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{j}\mu_{j}(L+t_{j}W)^{d}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}L^{d-k}W^{k}\sum_{j}\mu_{j}t_{j}^{k}.
``` |  | (S40) |

To obtain $`WL^{d-1}`$, its moments must satisfy
$`d\sum_{j}\mu_{j}t_{j}=1`$ and make every other relevant moment vanish.
The Lean kernel checks this implication from the supplied moment
conditions. Existence of a suitable choice of weights and parameters is
a separate algebraic construction, not a consequence of the implication
alone.

Over an algebraically closed characteristic-zero field, that existence
can be written explicitly. Let $`\zeta`$ be a primitive $`d`$th root of
unity and set $`t_{j}=\zeta^{j}`$ and $`\mu_{j}=\zeta^{-j}/d^{2}`$ for
$`0\leq j<d`$. Orthogonality of roots of unity gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mu_{j}t_{j}^{k}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{j(k-1)}=\begin{cases}1/d,&k=1,\\
0,&0\leq k\leq d,\ k\neq 1.\end{cases}
``` |  | (S41) |

Substitution into the binomial expansion leaves exactly

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
WL^{d-1}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-j}(L+\zeta^{j}W)^{d}.
``` |  | (S42) |

Thus every binary tangent block has an explicit $`d`$-term Waring
decomposition. Applying the identity to the $`q`$ independent tangent
blocks gives $`R_{\mathrm{W}}(B_{n,d})\leq dq`$ with no interpolation
limit in this final ordinary-rank step.

For the matching lower bound, write a putative decomposition as
$`F=\sum_{s=1}^{r}L_{s}^{d}`$ and split each $`L_{s}`$ into its $`X`$
and $`Y`$ parts. The vanishing of every second $`X`$ derivative gives,
for all $`X`$-directions $`a,b`$,

|     |                                              |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sum_{s=1}^{r}a(L_{s})b(L_{s})L_{s}^{d-2}=0.
       ```                                           |     | (S43) |

The first $`X`$ derivatives span the $`q`$-dimensional space generated
by the forms $`Q_{\alpha}(Y)=Y^{\alpha}`$. Grouping equal
$`Y`$-directions into stabilizer blocks turns the displayed quadratic
relations into orthogonality constraints on coordinatewise-product
subspaces. The source Schur–Kneser lemma bounds each block by $`d`$

#### J.5.1 Regular-representation multiplicities and determinant products

The finite quotient has order $`q^{3}`$. Its regular representation
contains each irreducible representation with multiplicity equal to its
dimension. Therefore an irreducible packet determinant must be raised to
that multiplicity when reconstructing the full determinant. The identity
$`\sum_{d\mid q}\varphi(d)n^{2}d^{2}=q^{3}`$ checks the dimension
accounting, but a complete reconstruction must also ensure that the
packets are neither omitted nor duplicated.

Writing $`\mathcal{P}_{d}`$ for the packets of exact central order $`d`$
and $`\Delta_{d,u,v}=1-m^{d}(u-v)`$, the regular determinant has the
product form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\det A_{m,q}|=\prod_{d\mid q}\prod_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{P}_{d}}|\Delta_{d,u,v}|^{d}.
``` |  | (S47) |

Consequently its normalized logarithm is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log D_{q}(A_{m})=\frac{1}{q^{3}}\sum_{d\mid q}d\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{P}_{d}}\log|1-m^{d}(u-v)|.
``` |  | (S48) |

This formula identifies exactly what the limit proof must control:
packet multiplicities, the main $`d\log m`$ contribution, and the
logarithmic error caused by possible proximity of $`u-v`$ to $`m^{-d}`$.

The scalar identity $`\det(I-mT)=1-m^{d}(u-v)`$ simplifies each packet
dramatically. It does not itself determine how frequently a given
$`(d,u,v)`$ occurs. This is why packet algebra and representation
classification are listed as separate verification layers. Incorrect
multiplicities can preserve each packet identity while giving the wrong
normalized finite determinant and limiting exponent.

The reverse characteristic polynomial is convenient because it encodes
$`\det(I-zT)`$ directly. If it equals $`1-cz^{d}`$, substituting $`z=m`$
proves the packet formula. The Lean kernel formalizes this algebraic
implication. The quantum-binomial relation and vanishing lower traces
are the mathematical route to its hypothesis in the registered
representation family; they remain to be connected formally for the full
theorem.

#### J.5.2 Why exact finite controls do not establish the limit

Agreement between direct integer determinants and resultants at
$`q\leq 9`$ tests packet reconstruction, signs, and normalization in
concrete cases. It cannot prove convergence as $`i\to\infty`$. A
limiting formula needs an error estimate that remains valid over all
packets and all levels in the chain. Near-cancellation in factors of the
form $`1-m^{d}(u-v)`$ is especially relevant after taking logarithms.

The retained proof divides the packets into small and large central
orders. Complete Galois products at small orders are nonzero integers,
giving a lower bound that would not be available for an isolated small
complex factor. At large orders, dominant terms and spacing among roots
of unity control the normalized error. The cutoff and uniform estimates
are part of the analytic proof, not parameters chosen to fit the finite
determinant values.

More explicitly, choose a cutoff $`D=D(q)`$ with $`D\to\infty`$ and
$`D=o(q)`$. For $`d\leq D`$, combine every Galois orbit before taking
logarithms. Nonvanishing of the resulting integer norm prevents an
individual near-zero complex factor from producing an uncontrolled
negative contribution; the total small-order contribution is
$`o(q^{3})`$ after normalization. For $`d>D`$, factor

|     |                                                             |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       1-m^{d}(u-v)=m^{d}(u-v)\left(1-\frac{1}{m^{d}(u-v)}\right).
       ```                                                          |     | (S49) |

The first factor supplies $`d\log m`$ plus the root-of-unity term
$`\log|u-v|`$. The spacing estimate controls the latter after complete
packet summation, while $`m^{-d}`$ makes the last logarithm uniformly
summable away from the paired zero modes. Under the retained
packet-counting lemma, the coefficient of $`\log m`$ converges to
$`1/(p+1)`$ and every residual sum is $`o(1)`$ after division by
$`q^{3}`$. Exponentiating therefore gives
$`D_{q}(A_{m})\to m^{1/(p+1)}`$.

Taking logarithms of the claimed limit gives $`(\log m)/(p+1)`$, while
the infinite determinant has logarithm $`\log m`$. Their difference is
$`p\log m/(p+1)`$. This elementary consequence makes the size of the
defect explicit but does not replace the convergence argument. It also
emphasizes chain dependence: a different quotient chain may have a
different packet distribution and a different limit or no limit.

#### J.6.1 The critical equation and its unique positive solution

Define $`f(u)=\sinh(2u)-2u-1`$ for $`u\geq 0`$. Then $`f(0)=-1`$,
$`f(u)\to\infty`$, and $`f^{\prime}(u)=2\cosh(2u)-2>0`$ for $`u>0`$.
Thus continuity and strict monotonicity give exactly one positive root.
This elementary argument is a written derivation here; the supplied Lean
kernel is reported only for the critical identity and coefficient
normalization.

For $`H(u)=(1+2u)\coth(u)/8`$, differentiation gives

|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|

       ``` math

The denominator is positive for $`u>0`$, so $`H`$ decreases before

#### J.6.2 Extracting the logarithmic correction from the recurrence

Let $`a=\cosh u_{*}`$ and $`b=\sinh^{2}u_{*}/6`$. The registered
expansion has the form $`S_{k}-S_{k-2}=a+b/S_{k-2}+O(S_{k-2}^{-2})`$. On
either parity class, set $`x_{j}=S_{2j+\eta}`$. Then

|     |                                                |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{j+1}-x_{j}=a+\frac{b}{x_{j}}+O(x_{j}^{-2}).
       ```                                             |     | (S55) |

Once the recurrence establishes $`x_{j}\sim aj`$, the reciprocal term
contributes $`(b/a)\log j`$ on summation. The quadratic error is
summable. Controlling the difference between $`1/x_{j}`$ and $`1/(aj)`$
refines the result to $`x_{j}=aj+(b/a)\log j+C_{\eta}+o(1)`$ under the
uniform expansion hypotheses.

Here is the summation step in detail. The recurrence first gives
constants $`c_{1},c_{2}>0`$ with $`c_{1}j\leq x_{j}\leq c_{2}j`$ for all
large $`j`$, hence $`x_{j}=aj+O(\log j)`$ by summing
$`b/x_{j}+O(x_{j}^{-2})`$. Therefore

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{x_{j}}=\frac{1}{aj}+O\!\left(\frac{\log j}{j^{2}}\right).
``` |  | (S56) |

Define $`y_{j}=x_{j}-aj-(b/a)\log j`$. Using

|     |                                         |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log(j+1)-\log j=\frac{1}{j}+O(j^{-2}),
       ```                                      |     | (S57) |

the recurrence gives $`y_{j+1}-y_{j}=O((\log j)/j^{2})`$. The series of
these increments converges absolutely, so $`y_{j}`$ converges to a
parity-dependent constant $`C_{\eta}`$. This proves

|     |                                          |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{j}=aj+\frac{b}{a}\log j+C_{\eta}+o(1)
       ```                                       |     | (S58) |

from the uniform one-step expansion, rather than inferring the
logarithmic term from numerical fitting.

Returning to $`k`$ gives $`S_{k}=(a/2)k+(b/a)\log k+C_{k\bmod 2}+o(1)`$,
with the parity-dependent constants absorbing fixed logarithmic shifts.
Squaring produces the cross term $`b\,k\log k`$, while the squared
logarithm and constant interactions are $`O(k)`$. This recovers exactly
the coefficient $`\sinh^{2}u_{*}/6`$ in the displayed expansion for
$`\beta`$.

The derivation clarifies which parts are formal algebra and which parts
require asymptotic control. The critical-value simplification is a
finite identity. Establishing the Bellman residual expansion uniformly,
selecting the appropriate root branch, proving $`S_{k}\to\infty`$, and
summing errors are analytic obligations. They cannot be discharged
merely by checking the coefficient identity in Lean.

#### J.6.3 Finite optimality, family asymptotics, and global extremality

The Arb calculations address the frozen finite optimization problem
through horizon 5,000, using outward rounding so that numerical
comparisons are certified as intervals. The two implementations reduce
the chance of a shared dynamic-programming error. Their agreement on a
finite range does not show that the alternating family is globally
optimal at every larger size.

The asymptotic statement concerns the alternating family itself. Even a
complete proof of that family recurrence and its limit would not
establish that no other family has a smaller limiting objective. The
global extremal question requires a comparison principle over all
allowed permutations or trees. The manuscript keeps these three levels
distinct: finite certified comparisons, the family’s analytic expansion,
and the unresolved all-family extremality claim.

The second-order finite estimates approaching
$`\sinh^{2}(u_{*})/(6\cosh(u_{*}))\approx 0.11`$ are consistency checks
at the $`\sqrt{\beta}`$ scale. The separate coefficient
$`\sinh^{2}(u_{*})/6\approx 0.16`$ belongs to the $`\beta`$ scale.
Keeping the exact symbolic expressions alongside two-decimal
approximations prevents those two coefficients from being conflated.

### K.9 AES CNOT Pareto cleanup

A separate local rewrite begins with the source 20-qubit AES circuit
````
