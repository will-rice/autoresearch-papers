###### Abstract

Long-horizon research agents must decide both how to investigate and
what to investigate next as evidence accumulates. This is hard to learn
because such decisions are sparse in long execution traces, and their
consequences may emerge several investigations later. We introduce
Meta-reasoning for Iterative Research Agents (MIRA), a hierarchical
architecture separating research allocation from execution. An
outer-loop meta-reasoner curates context from a persistent research
record, then writes a work order for the next investigation or ends the
episode. A fresh inner-loop executor carries out each work order, making
execution part of the transition between meta-reasoning actions. Without
policy training, MIRA improves long-horizon inference and allocates
additional compute more effectively in theorem proving and open-ended
neural-architecture research. Its decision boundaries also provide
natural units for credit assignment. At each boundary, we train a
generative critic to forecast expected remaining return from partial
states, outperforming token-level alternatives. Cross-environment
pretraining improves forecasting and adaptation, yielding a transferable
prior for valuing partial progress. We use this prior to initialize
MIRA-AC, a generative actor–critic jointly trained to forecast remaining
return and choose the next investigation, without a separate critic
model. MIRA-AC concentrates policy optimization on meta-reasoning
decisions, enabling efficient long-horizon reinforcement learning
without directly optimizing the longer execution traces they initiate.
Training MIRA-AC on the model’s own proxy hill-climbing signals improves
gold performance across four autoresearch environments; the actor
transfers with cross-environment value initialization. Together, these
results show that meta-reasoning can be learned as an explicit policy
for directing long-horizon autonomous research.

## 1 Introduction

Long-horizon research requires more than executing individual
investigations well. As evidence accumulates, an agent must decide which
hypothesis to test, which result to verify, whether to refine or abandon
a direction, and when to stop. These decisions draw on evidence from
many experiments ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib48); [Zou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27)), which can quickly exceed the model’s context
window ([Kausik et al., 2026](#bib.bib55)). The decisions themselves are
sparse within much longer execution traces, and their consequences may
be delayed ([Sutton and Barto, 2018](#bib.bib72); [Samanta et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib56)): an unsuccessful experiment can eliminate an
important hypothesis, while a locally promising direction can ultimately
lead to a dead end. In this sense, the quality of a research trajectory
often depends less on the outcome of any single investigation than on
the choices that determine what to investigate next. Learning to conduct
research therefore requires isolating the decisions that allocate future
research effort from the executions they initiate and assigning them
credit for their downstream consequences. This motivates the central
question of this work:

_Can a research agent learn what to investigate next from the downstream
consequences of its previous research decisions?_

We view this as a problem of _meta-reasoning_: deciding which
computation a resource-bounded agent should perform next and when it
should stop ([Russell and Wefald, 1991](#bib.bib57)). For research
agents, we take the relevant computation to be an _investigation_.
Sec. [2](#S2 "2 Research as a Sequential Decision Process ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
formalizes research at the level of investigations.
Sec. [3](#S3 "3 MIRA: Exposing Meta-Reasoning Decisions ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
introduces MIRA, a hierarchical architecture that separates deciding
what to investigate from carrying out the investigation. At decision
$`t`$, the outer loop curates evidence from a persistent research record
into an observation $`o_{t}`$. The meta-reasoning policy then samples a
complete decision response $`a_{t}`$, comprising its reasoning followed
by either a work order or a termination decision. For a work order, a
fresh executor carries out the investigation and updates the persistent
research record.

At the meta-reasoning level, the potentially long executor trace is
therefore compressed into one decision-level transition
$`\xi_{t}=(o_{t},a_{t},r_{t},o_{t+1},d_{t})`$, where $`d_{t}`$ denotes
true episode termination. The complete decision response is the action;
its implementation, experimentation, and tool use are part of the
environment transition. This isolates the decisions that determine how
future research effort is allocated from the longer computation used to
execute them.

For a work order, a fresh executor carries out the investigation and
writes its results back to the persistent record, producing $`H_{t+1}`$.
The next outer-loop observation is then constructed from this updated
record; a stopping decision instead terminates the research episode.
Thus, each investigation, however long, is represented as one transition
at the meta-reasoning level.

Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Learning to Forecast the Future Value of Research Progress ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
develops a model of remaining research value. Making research decisions
explicit also creates a natural boundary for value prediction and credit
assignment. Because an investigation’s immediate outcome need not
reflect its eventual contribution, we learn its expected remaining
research return $`V^{\pi}(o_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_{t}\mid o_{t}]`$. We
train a decision-level generative critic to forecast this quantity once
per research decision from the same observation available to the policy.
It uses the language model’s native next-token distribution to represent
beliefs over future returns ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61);
[Bellemare et al., 2017](#bib.bib64)). The critic forecasts the future
potential of the current curated research observation rather than
ranking candidate work orders.

Sec. [5](#S5 "5 MIRA-AC: Learning What to Investigate Next ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
uses this signal to assign delayed credit to the research policy. We
introduce MIRA-AC, a shared generative actor–critic jointly trained to
forecast remaining return and choose what to investigate next from each
curated research observation. Because one model plays both roles, no
separate critic model is required. MIRA-AC reconstructs complete
research episodes to assign delayed credit to research decisions. A
single decision-level advantage is propagated to every sampled token in
the corresponding decision response, including its reasoning and
resulting work order or termination decision. Context curation, executor
generations, experiments, and tool calls receive no direct policy loss.
MIRA-AC therefore concentrates policy optimization on _what to
investigate next_ without needing to directly optimize the much longer
execution trajectories. We train it with a buffered single-rollout
asynchronous optimization scheme ([Hou et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)) that
accommodates variable-duration research episodes. Overall, we show that
jointly training value forecasting and meta-reasoning from the same
curated research observations—while assigning credit directly to the
resulting decisions—can produce a stronger policy.

Sec. [6](#S6 "6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
evaluates each stage in turn. First, untrained MIRA improves
long-horizon inference in theorem proving on IMOProofBench ([Luong et
al., 2025](#bib.bib66)) and in open-ended neural-architecture research
with Residual Matrix Transformers ([Mak and Flanigan, 2025](#bib.bib65))
and Loop Transformers ([Geiping et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)). In the
latter settings, it selects per-cell residual-write gates for RMT and
signed attention with a leaky recurrent update for the Loop Transformer.
Second, a decision-level generative critic forecasts remaining return
more accurately than token-level alternatives, and cross-environment
pretraining improves value forecasting and adaptation. Third, under
proxy supervision, the strongest decision-level policy-learning
configuration improves the primary outcome over token-level regression
across symbolic regression ([Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)), physics
discovery ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), Bayesian causal
discovery on networks from the Bayesian Network Repository ([Scutari,
n.d.](#bib.bib2)), and CPU-based neural-architecture research.
Crucially, training on proxy hill-climbing feedback collected from the
model’s own research trajectories improves gold-evaluation performance
across all four environments. MIRA-AC provides additional gains in
several environments, and a policy trained without BNLearn actor updates
transfers to BNLearn when initialized from a critic pretrained across
environments, including BNLearn. This separation between proxy training
and gold evaluation mirrors autoresearch settings in which proxy
evaluations support inexpensive iteration, while gold evaluation may,
for example, require conducting in vivo experiments ([Stokes et al.,
2020](#bib.bib36)) or running a large-scale production system ([Novikov
et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)). Together, these results show that research
allocation can be effectively governed by an explicit, learnable
meta-reasoning policy rather than remaining an implicit byproduct of
long-horizon generation.

## 2 Research as a Sequential Decision Process

Long-horizon research interleaves two kinds of computation: deciding
_what to investigate next_ and carrying out the chosen investigation.
Our central modeling choice is to place the learning boundary between
them. One research decision is one action; when that action selects an
investigation, the potentially much longer computation used to execute
it is part of the environment transition.

### 2.1 Decision Process

Let $`H_{t}`$ denote the persistent research state immediately before
research decision $`t`$. It contains the current artifact together with
evidence accumulated from earlier investigations and the remaining
research budget. Meta-reasoning includes both evidence curation and
action selection. In this work, we hold curation fixed and train the
core action-selection policy. A fixed context-curation procedure $`C`$
constructs the observation $`o_{t}=C(H_{t})`$ presented to that policy.
Retrieval and summarization used to construct $`o_{t}`$ belong to
observation construction rather than the action.

Conditioned on $`o_{t}`$, the policy selects
$`a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid o_{t})`$, where $`a_{t}`$ is the
complete sampled meta-reasoning response: its emitted reasoning followed
by either a work order or a termination decision. A work order specifies
a research objective, a proposed approach, and criteria for determining
whether the investigation succeeded.

For a work order, an executor $`\mathcal{E}`$ carries out the
investigation. Its execution may contain many model generations, tool
calls, experiments, and revisions, but remains part of a single
environment transition, $`H_{t}\xrightarrow{a_{t},\mathcal{E}}H_{t+1}`$.

Let $`d_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate true termination after decision $`t`$.
For a non-terminal transition, the next observation is
$`o_{t+1}=C(H_{t+1})`$. For a terminal transition, we write
$`o_{t+1}=\bot`$ and use the convention $`V(\bot)=0`$. A termination
decision is itself a terminal research action.

At the meta-reasoning level, the stored transition is therefore
$`\xi_{t}:=(o_{t},a_{t},r_{t},o_{t+1},d_{t})`$. Context resets, executor
restarts, and learner-batch boundaries do not set $`d_{t}=1`$.

Writing $`T=\min\{t:d_{t}=1\}`$, a logical research episode is
$`\tau=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{T})`$.

Thus, the unit of control is the research decision: the policy chooses
_what_ to investigate or whether to terminate, while the executor
determines _how_ a selected investigation is carried out.

### 2.2 Research Feedback and Return

Our environments expose proxy feedback during research and apply a
separate gold evaluation only after final submission. The gold evaluator
reports the task-specific final metrics and is never available to the
agent during the research episode.

#### Proxy feedback.

Let $`q_{t}\in[0,1]`$ be the normalized proxy quality after decision
$`t`$, with $`q_{0}`$ the initial quality, and define
$`\bar{q}_{t}=\max_{0\leq u\leq t}q_{u}`$. The proxy reward is the
improvement in the incumbent,
$`r_{t}^{\mathrm{proxy}}=\bar{q}_{t}-\bar{q}_{t-1}`$. Hence the
remaining proxy return telescopes:
$`G_{t}^{\mathrm{proxy}}=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}^{\mathrm{proxy}}=\bar{q}_{T}-\bar{q}_{t-1}`$.
Each environment uses a monotone proxy normalization fixed before
training. The proxy and gold evaluations need not rank artifacts
identically.

#### Gold-binary feedback.

Gold evaluation may contain continuous metrics as well as an
environment-specific binary success criterion. For the _Gold binary_
training condition, let $`Y\in\{0,1\}`$ denote this terminal success
outcome. Intermediate rewards are zero and the terminal reward is $`Y`$,
so $`G_{t}^{\mathrm{gold\text{-}bin}}=Y`$.

For either feedback signal, we write $`G_{t}=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}`$ for
the undiscounted remaining return. Environment-specific definitions of
$`q_{t}`$ and $`Y`$ are given in
App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").

### 2.3 Remaining Research Value

For a fixed continuation policy and research budget, we define
$`V^{\pi}(o_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_{t}\mid o_{t}]`$. Because $`o_{t}`$
is a curated observation, this is an observation-conditioned value
rather than a value of the complete research state. Under proxy
feedback, $`V^{\pi}(o_{t})`$ is the expected additional best-so-far
improvement from continuing research. Under gold-binary feedback, it is
the probability of success under the continuation policy. It is not the
maximum improvement theoretically attainable from the underlying
research state.

The remainder of the method instantiates this decision process: MIRA
exposes the research actions, a critic forecasts remaining value from
the resulting observations, and MIRA-AC assigns downstream credit to the
policy that selects them.

## 3 MIRA: Exposing Meta-Reasoning Decisions

The decision-level formulation in
Sec. [2](#S2 "2 Research as a Sequential Decision Process ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
requires an agent architecture that exposes individual investigations as
explicit actions while preserving evidence across a much longer research
trajectory. In particular, the system must maintain research state
across investigations, separate the choice of what to investigate from
the computation used to carry it out, and produce a well-defined
decision boundary at which outcomes can later be valued and credited.
MIRA realizes these requirements with a persistent research substrate,
an outer-loop meta-reasoner, and a fresh inner-loop executor.

### 3.1 MIRA Architecture

Separating research allocation from execution requires evidence to
persist across investigations. We thus introduce MIRA, a hierarchical
research harness in which an outer-loop meta-reasoner and an inner-loop
executor are instantiated afresh at each iteration over a persistent
research substrate.

#### Persistent research substrate.

Each task has a Git repository that persists throughout the research
episode. It stores the current artifact, prior experiments and their
outcomes, analyses, code or proof artifacts, and handovers from earlier
executors. The repository implements the persistent research state
$`H_{t}`$; model contexts are temporary.

#### Outer-loop meta-reasoner.

At each decision boundary, the context-curation procedure constructs
$`o_{t}`$ from the persistent record. The meta-reasoning policy then
samples the complete decision response $`a_{t}`$: its emitted reasoning
followed by either a work order or a termination decision. A work order
specifies the next objective, proposed approach, and success criteria.
The outer loop therefore allocates the next unit of research effort.

#### Fresh inner-loop executor.

Each work order launches a fresh execution agent through the Codex
harness ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib74)), with the underlying model
specified by the experiment. It receives the fixed task instructions,
the work order, and access to the persistent workspace and task-specific
tools. The executor performs the investigation, updates the artifact,
and commits a handover recording its evidence and unresolved questions.
Both outer- and inner-loop contexts reset between decisions; only the
persistent research substrate survives.
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
illustrates this separation between research allocation and execution.

## 4 Learning to Forecast the Future Value of Research Progress

Figure 2: Forecasting remaining research progress. The critic receives
the same curated observation $`o_{t}`$ as the policy and forecasts a
distribution over remaining return. Its expectation is
$`V_{\phi}(o_{t})`$.

An investigation’s immediate outcome need not reflect its eventual
contribution. A failed experiment may eliminate an incorrect hypothesis
and enable later progress, while an immediate improvement may lead to a
dead end. We therefore learn a value function $`V^{\pi}(o_{t})`$ at
MIRA’s research-decision boundaries.

The environments report progress in different raw units, so we use the
gold and proxy returns defined in
Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Research Feedback and Return ‣ 2 Research as a Sequential Decision Process ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").
Each environment maps its proxy metric through a fixed
environment-specific transform to a quality in $`[0,1]`$. These
transforms are scaled to distinguish improvements attainable within the
research budget and convert partial trajectories into marginal rewards.
App. [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Defining Proxy Hill-Climbing Signals ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
describes their anchors, relation to gold evaluation, and construction
for new environments.

### 4.1 Decision-Level Generative Critic

The critic produces one value forecast immediately before each research
action. This aligns value forecasting with the unit of control, rather
than with intermediate token prefixes inside the language-model
generation of a decision response.

We parameterize the critic using the language model’s next-token
distribution. Following [Samanta et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib63), we elicit
its beliefs by renormalizing the next-token log probabilities assigned
to a set of single-token labels, each representing a possible
future-return value. Let $`\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1}<\cdots<z_{K}\}`$ denote
these values. For observation $`o_{t}`$,
$`p_{\phi}(z_{i}\mid o_{t})=\exp\ell_{\phi,i}(o_{t})/\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\ell_{\phi,k}(o_{t})`$,
and $`V_{\phi}(o_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{\phi}(z_{i}\mid o_{t})z_{i}`$.

We use support $`\{0.0,0.1,\ldots,1.0\}`$ for proxy returns and
$`\{0,1\}`$ for gold-binary outcomes. The critic forecasts expected
remaining return under the continuation policy; it does not estimate
maximum attainable improvement or score candidate work orders.

### 4.2 Critic Objective

The training target is the Monte Carlo return $`G_{t}`$ from the
complete research episode. For $`z_{j}<G_{t}<z_{j+1}`$, we project the
target onto its neighboring support points by setting
$`y_{j}(G_{t})=(z_{j+1}-G_{t})/(z_{j+1}-z_{j})`$ and
$`y_{j+1}(G_{t})=(G_{t}-z_{j})/(z_{j+1}-z_{j})`$, with all other
components zero. If $`G_{t}=z_{j}`$, then $`y_{j}(G_{t})=1`$. The
projection preserves the target expectation.

For a batch $`\mathcal{B}`$, the critic minimizes

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{critic}}(\phi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(y(G_{t})\,\|\,p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid o_{t})\right).
``` |  | (1) |

Operationally, we select the model’s next-token log probabilities for
the $`K`$ support labels and renormalize them over that support with a
log-softmax. We then evaluate the KL objective above; its
parameter-dependent term is
$`-\sum_{i}y_{i}(G_{t})\log p_{\phi}(z_{i}\mid o_{t})`$, because the
target entropy is constant with respect to $`\phi`$.

### 4.3 Cross-Environment Value Pretraining

Research environments differ in artifacts, tools, and objectives, but
partial research observations may share predictive structure. For a
target environment $`d\in\mathcal{D}`$, leave-one-environment-out (LOO)
pretraining uses trajectories from the source set
$`\mathcal{D}\setminus\{d\}`$, after which the critic is adapted on
target-environment support episodes and evaluated on held-out target
query episodes. The experiments below compare this initialization with
target-only and single-source training.

Value forecasting alone does not change the research policy. The next
section uses downstream outcomes to assign credit to the decisions that
select what to investigate.

### 5.1 Shared Actor and Critic

MIRA-AC uses one language model with shared parameters $`\theta`$. In
actor mode it represents $`\pi_{\theta}`$; in critic mode its restricted
next-token distribution defines $`V_{\theta}`$ as in
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Decision-Level Generative Critic ‣ 4 Learning to Forecast the Future Value of Research Progress ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").

The shared model is initialized from a pretrained critic checkpoint.
Actor and critic objectives use separate optimizer states while updating
the shared model. Thus, unlike a separate actor–critic, value learning
also updates representations used to generate subsequent research
decisions.

### 5.2 Credit Assignment Across Research Decisions

MIRA-AC reconstructs each logical episode $`\tau`$ before computing
learning targets. Context resets and learner-batch boundaries may
partition an episode in storage, but only $`d_{t}=1`$ stops value
propagation.

The critic is trained on the exact return $`G_{t}`$. For actor training,
we use generalized advantage estimation ([Schulman et al.,
2016](#bib.bib70)) with $`\gamma=1`$ and $`\lambda_{T}=1-1/(\alpha T)`$,
with the fixed hyperparameter $`\alpha`$ given in
Table [3](#A8.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix H MIRA-AC Training Details ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").

Because asynchronous collection allows successive decisions in one
episode to use different critic versions, let $`c_{t}`$ denote the
critic version used at decision $`t`$. At rollout time we record the
current baseline $`v_{t}=V_{\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t})`$ and same-version
successor bootstrap
$`\widetilde{v}_{t+1}^{(c_{t})}=V_{\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t+1})`$. The
temporal-difference residual is
$`\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma(1-d_{t})\widetilde{v}_{t+1}^{(c_{t})}-v_{t}`$.

At a non-terminal boundary where the critic version changes, define
$`B_{t}=v_{t+1}-\widetilde{v}_{t+1}^{(c_{t})}`$, where
$`v_{t+1}=V_{\theta^{c_{t+1}}}(o_{t+1})`$; otherwise $`B_{t}=0`$.
Advantages are computed backward as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{t}=\delta_{t}+\gamma\lambda_{T}(1-d_{t})\left(\hat{A}_{t+1}+B_{t}\right).
``` |  | (2) |

The bridge aligns critic baselines across model versions; it does not
modify the Monte Carlo target.
App. [I](#A9 "Appendix I Trajectory Stitching Across Artificial Boundaries ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
gives the complete episode-reconstruction details.

### 5.3 Decision-Level Actor Objective

Although each research action receives one decision-level advantage, the
language model generates it autoregressively. Let $`a_{t,j}`$ denote
token $`j`$ of action $`a_{t}`$, let $`L_{t}`$ be its sampled length,
and let $`b_{t}`$ denote the behavior-policy version that generated it.
Define
$`\rho_{t,j}=\pi_{\theta}(a_{t,j}\mid o_{t},a_{t,<j})/\pi_{\theta^{b_{t}}}(a_{t,j}\mid o_{t},a_{t,<j})`$.
We mask policy-gradient terms outside the allowed importance-ratio range
using
$`m_{t,j}=\mathbf{1}[1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}<\rho_{t,j}<1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}]`$.

For a batch of $`N`$ research decisions, the actor objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{1}{L_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{t}}\left[-m_{t,j}\tau_{A}\hat{A}_{t}\rho_{t,j}+\tau_{\mathrm{KL}}(\log\rho_{t,j})^{2}\right].\end{split}
``` |  | (3) |

Tokens outside the ratio interval are excluded from the policy-gradient
term but remain in the policy-mismatch penalty. The trainable span is
the complete sampled meta-reasoning decision response, including its
emitted reasoning and resulting work order or termination decision.
Prompts, curated observations, executor generations, tool calls, and
experiments are masked.

### 5.4 Buffered Single-Rollout Asynchronous Policy Optimization

Investigations have heterogeneous wall-clock durations, so synchronized
rollout cohorts can leave capacity idle. MIRA-AC instead collects
completed episodes continuously into separate actor and critic buffers,
which trigger updates independently on the shared model. If both are
ready, the critic update is applied first, followed by the actor update
within the same atomic update transaction. The resulting parameters are
published once. A live episode adopts newly published parameters only at
its next research-decision boundary and may therefore span multiple
model versions.

actor or critic buffer fills; live episodes adopt new weights at the
next meta-reasoning boundary. (c) The same language model serves as
actor and critic, forecasting remaining return and generating the next

## 6 Experiments

We evaluate the approach in four stages. First, we ask whether MIRA
improves long-horizon inference before policy training. Second, we test
whether remaining research value can be forecast at MIRA’s decision
boundaries and whether this forecasting capability transfers across
research environments. Third, we evaluate whether decision-level credit
improves the research policy and whether the learned policy transfers
across environments. Finally, we analyze how training changes research
behavior and how MIRA’s decision-level decomposition affects where
policy learning is applied and the cost of collecting long-horizon
trajectories.

These stages place different demands on the experimental setting. To
evaluate MIRA before learning, we use full-scale long-horizon settings
in theorem proving and open-ended neural-architecture research. Value
pretraining and policy learning, however, require many complete research
trajectories. We therefore evaluate the learning components in scalable
autoresearch environments that support repeated training and held-out
evaluation.

### 6.1 Does MIRA Improve Long-Horizon Inference Without Policy Training?

#### Experimental setup.

All comparisons in this subsection use frozen model weights. We compare
GPT-5.5, Codex, and MIRA. GPT-5.5 uses the task interface directly;
Codex operates as one persistent tool-using session; and MIRA assigns
each investigation to a fresh Codex inner loop. The Codex and MIRA
conditions use the same underlying GPT-5.5 model, Codex harness, and
tools, while MIRA adds explicit meta-reasoning and curated context
resets. We test the three conditions on IMOProofBench ([Luong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib66)) and HillClimbBench-RMT, and compare MIRA with Codex
on HillClimbBench-Loop. For theorem proving, we also include
output-token-matched continuation controls.

#### Neural-architecture research.

We introduce HillClimbBench to evaluate MIRA in open-ended experimental

learning. Each task provides proxy evaluations that the agent can use to
hill-climb during research. After submission, a held-out gold evaluation
scores the final design.

#### Residual Matrix Transformer.

The Residual Matrix Transformer (RMT) ([Mak and Flanigan,
2025](#bib.bib65)) replaces the usual vector-valued residual stream of
the Transformer architecture ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib69)) with a
matrix that each layer reads from and writes to. The research problem is
to regulate where attention and MLP updates write into this state
without discarding its matrix structure. HillClimbBench-RMT asks the
agent to improve the architecture under that constraint. We run matched
24-hour GPT-5.5 campaigns under the three orchestration conditions.
GPT-5.5 stays close to conventional training and scaling choices, while
Codex refines an RMT-specific normalization family. After more elaborate
routing mechanisms prove ineffective at scale, MIRA shifts from
normalization and cross-axis mixing to local write control and selects
learned per-cell residual-write gates. MIRA achieves the lowest proxy
and gold loss among the three campaigns
(Fig. [4](#S6.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Experimental setup. ‣ 6.1 Does MIRA Improve Long-Horizon Inference Without Policy Training? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")(b)).
App. [E](#A5 "Appendix E RMT Architecture Identified by MIRA ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
provides the matched controls, architecture, initialization, and
ablations.

#### Loop Transformer.

A Loop Transformer ([Geiping et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)) repeatedly
applies a shared block, increasing effective depth without introducing
distinct parameters at every iteration. Its central challenge is to
revise the recurrent state without erasing information needed by later
applications of the block. HillClimbBench-Loop asks the agent to improve
this information flow. We compare matched 48-hour MIRA and Codex
campaigns using GPT-6 Astra. MIRA first hypothesizes that repeated core
applications overwrite useful state. When an adaptive retention gate
does not beat constant interpolation with the previous state, it retains
the simpler leaky update. After memory and routing alternatives produce
small or inconsistent gains, MIRA hypothesizes that repeated positive
attention may reinforce information that later recurrences struggle to
remove. It arrives at what it calls *signed attention*, which subtracts
one attention read over shared values from another, and tests it against
a matched positive-mixture control before scaling. This resembles a
fixed-coefficient variant of Differential Attention ([Ye et al.,
2025](#bib.bib68)) applied in a Loop Transformer. MIRA achieves lower
NLL than Codex under both proxy and gold evaluation. Both campaigns also
outperform the task reference architecture on the proxy metric. The
selected RMT and Loop designs both regulate updates to persistent model
state: the RMT design scales how strongly attention and MLP branches
write to each matrix cell, while the Loop design allows recurrent
attention to suppress as well as reinforce information and preserves
part of the previous state.

### 6.2 Can We Forecast Remaining Research Value?

Using proxy-marginal returns, we compare three decision-level
critics—scalar, categorical-head, and generative—with scalar and
generative token-level baselines. For each environment, we form five
support/query splits, each with task-disjoint sets of 50 support and 50
query episodes. Under matched conditions, each critic starts from the
same Qwen-27B initialization and trains on all 50 support episodes. We
evaluate it on the query episodes using mean per-episode root mean
squared error (RMSE). For query episodes $`\mathcal{Q}`$, this is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{RMSE}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{\tau}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\tau}}\left(V_{\phi}(o_{t})-G_{t}\right)^{2}}.
``` |  | (4) |

The decision-level generative critic achieves the lowest mean held-out
RMSE among the tested designs, reducing RMSE by an absolute 0.110
relative to its token-level generative counterpart
(Fig. [8](#S6.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 6.2 Can We Forecast Remaining Research Value? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")(a)).
We use this design in the subsequent transfer and policy-learning
experiments.

We test whether critic pretraining improves forecasting in an unseen
research environment. For each target environment, we compare a
target-only model with models initialized by pretraining on one source
environment or on all remaining environments. The latter is the LOO
initialization defined above. We then adapt each critic on 0, 1, 5, 10,
25, or 50 target-environment support episodes and evaluate it on the
same 50 held-out query episodes. This measures how quickly forecasting
improves from each initialization as target-environment data increases.
Across environments, LOO initialization enables better zero-shot
forecasting and faster adaptation than either single-source pretraining
or no pretraining. After adaptation on five target-environment episodes,
it reduces RMSE by an absolute 0.053 compared with target-only training
(Fig. [8](#S6.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 6.2 Can We Forecast Remaining Research Value? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")(b)).
We use pretrained critics to assign delayed credit and train MIRA’s
research-decision policy.

### 6.3 Does Decision-Level Credit Improve the Research Policy?

#### Experimental protocol.

We evaluate on four environments: BNLearn causal discovery, constructed
from the Bayesian Network Repository ([Scutari, n.d.](#bib.bib2));
Physics Discovery ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), SRBench symbolic
regression ([Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)), and HillClimbBench-CPU.
The first three each use 100 tasks. HillClimbBench-CPU has 50
paper-derived tasks, each with two independently initialized rollouts.
Qwen-27B is the underlying model for every MIRA agent role. In trained
configurations, the learned checkpoint is used only for action selection
and value forecasting; curation and execution remain fixed. We organize
the comparison as an ablation ladder over supervision, credit
granularity, critic parameterization, and actor–critic co-training. We
begin with a separate actor–critic that uses token-level scalar
regression and gold-binary outcomes. This *Gold binary / Token
regression* condition is our analogue of single-rollout asynchronous
methods such as SAO ([Hou et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), which typically
optimize all rollout tokens. Restricting the actor loss to the complete
sampled MIRA decision response—its reasoning plus work order or
termination decision—tests the same approach for learning a
meta-reasoning policy. Holding the critic parameterization and
granularity fixed, we replace terminal feedback with marginal
improvements in the proxy metric. Under proxy supervision, i.e.,
training on proxy returns rather than the gold-binary outcome, we then
move value forecasting to MIRA’s decision boundaries using
decision-level scalar regression (*Step regression*) and replace scalar
regression with the decision-level generative critic (*Step
generative*). We evaluate the generative critic under both supervision
regimes, first with separate policy and critic parameters and then with
MIRA-AC training.

Token regression repeats the decision return target at each token prefix
within the meta-reasoning action; the decision-level regression and
generative critics forecast once per MIRA decision. In the
separate-critic variants, the actor starts from the base policy and the
critic from its pretrained checkpoint. MIRA-AC initializes the shared
actor–critic from the pretrained critic checkpoint. Accordingly, this
comparison evaluates the combined MIRA-AC design, not parameter sharing
alone. All comparisons hold the MIRA harness, executor, tools, research
horizon, inference budgets, and environment feedback fixed. Each
environment-specific run makes one pass over a fixed, ordered set of 100
training episodes. The cross-environment run uses 300 episodes,
comprising 100 from each of its three actor-training environments. We
evaluate each run’s final policy checkpoint using gold evaluation.

#### Training with decision-level credit assignment improves long-horizon research.

MIRA-AC shows that long-horizon research can be improved by assigning
credit directly to the meta-reasoning decisions that shape a trajectory.
Trained from proxy hill-climbing feedback across multiple autoresearch
environments, the policy achieves higher gold-evaluation point estimates
than untrained MIRA in all four environments. Among the separate
actor–critic ablations under proxy-marginal supervision, the strongest
decision-level variant improves the primary outcome over *Token
regression* in every environment. *Step generative* performs best on
Symbolic Regression, Physics Discovery, and HillClimbBench-CPU, while
*Step regression* performs best on BNLearn. MIRA exposes each
meta-reasoning decision, and MIRA-AC applies policy loss to its complete
sampled response while masking the much longer executor trace. MIRA-AC
therefore improves research outcomes without directly optimizing the
much longer executor traces, demonstrating the leverage of targeted
credit assignment at meta-reasoning boundaries.

#### Jointly learning to forecast partial progress and act from the same curated research observations produces a stronger policy.

Ordinarily, the critic affects the policy only through the advantages
used in the actor loss. Unlike *Step generative*, which uses separate
actor and critic adapters, MIRA-AC applies both objectives to the same
model with shared parameters. The critic loss therefore directly trains
the policy’s representation to recognize evidence predictive of future
progress. Under proxy-marginal supervision, the environment-specific
MIRA-AC policy further improves the primary outcome on the three
environments where *Step generative* is strongest. BNLearn instead
favors *Step regression*. The critic loss provides an outcome-supervised
auxiliary objective for learning what to investigate next.

#### MIRA-AC demonstrates the viability of training a general meta-reasoning policy.

Beyond the environment-specific ablations, we train a single MIRA-AC
policy from proxy hill-climbing feedback across Symbolic Regression,
Physics Discovery, and HillClimbBench-CPU. The cross-environment policy
matches or exceeds the strongest separate-adapter primary outcome in
every environment. On BNLearn, it matches *Step regression* without
receiving BNLearn actor updates. This experiment builds on the
transferable critic prior established in
Sec. [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Can We Forecast Remaining Research Value? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents"):
MIRA-AC starts from a critic pretrained on all four environments,
including BNLearn, to forecast progress from curated research
observations. Its BNLearn result therefore provides evidence that the
meta-reasoning policy learned from proxy feedback in the other
environments transfers to BNLearn. Together, these results show that
MIRA-AC can be scaled to a general meta-reasoning policy that learns
reusable research strategies for deciding what to investigate across a
variety of research domains.

### 6.4 What Changes After Meta-Reasoning Policy Training?

Beyond final task outcomes, we analyze how policy training changes
research behavior, which generated tokens receive actor loss, and how
context curation affects rollout input costs.

#### Analysis of trained meta-reasoning trajectories reveals notable shifts in research behavior, particularly a greater tendency to target unresolved uncertainty.

We use an LLM judge to compare 400 matched trajectory pairs while hiding
policy identities, using a rubric covering hypothesis revision,
experiment choice, evidence use, robustness testing, and stopping
decisions. The judge first evaluates research behavior without seeing
final outcomes. After outcomes are revealed, it assesses which policy’s
outer-loop behavior contributed more strongly to the corresponding
trajectory’s gold outcome. It favors MIRA-AC in 203 pairs and untrained
MIRA in 131, with 66 tied or unclear; among clear attributions, 60.8%
favor MIRA-AC.

Across environments, the judge more often identifies MIRA-AC
trajectories that revise explanations, test competing ideas, check
whether gains persist, and abandon stalled lines of inquiry. The largest
shifts are an increase in trajectories containing a test that
distinguishes competing ideas, from 16.8% to 28.0%, and a decrease in
trajectories repeatedly trying minor variants of an unproductive idea,
from 33.5% to 18.8%
(Fig. [9](#S6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Analysis of trained meta-reasoning trajectories reveals notable shifts in research behavior, particularly a greater tendency to target unresolved uncertainty. ‣ 6.4 What Changes After Meta-Reasoning Policy Training? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")).
In Symbolic Regression, this appears as changing the functional form in
response to structured errors rather than continuing to tune
coefficients. In Physics Discovery, it appears as choosing experiments
that vary one factor to distinguish competing physical laws. In
HillClimbBench-CPU, it appears as leaving a plateaued sequence of local
modifications for an architectural change tied to the diagnosed
bottleneck. In BNLearn, which receives no actor updates, the transferred
policy targets specific unresolved edges before expanding to broader
graph search.

provides matched case analyses and trajectory excerpts, including cases

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

MIRA-AC learns only part of the meta-reasoning process. The policy that
generates research decisions is trained, while evidence curation and
execution remain fixed. This makes learning tractable and provides a
clean credit-assignment boundary, but it also means that the policy can
act only on evidence surfaced by the curator and can improve outcomes
only through investigations the executor is capable of carrying out.
Relevant evidence omitted during curation cannot be recovered by the
actor or critic, and improvements in the executor may change which
research decisions are valuable. An important next step is therefore to
learn curation, allocation, and possibly execution jointly while
preserving decision boundaries for credit assignment.

The behavioral results raise a broader question: what should a general
meta-reasoner learn about how to conduct research? Policy training
shapes not only task performance, but the model’s research
temperament—how readily it abandons an incumbent, gathers more evidence,
or commits to a larger change. In HillClimbBench-CPU, MIRA-AC’s
willingness to pursue substantial architectural changes can escape local
optima that trap untrained MIRA on some tasks, yet the same willingness
can backfire when preserving and patiently refining the incumbent is
better. The calibration problem recurs elsewhere: a new experiment is
useful while physical laws remain ambiguous but wasteful once they are
resolved; a richer symbolic expression can improve proxy fit while
harming extrapolation; and broad graph search can be inferior to a
one-edge repair. The objective is therefore not maximal exploration or
aggressiveness, but context-sensitive judgment about when to persist,
refine, test, pivot, or stop.

The environments used for policy training cover only a small fraction of
the diversity required to learn this judgment. A general policy will
require a deliberately constructed training distribution spanning
distinct research regimes—exploration and exploitation, bold pivots and
incremental refinement, continued investigation and stopping—so that the
model learns not one research style, but which style and granularity of
investigation a situation warrants. In this work, we demonstrate that
decision-level training improves outcomes, changes research behavior,
and transfers to an environment held out from actor training, providing
a roadmap for scaling toward that goal.

We demonstrate that meta-reasoning updates transfer to an environment
held out from actor training: MIRA-AC improves on BNLearn despite
receiving no BNLearn actor updates. The model is, however, exposed to
BNLearn during value pretraining. Future work should examine broader
transfer to environments excluded from both value pretraining and actor
training.

Our experiments use a single MIRA configuration with the same
Codex-based inner-loop harness across research environments. This holds
the research interface fixed while varying the task. Training across
heterogeneous and evolving inner-loop scaffolds is a natural next step
for testing whether learned meta-reasoning remains useful as execution
capabilities change.

Our learning experiments also depend on proxy feedback. Dense proxy
signals make repeated autoresearch training feasible, but a proxy need
not rank research directions in the same way as the final gold
evaluation. Our results show that proxy-trained policies can improve
gold-evaluated outcomes in the environments studied here, but they do
not establish when such transfer should be expected or how robust
learning is to systematically misaligned proxies. Future work should
characterize the degree of proxy–gold alignment required for stable
policy improvement and develop methods that can learn from sparse gold
feedback without giving up the sample efficiency of intermediate
signals.

The behavioral analysis should likewise be interpreted as descriptive.
Its conclusions rely on an LLM judge assigning process labels and
comparing matched trajectories. These annotations provide evidence that
training changes observable research behavior, but they do not establish
that any individual behavior—such as testing competing hypotheses,
abandoning stalled directions, or making larger pivots—causes the
associated performance difference. More direct interventions on research
strategy, together with human or task-specific behavioral evaluation,
would be needed to identify which learned behaviors are causally useful
and under which research regimes.

Finally, the current optimization results are limited by scale. We train
LoRA adapters rather than all model parameters, and our asynchronous
procedure is evaluated only at investigation durations supported by the
present infrastructure. We have not tested episodes in which individual
investigations last hours or days and a single trajectory spans many
more actor and critic versions. At those timescales, delayed and
heterogeneous feedback, policy staleness, and interference between actor
and critic updates to shared parameters may become substantially more
important. Scaling to full-parameter training and longer-lived research
episodes will therefore require studying update scheduling, value
calibration under changing policies, and stable joint optimization over
much larger temporal gaps.

More broadly, MIRA-AC assumes that research can be decomposed into
meaningful decision boundaries at which accumulated evidence is
summarized, future value can be estimated, and credit can be assigned.
The environments studied here support that decomposition. Whether the
same abstraction remains effective for less structured research, where
useful investigations overlap, branch, or evolve continuously, is an
open question. Testing that boundary is important for determining how
far decision-level meta-reasoning can scale toward more general
autonomous research.

## 8 Conclusion

This work asks whether a research agent can learn what to investigate
next from the downstream consequences of its previous research
decisions. Across the settings studied here, our results support a
positive answer. MIRA represents long-horizon research as a sequence of
explicit meta-reasoning decisions over a persistent research record,
treating the potentially much longer execution of each investigation as
part of an environment transition. This decomposition improves
long-horizon inference even before policy training and provides a
natural boundary at which remaining research value can be forecast and
delayed outcomes assigned to earlier decisions.

Building on this representation, MIRA-AC jointly trains one model to
forecast remaining return from a curated research observation and choose
how to allocate research effort next. Decision-level value forecasting
outperforms token-level alternatives, and decision-level policy learning
improves gold-evaluated research outcomes, including when trained from
proxy feedback, without directly optimizing the longer executor
trajectories. We also find initial evidence that actor updates learned
across research environments can transfer to a held-out actor-training
environment when initialized from a cross-environment value model.

The broader implication is that research strategy need not remain an
implicit byproduct of long-horizon generation. Once decisions about what
to investigate are represented explicitly and linked to their downstream
consequences, they become objects that can be valued, credited, and
improved through experience. This suggests a path toward research agents
that learn not only to execute individual investigations more
effectively, but to improve the strategy by which they decide what
research is worth doing next.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### Rational meta-reasoning.

MIRA builds on the classical account in Principles of
Metareasoning ([Russell and Wefald, 1991](#bib.bib57)), which asks which
computation a resource-bounded agent should perform next and when it
should stop. In long-horizon research, the relevant computations are
investigations: testing a hypothesis, revising an implementation,
gathering evidence, or terminating the search. MIRA makes these
allocation decisions explicit as work orders, thereby turning a
classical meta-reasoning problem into a trainable sequential decision
process.

#### Benchmarks for autonomous research.

Recent benchmarks expose complementary aspects of long-horizon research.
MLAgentBench ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) and MLE-bench ([Chan et
al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) evaluate iterative machine-learning engineering
over executable tasks, while RE-Bench ([Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib12))
compares agents with human experts over increasing time budgets.
PaperBench ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) evaluates end-to-end
replication using author-developed hierarchical rubrics, and
HCAST ([Rein et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) grounds task difficulty in the
time required by skilled humans. ScienceAgentBench ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9)) similarly derives data-driven discovery tasks from
published scientific workflows.

Other environments broaden either the training interface or the space of
research problems. MLE-Dojo ([Qiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) provides
interactive Kaggle environments with normalized rewards suitable for
collecting learning trajectories; PostTrainBench ([Rank et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)) extends autoresearch to coupled post-training
decisions; and FrontierScience ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib11))
targets expert-level scientific tasks. DiscoGen ([Goldie et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26)) supplies procedurally generated algorithm-discovery
tasks with meta-train/meta-test splits, whereas AlgoTune ([Press et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)) evaluates measurable speedups to established
numerical programs. Finally, Frontier-Eng ([Chi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)) characterizes how improvements diminish over extended
engineering runs, and FML-bench ([Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib27))
compares search strategies across controlled landscapes. In addition to
introducing HillClimbBench, we cast several existing environments as
instances of a common research decision process and ask whether research
strategy can be learned across trajectories.

#### Scientific and algorithmic discovery agents.

Several systems automate substantial portions of the research lifecycle.
The AI Scientist ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)) and AI
Scientist-v2 ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)) combine experiment
execution, evaluation, and paper generation, with the latter adding tree
search and structured memory. FunSearch ([Romera-Paredes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)) established language-model-guided evolution as a
mechanism for discovering programs, and AlphaEvolve ([Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)) scales this approach through model ensembles,
evaluation cascades, and quality-diversity search. SAGA ([Du et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44)) extends automation to the evolution of scientific
objectives, while the AI co-scientist ([Gottweis et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)) and AutoDiscovery ([Agarwal et al.,
2025](#bib.bib53)) organize hypothesis development through evolutionary
selection and Bayesian surprise, respectively.

Research agents also differ in how they divide and retain work.
R&D-Agent ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib37)) separates researcher and
developer roles, AutoScientists ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib48))
coordinates decentralized teams through shared experimental memory, and
MARS ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) performs budget-aware tree search
over repository states. AutoSOTA ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)) targets
reproduction and improvement of existing models, LLM-AutoSciLab ([Kabra
et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)) casts discovery as active experimental
design, and Arbor ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)) refines explicit
hypothesis trees. DiscoverPhysics ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43))
evaluates open-ended scientific reasoning in physics, whereas
AInstein ([Mishra et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)) studies iterative critique
using parametric knowledge alone. These systems primarily improve a
fixed model through inference-time organization. MIRA is compatible with
such execution mechanisms, but our central concern is learning the
policy that chooses the next research objective from prior experience.

#### Search, memory, and evolving agent structure.

Existing agents encode a wide range of search biases. AIDE ([Jiang et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib38)) uses explicit drafting, improvement, and
debugging operators; LATS ([Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)) combines
tree search with acting, planning, reflection, and model-generated
values; and LoongFlow ([Wan et al., 2025a](#bib.bib33)) couples
plan–execute–summarize cycles with evolutionary archives.
DataMaster ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)) schedules exploration over
the available budget, AIRA ([Pepe et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)) applies
UCT-style search to neural-architecture discovery, and SciMaster ([Chai
et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)) distributes exploration across multiple
agents. Automated AI-research Speedrunning ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)) provides a controlled autoresearch setting, while
Agent Laboratory ([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)) organizes
research into fixed phases. The diversity of successful scaffolds,
together with the strategy-dependent results of Frontier-Eng ([Chi et
al., 2026](#bib.bib17)) and FML-bench ([Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)),
motivates selecting research actions conditionally rather than
committing to one search heuristic for an entire investigation.

A complementary line of work changes the agent’s persistent artifacts.
SkillGrad ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib20)) treats trajectory diagnoses
as textual gradients, and SkillOpt ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib21))
uses bounded edits and validation to improve reusable skill documents.
Harness self-evolution analysis ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib19))
distinguishes the ability to modify a harness from the ability to
benefit from those modifications, while Compiling Agentic Workflows into
LLM Weights ([Dennis et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) shows that procedural
workflows can be distilled into model weights. MIRA keeps the execution
harness fixed in our learning experiments and instead updates the model
that allocates research effort; evolving the harness and learning the
meta-reasoning policy are therefore complementary directions.

#### Feedback and value learning.

Language feedback can convey substantially more structure than a scalar
reward, as illustrated empirically by LLF-Bench ([Cheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22)) and formalized by Formalizing Learning from Language
Feedback with Provable Guarantees ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)).
Training AI Co-Scientists Using Rubric Rewards ([Goel et al.,
2025](#bib.bib51)) uses rubric rewards to train research-plan
generation, while HypoAgents ([Duan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)) uses
Bayesian belief and entropy updates to guide which hypotheses to refine.
Our experiments instead use scalar, outcome-grounded targets: either a
gold-binary outcome or changes in a proxy experimental metric. This
choice isolates whether delayed research outcomes alone can train value
estimates at MIRA’s decision boundaries; versioned artifacts could
support richer feedback in future work.

MIRA-AC also relates to shared actor–critic representation learning.
A3C ([Mnih et al., 2016](#bib.bib58)) uses a shared backbone with
separate policy and scalar-value outputs, while Phasic Policy
Gradient ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib59)) studies the balance between
useful representation transfer and interference between policy and value
objectives. UNREAL ([Jaderberg et al., 2017](#bib.bib60)) demonstrates
how auxiliary prediction objectives can shape a shared policy
representation. More recent generative approaches express verification
or value estimation through language modeling itself: Generative
Verifiers ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)) formulates verification as
next-token prediction, and GenAC ([Shan et al., 2026](#bib.bib62)) uses
a generative critic for value modeling. MIRA-AC similarly uses one
language model and one next-token interface for both research actions
and return prediction, without adding a scalar head or maintaining a
separately resident critic.

#### Credit assignment in multi-turn agents.

Several methods localize outcome supervision within long interactions.
Credit assignment can also proceed through a learned step-level critic:
Iterative GRPO ([Jiang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib4)) fits a turn-level
$`Q^{\pi}`$ from logged Monte Carlo returns and uses it as the reward
model for single-turn policy improvement in batch-online multi-turn
settings. Credit Assignment with Resets ([Samanta et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib56)) uses counterfactual continuations from intermediate
states, and Tree-GRPO ([Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)) obtains step-level
relative signals from sibling branches that share a prefix.
R1-Searcher ([Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)) learns retrieval behavior
from outcome-only reinforcement learning, StepSearch ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29)) applies step-wise policy optimization to
search-augmented reasoning, and strategy-guided exploration ([Szot et
al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) operates over high-level language strategies.
PokeeResearch ([Wan et al., 2025b](#bib.bib46)) studies
reinforcement-learning design choices for deep-research agents. MIRA-AC
differs in where it places the learning boundary: it reconstructs
complete research episodes but applies policy loss only to the complete
sampled decision responses that allocate future computation. Its
decision-level generative critic assigns delayed credit at those
boundaries, and buffered asynchronous optimization accommodates
investigations with heterogeneous durations.

## Appendix B Experimental Setup

We study four autoresearch environments: BNLearn causal discovery,
Physics Discovery, SRBench symbolic regression, and HillClimbBench-CPU
architecture research. In the reported policy evaluation, BNLearn,
Physics, and SRBench each contain 100 tasks with one rollout per task.
HillClimbBench-CPU contains 50 distinct paper-derived tasks with two
independently initialized rollouts per task. Each rollout permits at
most 20 MIRA iterations.

### B.1 Shared Interaction Protocol

Each task is an isolated, persistent workspace. At every iteration, a
fresh executor receives the MIRA work order, the fixed task prompt, and
stable environment-specific instructions. The prompt identifies the
available data and tools, interaction budget, editable files, submission
artifact, and hidden information. The executor may write analyses and
programs, run proxy evaluators, update its artifact, and leave evidence
for later iterations. The shared procedure governing outer-loop and
executor interactions is described in
App. [C](#A3 "Appendix C MIRA Implementation ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents");
below we specify the environment-facing part of the prompt.

For the three scientific environments, policy training and reported
evaluation use independently initialized trajectories on the same fixed
task identities and seeds. They therefore test new research trajectories
and held-out evaluations within the same tasks, not generalization to
unseen task identities.

### B.2 Defining Proxy Hill-Climbing Signals

Each environment exposes a different proxy measure of progress:
graph-edit distance, trajectory error, expression error, or architecture
performance. We map that measure to a higher-is-better quality
$`q_{t}\in[0,1]`$ after iteration $`t`$, with $`q_{0}`$ denoting the
fixed initial quality. We construct each map from constants in the
environment specification and freeze it before training.

The four maps use natural task-level anchors. BNLearn measures how much
of the empty graph’s structural Hamming distance (SHD) has been removed.
Physics scales trajectory mean squared error (MSE) by position variance
and applies a fixed reciprocal map capped at $`0.95`$. SRBench measures
log-normalized mean squared error (log-NMSE) improvement from the
initial expression toward the float64 numerical floor.
HillClimbBench-CPU measures improvement from the supplied reference
architecture toward the task’s ideal score of one. The exact equations
appear below. The endpoints need not be attainable; they are placed to
distinguish plausible within-budget improvements.

These proxies provide a task-local ordering of partial progress. They
turn changes across successive research iterations into marginal rewards
at a resolution suited to the current model class and fixed research
budget. The mapping remains imperfect: it can miss real progress, reward
a brittle shortcut, or saturate too early. Gold evaluation therefore
remains the final measure of the selected artifact. It stays hidden
during research, while reward depends on increases in best-so-far proxy
quality.

For a new environment, the construction begins with a repeatable proxy
metric that usually moves with the desired outcome. Natural anchors
include a supplied baseline, an empty prediction, or a task-defined
target. A monotone transform can spread raw errors that span orders of
magnitude, while its scale should keep plausible improvements away from
a narrow or saturated range. Invalid artifacts receive no improvement.
The completed map is frozen before policy training. Development tasks
then establish its usable range and its association with gold outcomes;
poor alignment calls for redesign before final evaluation.

The $`q_{t}`$ values define best-so-far progress and marginal reward in
Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Research Feedback and Return ‣ 2 Research as a Sequential Decision Process ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents");
other visible diagnostics do not affect reward. After selection, a
hidden gold evaluation produces the score reported in the results table.
It uses the same hidden target as the proxy for BNLearn and separate
held-out data or conditions for the other environments. A task is solved
when this gold evaluation meets the environment-specific success
criterion.

### B.3 Environment Interfaces

#### BNLearn causal discovery.

We construct 100 tasks from the Alarm (32 tasks), Child (17), Insurance
(24), and Water (27) networks in the Bayesian Network
Repository ([Scutari, n.d.](#bib.bib2)). Each task is a randomly
permuted, anonymized 14–34-node induced subgraph. A linear-Gaussian
structural equation model provides 1,800 training and 600 visible
validation observations.

The agent may analyze the visible data, query validation Bayesian
information criterion (BIC) diagnostics, and, on an $`n`$-node task,
request $`n`$ interventions that report estimated total effects. These
diagnostics do not determine reward, and the executor cannot query graph
accuracy while carrying out an investigation. The final artifact is a
directed acyclic graph (DAG).

After each completed iteration, the harness reports directed structural
Hamming distance (SHD), including missing, extra, and reversed edges.
For the empty-graph SHD $`S_{0}`$ and submitted-graph SHD $`S_{t}`$,
proxy quality is $`q_{t}=\operatorname{clip}((S_{0}-S_{t})/S_{0},0,1)`$.
The best model-authored graph is retained and restored for final scoring
under the same hidden-DAG objective. Its gold score is the normalized
SHD gap closure above, evaluated on the submitted graph; the task is
solved only by exact directed recovery.

#### Physics discovery.

We adapt DiscoverPhysics ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)) into 100
anonymous worlds spanning 12 mechanisms and three difficulty levels. The
agent receives a mission and topology-specific laboratory interface, but
not the governing law. Across 20 MIRA iterations, it may allocate 15
experiment rounds among the first 19; the last iteration is reserved for
finalization. It may validate experiment plans and repeatedly fit
executable candidate laws to all acquired trajectories.

The agent selects an executable law and a short explanation. For
acquired-trajectory MSE $`m_{t}`$ and position variance $`v_{t}`$, proxy
quality is $`q_{t}=0.95/(1+\sqrt{m_{t}/v_{t}})`$. After the fixed
horizon, the law is evaluated once on held-out trajectories and the
explanation is graded once by a hosted GPT-5.5 judge using a hidden
world-specific reference and rubric. Let $`n`$ be trajectory MSE
normalized by the held-out world’s ground-truth trajectory variance, and
let $`e`$ be the explanation score. For an unsolved task, the gold score
is the product of the threshold-scaled components $`0.1/\max(n,0.1)`$
and $`\min(1,e/0.75)`$, capped strictly below one. A task is solved, and
receives a gold score of one, when $`n<0.1`$ and $`e\geq 0.75`$.

#### SRBench symbolic regression.

## Appendix C MIRA Implementation

This appendix describes how MIRA constructs each decision context,
executes the resulting work order, and delegates evaluation and
submission to the task environment.

### C.1 Context Construction

The outer-loop context is rebuilt at every iteration. Its fixed portion
contains the task statement, a compact workspace summary, the latest
proxy evaluation, a structured summary of the immediately preceding work
episode and a pointer to its handover when available, and the remaining
budget. The full episode record and handover text are not copied
automatically. Instead, the fixed curator has read-only access to
repository files, diffs, commit history, and prior work records, from
which it selects a bounded set of decision-relevant evidence. The fixed
portion and this selected evidence form the new decision context.

The policy is instructed to select a bounded objective or coherent
experiment set while considering its information value, cost, remaining
time, and risk to the current solution. Its work order states the
objective, relevant evidence, resource guidance, acceptance criteria,
and stopping condition. The same structure is used for theorem proving,
with experiments replaced by proof construction and verification.

### C.2 Decision and Execution

A fresh executor in the Codex harness receives the work order, its
selected repository references, and the stable environment instructions.
It may inspect and modify the workspace, run tests and authorized tools,
and evaluate candidates. It validates substantive work, commits useful
artifacts and evidence, and leaves a concise handover. Recorded failures
and contradictory results remain available to later decisions through
the repository.

The repository—including the current candidate, experiments, results,
analyses, and handovers—persists across iterations; the model contexts
do not. Any proxy evaluation produced after execution becomes evidence
for the next decision. When an environment maintains an incumbent, it
protects the strongest validated artifact against later regressions.

### C.3 Environment-Controlled Evaluation and Submission

The environment adapter supplies the task-specific prompt, tools, proxy
feedback, reward mapping, submission artifact, and terminal evaluator.
It also determines who authorizes submission: Codex may submit under
inner-loop control, the policy chooses between further investigation and
submission under outer-loop control, and the environment evaluates only
after research ends under external control.

A run ends when a model-visible success condition is met, submission is
authorized, or the iteration or wall-clock budget is exhausted. Gold
evaluation remains outside both model contexts and is applied only to
the selected repository artifact. Environments that retain an incumbent
may restore it before final evaluation.

## Appendix D Semantic Exploration Graphs and Strategic Inertia

We construct the exploration graphs after each run has terminated. The
graphs are used only for analysis and are never shown to the evaluated
system.

#### Comparable evidence projection.

We project every run into the same chronological evidence format: the
common task statement, text authored by the evaluated models, and the
submitted proof. For MIRA, model-authored text includes the outer-loop
policy’s work orders and the executors’ reports; for Codex, it includes
assistant messages. We exclude commands, tool outputs, diffs, commits,
repository metadata, handovers, submission reviews, and external
evaluations from every condition. The same projection is applied at each
forced-continuation checkpoint, and the same downstream extraction
procedure is applied to every inference system.

#### Semantic graph extraction.

A GPT-5.5 pass at temperature zero converts the projected trace into a
graph. A node represents a semantically meaningful proof or research
step, such as an approach, claim, lemma, counterexample, experiment,
candidate, result, or submission. Several raw turns may therefore map to
one node, while one long response may contain several nodes. Nodes
record their status, such as supported, proved, refuted, stalled,
abandoned, or submitted. Typed edges record evidence-supported
relationships including refinement, dependence, support, refutation,
pivoting, supersession, and production of a result. Chronology alone
does not create an edge.

The extractor receives the trajectory content but not the method name,
model name, grader feedback, or final grader score. Every node and edge
must cite a source segment in the projected evidence; outputs without
valid provenance are rejected. The extracted nodes are ordered by first
appearance in the trace. For visualization, the submission ancestry is
placed on a central lane and other connected components are assigned
separate lanes. Shortened labels and display coordinates affect only
presentation, not the analysis.

#### Research-direction annotation.

A separate GPT-5.5 pass at temperature zero groups the fixed semantic
nodes into research directions. A direction is a high-level hypothesis,
proof plan, construction, mechanism, or algorithmic approach. Lemmas,
tests, repairs, and refinements remain in the same direction when they
serve the same high-level plan; a materially different plan receives a
new identifier, and a later return to a previous plan reuses its
identifier. This pass may correct the order of fixed nodes when their
provenance establishes a different first-introduction order, but it may
not add, remove, split, or merge nodes. It observes node content,
provenance episode identifiers, and graph relations, but not the method
or model name, final grader score, grader feedback, node status,
terminal submission, graph title, or graph summary.

#### Strategic inertia.

Two consecutive non-submission semantic steps form a continuation when
they belong to the same annotated research direction and a pivot
otherwise. For graph $`\mathcal{G}`$, we define
$`I(\mathcal{G})=N_{\mathrm{continue}}/(N_{\mathrm{continue}}+N_{\mathrm{pivot}})`$.
The root and terminal submission are excluded. Graphs with fewer than
two non-submission nodes have no transitions and receive no inertia
value. The statistic is descriptive: high inertia can sustain a
promising direction or prolong a local minimum, while low inertia can
expose alternatives or reflect premature switching.

For the natural GPT-5.5 comparison, the analysis contains 30 problems,
three trials, and three inference systems. We compute inertia for each
run, average trials within each problem, and obtain 95% confidence
intervals by resampling problems with 10,000 bootstrap draws. Pairwise
contrasts match runs by problem and trial, average the paired
differences within each problem, and again bootstrap problems. Natural
and forced-continuation runs are analyzed separately.

## Appendix E RMT Architecture Identified by MIRA

#### Matched search protocol.

We run one 24-hour GPT-5.5 campaign for each orchestration condition.
All three campaigns use the same task reference architecture, proxy
evaluator, compute budget, and parameter limit. Proxy evaluations guide
research, and the final submitted architecture is scored separately by
the gold evaluator.

#### Selected architecture.

An ordinary RMT stores each token’s residual state as a matrix
$`X_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{k}\times D_{v}}`$. Attention and MLP
branches read this state through a lower-rank interface, produce a
matrix update $`\Delta X_{\ell}^{(b)}`$, and add it uniformly as
$`X_{\ell,\mathrm{out}}^{(b)}=X_{\ell,\mathrm{in}}^{(b)}+\Delta X_{\ell}^{(b)}`$
for $`b\in\{\mathrm{attn},\mathrm{mlp}\}`$. MIRA’s final model retains
this matrix state and low-rank read/write interface, uses a GELU-gated
linear unit (GEGLU) feed-forward block, and introduces a separate
learned gate for each branch and layer:
$`X_{\ell,\mathrm{out}}^{(b)}=X_{\ell,\mathrm{in}}^{(b)}+G_{\ell}^{(b)}\odot\Delta X_{\ell}^{(b)}`$,
where $`G_{\ell}^{(b)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{k}\times D_{v}}`$. Every
coefficient is initialized to one, so the new update is exactly the
ordinary additive update at initialization. The two gates add only
0.056% to the model’s parameter count. Unlike a generic GEGLU block,
this mechanism is specific to the matrix residual stream: it learns
where each branch can amplify or suppress a proposed matrix-state write.

MIRA first tested the gate at small scale, then reran a final-scale
comparison matched in geometry, seed, optimizer, and training tokens.
Relative to the ungated GEGLU control, the gated model improved
last-token cross-entropy by 0.009 at small scale and by 0.021 at the
final scale. Both comparisons use one seed, and the ungated
final-geometry control was not submitted to the gold evaluator. These
ablations support the gate as a promising mechanism, but they do not
isolate it as the cause of the full gold-loss improvement in
Fig. [4](#S6.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Experimental setup. ‣ 6.1 Does MIRA Improve Long-Horizon Inference Without Policy Training? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")(b).

The learned gates did not remain near their identity initialization.
Attention gates generally amplify writes, whereas MLP gates generally
suppress them, and both vary with depth. A two-way additive
decomposition into row and column effects explains roughly three
quarters of the variation in the depth-averaged attention map and nearly
nine tenths in the MLP map, with stronger variation along the
residual-key axis. This supports a structured, branch-, depth-, and
address-dependent *write-allocation* interpretation. It does not
establish dynamic routing: the gates are fixed across tokens and
examples, and their low exact-map correlation across layers does not
support a clean permanent partition into “durable” and “temporary”
cells.

Figure 16: Architecture progression for the Loop Transformer study. A

#### MIRA search trajectory.

MIRA begins by hypothesizing that repeated applications of the shared
core overwrite useful state. It compares a learned adaptive retention
gate with a constant interpolation between the previous and proposed
states. The learned gate does not outperform this control, so subsequent
candidates retain the simpler leaky update. After explicit fast and slow
states, iteration-history mechanisms, and other routing alternatives
produce small or inconsistent gains, MIRA hypothesizes that repeated
positive attention may reinforce information that later recurrences
struggle to remove. It tests the resulting subtractive attention
mechanism against a matched positive-mixture control, then promotes it
only after the advantage persists across seeds and model scales. The
selected design combines what the campaign calls “signed attention” with
a leaky recurrent update
(Fig. [16](#A6.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ Matched search protocol. ‣ Appendix F Loop Transformer Search and Selected Designs ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")).
If the two branches produce attention reads $`A_{1}`$ and $`A_{2}`$ over
shared values, the attention module returns $`A_{1}+(A_{1}-A_{2})`$.
Each branch separately uses ordinary softmax attention; the subtraction
creates effective positive and negative coefficients over the shared
values. The second branch copies the first branch’s query and key
parameters at initialization, making the additional contrastive term
exactly zero before training. Training can then break this symmetry,
allowing the effective coefficients to suppress as well as reinforce
value components. After the block proposes $`F(h^{(k)})`$ from recurrent
state $`h^{(k)}`$, the leaky update mixes the previous and proposed
states, $`h^{(k+1)}=\rho h^{(k)}+(1-\rho)F(h^{(k)})`$, with fixed
$`\rho=0.25`$. The attention variant and leaky update therefore play
complementary roles: one supports subtractive routing, while the other
limits how completely a recurrent step can overwrite its input. Applied
in a Loop Transformer, this mechanism resembles a fixed-coefficient
variant of Differential Attention ([Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib68)). It
uses two full query–key branches initialized identically and the fixed
mixture $`2A_{1}-A_{2}`$.

#### Comparison.

Codex selects a tangent-state update with local/global attention routing
and learned write gates on both the attention and MLP branches, a
complementary strategy that regulates how aggressively each recurrent
pass changes the state. MIRA’s incumbent improves proxy loss at every
completed scale. Its final proxy NLL is 3.0331, compared with 3.2419 for
Codex and 3.2940 for the task reference. On the separate gold
evaluation, its NLL is 3.0464, compared with 3.2275 for Codex and 3.2653
for the task reference.

## Appendix G Critic Training and Validation

#### Targets and value contracts.

All critic variants use the stitched Monte Carlo target $`G_{t}`$. Under
proxy-marginal supervision, $`G_{t}`$ is the future increase in
best-so-far proxy quality, where $`q_{t}`$ is defined for each
environment in
App. [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Defining Proxy Hill-Climbing Signals ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").
The corresponding proxy qualities are directed-SHD gap closure for
BNLearn, acquired-trajectory normalized fit for Physics Discovery,
validation log-NMSE gap closure for SRBench, and initial-to-ideal
proxy-score gap closure for HillClimbBench-CPU.

Under gold-binary supervision, the label $`Y\in\{0,1\}`$ records whether
the final submission meets the environment’s gold success criterion.
This means exact directed recovery for BNLearn; the joint
hidden-trajectory and explanation criterion for Physics Discovery; the
ID-test pointwise-accuracy criterion for SRBench; and a positive lower
endpoint of the paired 95% confidence interval for improvement over the
reference architecture for HillClimbBench-CPU. These are the same
success criteria specified in
App. [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents");
the policy-comparison win rate is not a critic target.

#### Critic parameterizations and objectives.

For proxy-marginal targets, a scalar critic minimizes
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scalar}}(\phi)=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(V_{\phi}(o_{m})-G_{m})^{2}`$.
For the categorical critics, let $`z_{1},\ldots,z_{K}`$ be the ordered
support. If $`z_{j}<G_{t}<z_{j+1}`$, we set
$`y_{j}(G_{t})=(z_{j+1}-G_{t})/(z_{j+1}-z_{j})`$ and
$`y_{j+1}(G_{t})=(G_{t}-z_{j})/(z_{j+1}-z_{j})`$, with all other entries
zero; if $`G_{t}=z_{j}`$, then $`y_{j}(G_{t})=1`$. A categorical head
produces
$`q_{\phi}(z_{i}\mid o)=\operatorname{softmax}(h_{\phi}(o))_{i}`$, while
the generative critic uses the restricted next-token distribution
$`p_{\phi}(z_{i}\mid o)`$ from
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Decision-Level Generative Critic ‣ 4 Learning to Forecast the Future Value of Research Progress ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").
They minimize
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cat}}(\phi)=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(y(G_{m})\,\|\,p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid o_{m}))`$,
replacing $`p_{\phi}`$ with $`q_{\phi}`$ for the categorical head. For
the generative critic, the implementation gathers the next-token log
probabilities of the support labels, renormalizes them across that
support, and evaluates the KL objective in
Sec. [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Critic Objective ‣ 4 Learning to Forecast the Future Value of Research Progress ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents");
no probability mass outside the support enters the critic loss.

Under gold-binary supervision, the scalar critic outputs a single logit
$`\ell_{\phi}(o)`$ and represents value as
$`V_{\phi}(o)=\sigma(\ell_{\phi}(o))`$. It is trained with binary
cross-entropy with logits,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bin}}(\phi)=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left[Y_{m}\log\sigma(\ell_{\phi}(o_{m}))+(1-Y_{m})\log\!\left(1-\sigma(\ell_{\phi}(o_{m}))\right)\right].
``` |  | (6) |

The gold-binary generative critic instead restricts the next-token
distribution to the support $`\{0,1\}`$ and uses the corresponding
one-hot target. Token-level variants repeat the decision return at each
causal token prefix within the action; decision-level variants forecast
once from the curated observation.

#### Target and forecast validation.

Proxy-marginal returns are validated and clipped to
$`G_{t}\in[0,1-\bar{q}_{t-1}]`$. Before bootstrapping,
temporal-difference targets, or advantage estimation, proxy-marginal
forecasts must be finite and are clipped to the same interval. For a
generative critic,
````

## Appendix I Trajectory Stitching Across Artificial Boundaries

MIRA reconstructs a fresh curated context at every research decision.
Context-window limits, compaction, sequence packing, and learner
batching may further split a single logical episode into multiple
samples. None of these boundaries is an environment termination.
Treating them as terminal either removes future TD residuals from
earlier advantages or places later rewards at the wrong temporal
distance.

Let a logical episode $`\tau=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{T})`$ be partitioned
into stored samples $`\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{K}`$. The
partition does not alter the termination indicator: $`d_{t}=1`$ only
when the environment truly terminates. Each stored transition contains
$`o_{t}`$, $`a_{t}`$, $`r_{t}`$, $`o_{t+1}`$, actor behavior log
probabilities and version $`\theta^{b_{t}}`$, and the critic version
$`c_{t}`$ assigned to the transition. For that critic version, the
system records both the current baseline
$`v_{t}=V_{\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t})`$ and its successor bootstrap
$`\widetilde{v}_{t+1}^{(c_{t})}=V_{\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t+1})`$.

An asynchronous episode may adopt a different critic before decision
$`t+1`$. At a nonterminal boundary where this occurs, we use the bridge
$`B_{t}`$ from
Sec. [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Credit Assignment Across Research Decisions ‣ 5 MIRA-AC: Learning What to Investigate Next ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents"),
and set $`B_{t}=0`$ otherwise. Terminal successor values and bridges are
zero. The implemented residual and backward recurrence follow
Sec. [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Credit Assignment Across Research Decisions ‣ 5 MIRA-AC: Learning What to Investigate Next ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
and
Eq. [2](#S5.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 5.2 Credit Assignment Across Research Decisions ‣ 5 MIRA-AC: Learning What to Investigate Next ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents").
The bridge aligns the outgoing critic’s successor baseline with the
baseline recorded by the critic version assigned to the next decision.

After this episode-level pass, $`\hat{A}_{t}`$ is scattered back to its
original sample and token span for actor training. The critic target is
the Monte Carlo return $`G_{t}`$, computed separately over the same
reconstructed episode. The recorded value forecasts are treated as
constants during the actor update, so the policy loss does not
differentiate through the shared critic baseline.

#### Partition invariance.

For any partition of a fixed logical episode into stored samples,
stitching produces the same actor advantages and Monte Carlo critic
targets as processing the unpartitioned episode, provided the logged
transitions, critic versions, and successor bootstraps are preserved.
Stitching restores the same ordered rewards, terminal indicators,
baselines, and cross-version bridges. A backward induction on the
recurrence therefore yields the same $`\hat{A}_{t}`$ at every decision,
while every reward remains in the same Monte Carlo return.

In our implementation, $`\gamma=1`$ and the critic is trained on the
exact Monte Carlo return $`G_{t}=\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}`$. Actor advantages
use episode-length-adaptive $`\lambda_{T}=1-1/(\alpha T)`$, giving
$`\hat{A}_{t}=\delta_{t}+\lambda_{T}(1-d_{t})(\hat{A}_{t+1}+B_{t})`$.
The full Monte Carlo return receives weight
$`\lambda_{T}^{T-1}\geq 1-1/\alpha`$ at the earliest decision and
greater weight at later decisions. The bridges correct
baseline-coordinate changes but do not alter the Monte Carlo critic
target. Stitching remains necessary: $`G_{t}`$ must include rewards from
all later MIRA iterations, even when those iterations occupy different
contexts, samples, or learner batches.

The governing invariant is that only true environment termination may
stop value bootstrapping; infrastructure boundaries never do.

## Appendix K Behavioral Analysis of MIRA-AC

This appendix examines the changes we observe in the model’s
meta-reasoning and scientific behavior as a result of training. We
compare the cross-environment MIRA-AC policy with untrained MIRA on 400
matched evaluation pairs (800 trajectories): 100 pairs each from
symbolic regression, Physics Discovery, HillClimbBench-CPU, and BNLearn.
Each pair contains the two policies’ trajectories on the same evaluation
instance. We call the held-out evaluation result the _gold outcome_. In
both systems, an outer policy selects the next research direction, an
inner executor carries it out, and the harness manages execution and can
force continuation after an incomplete iteration.

### K.1 Analysis Protocol

We use GPT-5.5 as an LLM judge in two passes. In the first pass, the
judge sees the sequence of work orders, executor reports, and proxy
feedback under anonymous labels A and B, but not the policy identities
or gold outcomes. It records what each trajectory’s policy and executor
did, how they responded to evidence, and whether the policy’s next
action could resolve an uncertainty visible at the time. This assessment
is fixed before the second pass.

In the second pass, the judge receives this fixed assessment and each
side’s gold outcome; policy identity remains hidden. It separately
assesses whether a decision was reasonable when made and whether it
ultimately helped or harmed the gold result. The judge also attributes
each claimed contribution among the outer policy, inner executor,
harness, and unobserved factors. The pair-level verdict used below asks
which side’s outer-policy decisions contributed more to its own final
outcome. The verdict excludes executor-only and harness-only
contributions. Policy identity is revealed only after both passes are
complete. All 400 pairs produced valid judgments in both passes.

For behavior rates, the unit is a trajectory, not an action. A
trajectory is counted once for a behavior if the second-pass judge
identifies at least one instance attributable at least partly to the
outer policy; repeated instances do not increase the count. Behaviors
attributed only to the executor, harness, or unobserved factors are
excluded. The exception is _productive risk_, a whole-trajectory label
indicating that a reasonable exploratory bet ultimately improved the
gold result. Every rate therefore has a denominator of 400 trajectories
per policy.

The matched comparisons describe observed differences between the
policies. They do not estimate the effect of independently inducing a
single behavior: executor stochasticity, interactions among decisions,
and the evaluation environment can also affect the final result. The
aggregate rates establish how common each behavior was; the excerpts
below show what those behaviors looked like in individual trajectories.

### K.2 Pooled Behavioral Changes

The outcome-aware judge credited MIRA-AC’s outer-policy decisions with
the larger contribution in 203 pairs and untrained MIRA’s in 131; 66
pairs were tied or unclear. MIRA-AC therefore received 60.8% of the 334
decisive attributions. It was also more often classified as taking a
defensible risk that improved the gold result (210/400 trajectories,
52.5%, versus 139/400, 34.8%).

The remaining rows of
Fig. [9](#S6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Analysis of trained meta-reasoning trajectories reveals notable shifts in research behavior, particularly a greater tendency to target unresolved uncertainty. ‣ 6.4 What Changes After Meta-Reasoning Policy Training? ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Investigate Next: Meta-Reasoning for Long-Horizon Research Agents")
describe what changed in the research process. Relative to untrained
MIRA, MIRA-AC more often changed its explanation of the underlying
mechanism or the form of its solution (185/400 vs. 148/400), ran a test
that distinguished competing ideas (112/400 vs. 67/400), checked whether
an improvement held across conditions or edge cases (28/400 vs. 10/400),
and made an evidence-supported decision about whether to continue
(53/400 vs. 29/400). It less often repeated minor variants that yielded
little new information (75/400 vs. 134/400).

### K.3 Selecting and Attributing Trajectory Examples

For each environment, the examples include at least one case in which
MIRA-AC produced the better gold result and one in which it performed
worse than untrained MIRA. We select cases only after computing the
aggregate statistics across all trajectories, favoring cases with a
clear chain from the available evidence to an outer decision, an
executor result, and the gold outcome. Additional cases are included
only when they clarify a distinct behavior or the boundary between
policy and executor credit. The examples are thus illustrative rather
than estimates of how common each behavior was, and every quotation is
linked to its original decision or result record.

A case in which training hurt performance is included only when it
reveals an interpretable decision tendency consistent with the training
feedback or data distribution, not merely because MIRA-AC lost the pair.
Its discussion reconstructs why the choice was reasonable given the
information available at the time, explains why the proxy evidence
favored a decision that later performed poorly on the gold evaluation,
and states any suggested remedy as a testable hypothesis rather than an
established correction.

A useful idea reported by the executor can still have been elicited by
the outer loop. We distinguish five relationships between the outer
decision and the eventual idea:

1.  1.  Direct outer specification. The outer work order names the useful
        hypothesis, test, or planned response before execution.
2.  2.  Outer-seeded executor discovery. The outer policy isolates the
        relevant uncertainty or asks for a test likely to resolve it. The
        executor then supplies the concrete mechanism or implementation.
3.  3.  State scaffolding. Earlier outer decisions create or preserve a
        useful best candidate or body of evidence, but do not point the
        executor toward the eventual idea.
4.  4.  Executor-independent pivot. The executor introduces a useful
        direction outside the requested experiment and its stated
        contingencies.
5.  5.  Harness-enabled opportunity. Forced continuation or retry makes
        later work possible but contributes no research hypothesis. Any
        later outer decision or executor discovery is credited separately.

These categories describe an idea’s provenance, not whether it was good.
We require a specific link in content between a work order and a later
discovery. An executor idea is not outer-seeded merely because the work
order came first, requested a progress update, or was followed by an
iteration forced by the harness.

### K.4 Symbolic Regression

In symbolic regression, productive research means identifying which
variables and mathematical form explain the data, testing that
explanation where it is most likely to fail, and resisting improvements
that depend on a feature specific to the proxy validation set. Across
the 100 matched tasks, MIRA-AC obtained the higher gold score in 58
cases, compared with 32 for untrained MIRA (10 ties). The LLM trajectory
judge more often found that MIRA-AC ran a test designed to distinguish
two explanations (14% vs. 6%), checked robustness or an edge case (12%
vs. 4%), and made a well-supported decision to stop or continue (25% vs.
11%). It also found fewer premature stops (13% vs. 29%) and fewer
stretches of repeated minor variants that added little information (3%
vs. 13%). These rates count whether a behavior appeared at least once in
a trajectory; the matched cases below illustrate how the same tendency
to act on specific evidence could help or hurt.

#### Testing a specific error reveals a better equation.

In MatSci25 (pair symbolic:047), the task asks for a compact equation
for stress as a function of strain $`\epsilon`$ and temperature $`T`$.
The proxy fit suggested that the coefficient relating stress to strain
varied with temperature, but its worst relative errors remained too
large. MIRA-AC made that error the target of the next experiment:

> Outer policy (B-D002): “Scan alternative parametric forms for the
> temperature-dependent modulus $`E(T)`$ in Stress
> $`\approx\epsilon E(T)`$ to reduce max relative error and potentially
> achieve acc_0_1.”
>
> Executor (B-R002): “epsilon and T are perfectly correlated
> ($`r\approx 1.0`$) in the data, so fitting $`\epsilon^{p}E(T)`$ with
> $`p\neq 1`$ reduces MRE substantially.”

The resulting expression reduced the maximum relative error from 0.140
to 0.052. MIRA-AC scored 0.9949 on the gold evaluation; untrained MIRA
scored 0. This was an outer-seeded executor discovery: the outer policy
selected the relationship to inspect and the error criterion, which
exposed the $`\epsilon`$–$`T`$ correlation, while the executor
introduced the decisive generalization from $`\epsilon`$ to
$`\epsilon^{p}`$. The result therefore supports credit for the policy’s
choice of question, not sole credit for the formula.

#### Optimizing proxy error preserves a model that does not extrapolate.

In the CRK25 reaction-kinetics task (pair symbolic:020), the target
depends on time $`t`$ and concentration $`A`$. Untrained MIRA tested
whether time added information after conditioning on concentration; its
executor reported “Pearson(residual,t)=0.0145, confirming t carries no
independent predictive power once A is conditioned on” (A-R001), then
refined equations using concentration alone until it passed the proxy
relative-error threshold. MIRA-AC instead found a more elaborate
candidate with the best proxy score, but that expression used a boundary
value estimated from the observed data. Its own work order identified
the danger:

> Outer policy (B-D005): “The incumbent F34 expression \[…\] achieves
> acc_0_1=True with max_re=0.052 and NMSE=0.000689, but has 5+ free
> parameters plus a hardcoded A_max from data maximum — raising OOD
> generalization risk.”
>
> Executor (B-R005): “Discovered Family 38 (F38) — combining rational
> correction with A-correction in exponent — achieving a 1133$`\times`$
> NMSE improvement.”

The proxy gain was substantial: validation NMSE fell from
$`6.89\times 10^{-4}`$ to $`6.08\times 10^{-7}`$ while satisfying the
relative-error criterion. Retaining the model was therefore reasonable
given the available metrics, despite the known extrapolation risk. The
gold result reversed the proxy ordering: untrained MIRA scored 0.9998
and MIRA-AC scored 0. The executor fitted and compared the candidate
equations, but the outer policy directly set the criterion for choosing
the final equation and selected the formula after naming its weakness.
Forced continuation preserved later opportunities to work, but supplied
neither the candidate equation nor the selection criterion.

_Interpretation._ Training did not simply make the model change the
mathematical form of its answer more often: the judge attributed such a
change to the outer policy, or jointly to the outer policy and executor,
in 27% of MIRA-AC trajectories and 36% of untrained MIRA trajectories,
but those trajectories produced the higher gold score 66.7% and 47.2% of
the time, respectively. The two cases show when this tendency helps and
when it hurts. A test tied to a systematic pattern in the errors can
reveal a better equation; the same pressure to improve a proxy error can
favor an expression that uses a constant fitted at the edge of the
observed data and may not extrapolate. One testable remedy is to
evaluate expressions containing such constants on held-out examples near
and beyond the range seen during fitting.

Forced continuation appeared in 57 MIRA-AC trajectories and 17 untrained
MIRA trajectories in this environment. The attribution analysis excludes
harness-only contributions, but forced continuation gave the policies
unequal opportunities to make later decisions. This weakens a causal
interpretation of the aggregate difference.

### K.5 Physics Discovery

Physics Discovery gives the agent a limited budget for simulator
experiments and then evaluates its submitted law on hidden trajectories.
Productive research therefore uses an experiment to distinguish physical
explanations or test conditions in which they disagree; repeatedly
fitting the same acquired trajectories need not produce new evidence.
Across 100 matched tasks, MIRA-AC obtained the higher gold score in 65
cases and untrained MIRA in 26 (nine ties). The LLM judge more often
observed MIRA-AC revising its mechanism (51% vs. 22%), running a
discriminating experiment (28% vs. 15%), and testing an edge case (13%
vs. 4%). Repeated minor variations of an unproductive idea fell from 51%
to 23%, and premature stopping from 27% to 6%. Perfect gold scores rose
only from eight to ten; much of the outcome gain was better partial
recovery of the hidden law.

#### Varying one parameter separates two candidate laws.

In pair physics:012, both trajectories inferred a plausible
distance-dependent interaction, but the dependence on the second
particle’s parameter remained unresolved. Untrained MIRA stopped after
five research iterations even though its executor reported that “14
experiment rounds and unlimited MSE evaluations remain for further
improvement” (A-R002). MIRA-AC eventually isolated the ambiguity:

> Outer policy (B-D013): “The cheapest discriminating action is one
> experiment round varying p2 independently.”
>
> Executor (B-R014): “Discovered the correct charge model:
> $`p_{1}/p_{2}`$ (not $`p_{1}/\sqrt{p_{2}}`$ as previously believed).”

MIRA-AC then preserved its best prior candidate when later changes to
the numerical integration failed to improve the fit. It received a gold
score of 1.000, compared with 0.005 for untrained MIRA. The outer policy
selected the controlled parameter variation and, in the following work
order, made the competing coupling laws explicit. The executor ran the
experiment and identified the exact relation. This is not an independent
executor pivot. A forced continuation preserved the opportunity to run
the following iteration, but the preceding policy decision had already
selected the informative parameter variation.

#### After finding the law, further exploration chases a proxy metric.

Pair physics:001 shows the same willingness to act on uncertainty after
the model had largely resolved which physical law applied. MIRA-AC’s
executor first reported, “Discovered the force law: F = -p1\*p2 \* r_hat
/ r²” (A-R002). The outer policy nevertheless redirected the remaining
experiment budget:

> Outer policy (A-D007): “pivot directly to acquiring diverse
> trajectories to improve trajectory_shaping_quality.”
>
> Executor (A-R007): “0.006 gain over 6 rounds.”

Untrained MIRA instead instructed its executor to “probe distance
scaling, sign reversal, and magnitude coupling” (B-D002), validated the
inverse-square law, and scored 1.000 on the gold evaluation. MIRA-AC
exhausted all 15 experiment rounds and scored 0.00085. Acquiring broader
trajectories could reasonably have exposed a hidden failure and did
improve the visible trajectory_shaping_quality score, but the new
trajectories no longer distinguished between competing physical laws.
The costly pivot was directly specified by the outer policy; the
executor implemented it. Forced continuation made later work possible
but supplied no scientific direction.

_Interpretation._ The principal change in Physics Discovery was not
simply that MIRA-AC did more work. It was more willing to run a new
experiment when uncertainty remained. That tendency helped when the
uncertainty distinguished physical laws and hurt when the policy
continued optimizing a proxy metric after competing physical laws were
no longer in question. A policy trained to seek incremental improvements
in proxy score could learn this preference, although the matched
trajectories alone cannot establish that explanation. A direct follow-up
is to require each costly experiment to state which competing physical
laws it is meant to distinguish, or stop collecting data solely to
improve the proxy metric after several rounds with negligible gains.

### K.6 HillClimbBench-CPU: Larger Architectural Changes

HillClimbBench-CPU asks the agent to improve a neural architecture under
a fixed CPU training budget. Productive research must therefore do more
than change hyperparameters: it must identify a computational
bottleneck, propose an architecture that addresses it, and distinguish a
genuine improvement from a proxy-evaluation shortcut. Across 100 matched
pairs, MIRA-AC achieved the higher gold score in 55, compared with 45
for untrained MIRA. Its trajectories were more often judged to change
the model’s computation rather than only its settings (73% vs. 60%), or
to take a productive risk (66% vs. 55%), and less often remained in an
unproductive sequence of minor variants after they stopped improving
(23% vs. 41%). The shift was not uniformly beneficial: continuing
despite contrary evidence was also more common (26% vs. 10%). When the
judge examined the research process without seeing the final scores, it
preferred untrained MIRA in 53 pairs and MIRA-AC in 45 (two ties).
MIRA-AC’s gold-score advantage therefore came from decisions with larger
effects on the final outcomes, not from a uniformly better research
process.

#### An architecture change that matched the task.

In the scaled-Cayley forgetting task, the model had to retain a
Cayley-rotation structure while extrapolating to longer horizons and
larger amplitudes. After the current architecture reached an
extrapolation ceiling, MIRA-AC’s outer policy identified the restrictive
component: “Allowing scale \> 1 enables growth modes while preserving
the Cayley rotation structure” (B-D006). The executor implemented a
clamped exponential scale and reported, “Score: 0.5710 (delta +0.4008 vs
incumbent 0.1703),” with extrapolation rising from 0.072 to 0.615
(B-R006). The final gold score was 0.683, compared with 0.195 for the
matched untrained trajectory, which continued to explore nearby
direct-mapping variants.

The ability to model growth was specified directly by the outer policy.
A later executor report introduced a diagonal preconditioner, which
scales different internal modes by different amounts, but only after the
outer policy had focused the search on scaling those modes; we therefore
treat that detail as an outer-seeded executor discovery rather than an
independent pivot. The executor supplied part of the implementation,
while the outer loop had already identified the model’s inability to
scale different internal modes as the bottleneck.

#### The same willingness to change the architecture can follow the wrong signal.

The Universal Transformer task evaluates a model that repeatedly applies
the same block and learns when to stop while solving cellular automata.
MIRA-AC instead ordered the executor to “Switch to a direct
local-neighbor transition” (B-D001). This was plausible given the
available evidence: cellular-automaton updates are local, and successive
refinements raised the proxy score to 0.985. The gold evaluator
nevertheless assigned the final candidate a score of 0 after marking it
invalid. Untrained MIRA preserved the benchmark’s intended
design—“Single weight-tied attention+MLP block reused across steps”
(A-D001)—and scored 0.809 on the gold evaluation.

Both architectures were specified directly by their respective outer
policies; the contrast is not explained by an independent executor
discovery or a harness-provided hypothesis. The trained policy made a
meaningful architectural bet, but the proxy signal rewarded a solution
tailored to the proxy evaluator that did not survive the gold
evaluation. The zero alone does not show whether the candidate violated
the intended architecture or merely failed because of an implementation
error. Because the actor was trained to seek incremental improvements in
proxy score, selecting a rapidly improving proxy candidate was aligned
with its training signal even though that candidate failed gold
evaluation. One useful safeguard would require architecture-changing
work orders to state which intended architectural constraint they
preserve and to pass basic validity tests across input shapes before
being selected as the best candidate. Whether this retains the gains
from architecture search while filtering proxy shortcuts remains to be
tested.

Together, the two cases clarify the aggregate shift. MIRA-AC more often
pivoted from repeated minor variants to changes in the computation
itself. That behavior created value when the proposed architecture
addressed the true bottleneck, but produced a larger downside when the
proxy evaluator rewarded an easier shortcut.

### K.7 BNLearn: Resolving Remaining Graph Uncertainty

BNLearn asks the agent to recover a causal directed acyclic graph from
observational data and a limited intervention budget. An intervention
fixes one variable to a chosen value and reveals how other variables
respond. Useful exploration is usually conditional: choose an
intervention that separates a few remaining graph hypotheses, or search
a small, explicitly enumerated set of edge edits after localizing the
remaining errors. We summarize those errors using structural Hamming
distance (SHD), the number of missing, extra, or reversed edges. Because
many trajectories reach the score ceiling, different research paths
often have the same final score. The outcome-aware judge credited
MIRA-AC with decisions that contributed more to its own final outcome in
25 of 100 pairs and untrained MIRA in 22, with 53 ties. MIRA-AC
trajectories more often contained a test aimed at resolving uncertainty
about a specific edge (55% vs. 34%) and a productive risk (61% vs. 40%),
but less often preserved the strongest current graph while deciding what
to try next (19% vs. 32%). BNLearn was held out from actor training,
although it was included in critic pretraining; these differences
therefore reflect behavior transferred from other training environments.

#### Targeted interventions fix the remaining graph errors.

On bnlearn:004, MIRA-AC reduced its graph to one missing, one extra, and
one reversed edge. Its outer policy then selected two interventions:
“Intervene on X0” and “Intervene on X16” (A-D003). The executor
reported, “Fixed all 3 SHD errors using 2 strategic interventions (X0,
X16)” (A-R003), identifying the precise reversal, deletion, and addition
implied by the new evidence about which variables were affected. MIRA-AC
achieved a gold score of 1.0; untrained MIRA repeatedly launched related
combinatorial searches after visible wall-time failures and finished at
0.833.

The outer policy receives direct credit for selecting the two
informative interventions and the narrowly defined correction problem.
The executor receives credit for identifying the exact three edge edits;
because those edits followed from the requested interventions, this is
an outer-seeded executor discovery rather than an executor-independent
pivot. The useful behavior was not exploration in the abstract, but
spending scarce interventions on observations that could change a small
set of decisions.

#### Broad search loses to a one-edge repair.

The reverse pattern appears on bnlearn:057. Untrained MIRA used two
interventions to raise its score for recovering which variables affect
one another from 0.935 to 0.982, then issued the narrow work order
“close the SHD=1 gap by identifying and adding the single missing edge”
(B-D009). The executor compared the effects implied by the current graph
with the variables affected by each intervention and reported,
“Identified and added the single missing edge X15-¿X14 to close the
SHD=1 gap” (B-R009). Untrained MIRA therefore reached a gold score of
1.0. MIRA-AC’s reconstruction based on its interventions made
substantial progress, but its early searches incurred wall-time failures
and later graph-learning methods and exhaustive edge-swap searches
concluded that the current SHD=7 graph was “the best achievable”
(A-R011). It finished at 0.844.

Untrained MIRA’s outer policy directly asked the executor to find the
one missing edge; the executor supplied the exact edge, making the
solution an outer-seeded executor discovery. MIRA-AC’s broader search
was reasonable after several methods agreed on the same best graph, but
it failed to turn the available evidence into a sufficiently narrow
correction. This is consistent with a preference learned in other
environments for gathering new evidence, even in an environment where
many near-ceiling solutions require only a small repair to the remaining
graph errors. A late-stage work order could instead test the smallest
set of edge edits consistent with the intervention evidence before
rebuilding the graph or expanding to a broad search.

BNLearn therefore shows both sides of the same tendency. Targeted
experiments can close the last graph errors efficiently, but exploration
that begins before the remaining errors are identified can spend the
remaining budget without improving the graph. The large number of tied
outcomes also means that many differences in behavior do not appear in
final-score comparisons.

### K.8 Cross-Environment Interpretation

Across the four environments, training did not simply increase the
amount of exploration. The trained outer policy more often acted on
unresolved uncertainty: run a test that could separate competing
explanations, change the form of the solution when the current class of
solutions stopped improving, or abandon a line of minor variants that
had stopped yielding new information. In Physics Discovery and
HillClimbBench-CPU this often produced larger changes to the proposed
physical law or neural architecture. In symbolic regression and BNLearn
it more often appeared as a controlled test or a focused attempt to
resolve the remaining errors.

The same shift explains the observed degradations. Acting on uncertainty
was valuable when resolving that uncertainty improved the gold
evaluation, but costly when a proxy metric rewarded a shortcut, a
constant fitted to the edge of the observed data, or additional data
collection after the central question was already resolved. The trained
policy therefore made the model’s research decisions more likely to
change the final result, for better or worse, rather than uniformly more
rigorous. The paired examples suggest three concrete directions for
future evaluation: require costly experiments to name the hypotheses
they separate, test architecture and expression changes on deliberately
shifted or out-of-distribution cases before selecting them as the final
answer, and prefer the smallest set of possible edits once only a few
uncertainties remain.
