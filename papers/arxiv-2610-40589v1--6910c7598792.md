# Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins

El Mustapha Mansouri^(\*)    Eikichi Kimura    Masato Koga    Keigo
Arai^(†) Affiliation: School of Engineering, Institute of Science Tokyo,
Yokohama, Kanagawa 226-8501, Japan

29 October 2026

###### Abstract

An autonomous research laboratory could conduct an entire study, from
formulating questions to preparing manuscripts. Artificial intelligence
(AI) offers one route towards this goal. Recent AI systems take on
several research roles, but may draw conclusions that the evidence does
not support. Here, we present an autonomous research laboratory in which
agents based on large language models assess one another’s work and can
stop or redirect investigations. They can generate and reject research
questions, require separate theoretical checks and conduct experiments
designed to challenge their claims. We demonstrate these capabilities by
investigating ensembles of quantum spins in diamond. The agents derived
the minimum number of calibration settings needed to predict responses
to a defined family of microwave pulse sequences. They checked the
result computationally and designed experiments to distinguish competing
predictions. An initial experiment agreed with the theoretical
predictions, but repeated reference measurements showed that the signal
was drifting during data collection. The agents therefore treated the
result as inconclusive and revised the measurement protocol without
relaxing the acceptance criteria. The revised experiment met those
criteria and supported the theoretical predictions. This study
demonstrates that collaborating AI agents can select research questions,
derive theoretical results and test their predictions experimentally.

To conduct research autonomously, a system must decide what to
investigate, how to test its ideas and what to conclude from the
results. Robot scientists have generated hypotheses and tested them in
the laboratory \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\], while autonomous
laboratories have used experimental feedback to select and carry out
further experiments \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4)\]. Systems based on
large language models (LLMs) can also search the literature, propose
ideas, plan experiments and analyse data \[[5](#bib.bib5),
[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\].

In recent studies, LLMs have challenged hypotheses, checked physical
reasoning and revised interpretations in response to experimental
evidence \[[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10),
[6](#bib.bib6)\]. A demanding test is whether agents can use this
criticism to guide a study in which they develop a theoretical claim and
design experiments to test it. Agents may complete every step of a study
yet still draw a conclusion that the evidence does not support. In one
evaluation, an AI Scientist reported performance improvements that were
based on flawed experiments \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. We therefore ask
whether agents can revise their experiments or conclusions when
criticism reveals a flaw.

Scientific judgement becomes most consequential when it prevents an
apparently promising investigation from proceeding. A plausible research
question may already have an answer, and an elegant mathematical
argument may rest on an unchecked assumption. For a proposed physical
law, even a rigorous mathematical derivation cannot establish its
empirical validity and relevance: its predictions must also withstand
experiments designed to distinguish them from competing explanations.
Organized scepticism requires these uncertainties to be confronted
rather than merely acknowledged \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. Independent
verification, precommitted predictions and acceptance criteria, and
experimental controls provide safeguards against unjustified
conclusions \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\].
The critical test is whether AI agents can enforce these safeguards,
even when doing so means rejecting their own work.

Ensembles of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres in diamond provide a suitable
setting for this test. Their optically readable spins can be manipulated
through precisely controlled microwave-pulse sequences, while variations
in spin properties create a nontrivial problem of predicting ensemble
responses \[[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18)\]. This
combination of experimental control and physical complexity allows
mathematical predictions to be independently examined and tested against
simpler calibration methods. The platform thus provides an opportunity
to evaluate whether AI agents can formulate a meaningful question,
establish a theoretical result and design an experiment capable of
challenging that result rather than merely confirming it.

Here we present a collaborative AI laboratory that uses scientific
criticism to guide an investigation from question selection to
experimental assessment. Our objective is to determine whether
collaborating agents can develop a substantive theoretical result and
establish what experimental evidence supports its predictions. The
agents pursued this objective by identifying the minimum calibration
information needed to predict unmeasured responses of an NV spin
ensemble, without reconstructing the unknown distribution of spin
errors. The transition from theory to experiment required further
collaboration: an experimentalist agent developed the control software
and performed measurements, while a manager agent proposed redesigning
the experiment rather than stopping after an agent serving as principal
investigator rejected an initial design. During the research an
experiment failed, the agents investigated the discrepancy and revised
the acquisition procedure. The final measurements supported the
calibration law developed by the proposer and PI. Together, the
mathematical result and its experimental test demonstrate a scientific
investigation in which AI agents collectively selected, challenged and
substantiated a new physical claim.

## 1 Collaborative AI laboratory for scientific discovery

To investigate whether scientific judgement can emerge through
collaboration among AI agents, we developed a research system that
distributes scientific responsibilities across specialized agents
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Collaborative AI laboratory for scientific discovery ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")).
The system connects research-question generation, theoretical analysis,
computational verification and physical experimentation through
exchanges of research outputs and critical feedback. Rather than
automatically advancing results from one stage to the next, agents
evaluate the work of their collaborators and can reject or request
revisions when scientific criteria are not satisfied. By separating the
generation and evaluation of scientific claims across agents with
distinct contexts, this design aims to reduce self-confirmation bias and
the reinforcement of erroneous assumptions across successive reasoning
steps \[[19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20)\].

The laboratory comprised six principal agent roles: proposers, a
principal investigator (PI), a computational researcher, an
experimentalist, a manager and independent reviewers
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Collaborative AI laboratory for scientific discovery ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")).
The proposers independently generated candidate research questions,
which the PI evaluated for scientific novelty, logical consistency,
significance and experimental feasibility. After selecting a research
question, the PI developed its theoretical foundations, while the
computational researcher independently verified the mathematical results
and examined the robustness of their underlying assumptions on a
supercomputer. The experimentalist developed the instrument-control
software and performed measurements on an NV-diamond platform. The
manager coordinated exchanges between the agents and contributed to
experimental planning, while independent reviewers assessed the
resulting manuscript.

Scientific evaluation was integrated into the communication between
agents rather than restricted to a final review. Research outputs were
exchanged through written documents, and key decisions required explicit
assessments before the investigation could proceed
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Collaborative AI laboratory for scientific discovery ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")).
Rejected proposals could be revised in response to criticism,
mathematical claims were subjected to independent verification, and
experimental designs could be reconsidered when they failed to
distinguish the proposed theory from alternative explanations.
Experimental predictions and acceptance criteria were fixed before the
target measurements were analysed. These mechanisms allowed criticism to
influence not only the interpretation of results, but also the direction
and execution of the research itself.

Human researchers assembled the apparatus and configured the safety
agent with preset operating limits. Within these boundaries, the AI
agents carried out the calibration investigation through their
respective roles, from research-question selection to experimental
assessment.

## 2 Critical evaluation and selection of research questions

We first examined how the laboratory selected a research question from
independently generated proposals
(Fig. [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Critical evaluation and selection of research questions ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")).
Each proposer received a description of the experimental platform and
was tasked with identifying a mathematically substantive research
question in machine learning or information science in which physics was
essential, without access to the other proposals. The
principal-investigator (PI) agent evaluated the proposals in batches,
assessing their scientific novelty, logical validity, significance and
experimental feasibility. Rather than selecting the most promising
candidate by default, the PI could reject an entire batch and provide
written criticism to guide subsequent proposals.

This selectivity proved consequential. Over approximately 30 hours, the
PI rejected 75 proposals, accepting none during the first 17 batches
(Fig. [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Critical evaluation and selection of research questions ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")a,b).
The rejections reflected recurring problems: previously established
results, logical inconsistencies, insufficient scientific significance
and the absence of feasible experimental tests. Moreover, independent
proposers repeatedly converged on similar unsuccessful research
directions. To overcome this limitation, the manager agent independently
identified recurring rejected ideas and excluded them from subsequent
proposal instructions. These ideas had accounted for 52 of the 56
earlier fresh proposals; following their exclusion, nine new proposals
explored seven previously unrepresented research directions. The
feedback therefore changed not only which proposals were rejected, but
also the directions explored by the agents.

The eventual selection emerged through revision rather than acceptance
of an initial proposal. The PI identified a previously rejected idea as
a promising starting point and provided a critique that a new group of
proposers used to reformulate the problem. The revised proposal replaced
an approximation based on leading-order effects with a formulation
retaining the full dependence on pulse sequences. This led the PI to
select and refine a research question concerning the predictive
information contained in measurements of an inhomogeneous spin ensemble:
what is the minimum number of calibration measurements required to
predict the ensemble response to arbitrary phase-controlled pulse
sequences?

## 3 Discovery and verification of an ensemble-calibration law

The selected research problem concerned predicting the collective
response of an inhomogeneous spin ensemble to phase-controlled
microwave-pulse sequences. Individual spins experience different
detunings and microwave-drive strengths, so the measured signal averages
over an unknown distribution of pulse errors. The agents considered
sequences of pulses with fixed duration, amplitude and shape but
independently variable phases, assuming that each spin retained the same
error parameters throughout a sequence. The selected proposal
established a polynomial upper bound of $`8L^{2}+6L+1`$ independent
response functions for sequences of up to $`L`$ pulses, but did not
determine the exact number of calibrations required.

During the subsequent theoretical investigation, the
principal-investigator (PI) agent reformulated the problem and derived
an exact result
(Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Discovery and verification of an ensemble-calibration law ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")a).
It proved that $`L^{2}+1`$ calibration measurements are both necessary
and sufficient to predict the ensemble response to every
phase-controlled sequence of at most $`L`$ pulses, independently of the
distribution of persistent spin errors. The proof established that these
measurements span the space of possible ensemble responses, even with an
unknown common fluorescence background and contrast. Conversely, fewer
calibrations cannot guarantee reconstruction, because distinct
physically admissible ensembles can produce identical calibration
measurements while differing on unmeasured sequences. The PI thus
transformed the proposal’s sufficient upper bound into an exact minimum,
reducing the required count from the proposed bound of 91 to 10 for
$`L=3`$. More generally, the calibration requirement grows quadratically
with sequence length, whereas the number of possible pulse sequences
grows exponentially for a fixed discrete set of phase choices
(Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Discovery and verification of an ensemble-calibration law ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")b).

This result makes it possible to predict an entire family of control
responses without reconstructing the unknown distribution of spin
errors. With sufficient calibration, different microscopic ensembles can
remain compatible with the measurements while giving identical
predictions for every sequence in the specified family. The conceptual
advance is the exact identification of the response information needed
for this task and its equivalence to physically necessary calibration
measurements. The resulting predictor takes the calibrated mean
responses as inputs and returns mean responses for unmeasured
phase-controlled sequences. The matching lower bound establishes a
fundamental limitation: if calibration leaves a response direction
unresolved, a more flexible predictor cannot guarantee its recovery for
every admissible ensemble. The law therefore provides both a
constructive way to select sufficient measurements and a criterion for
when additional calibration is needed. The minimum $`L^{2}+1`$ refers to
calibration settings with linearly independent responses; repeated
acquisitions determine the precision of the measured means.

The mathematical result was then subjected to independent computational
verification. A computational-researcher agent reproduced the symbolic
checks and independently reconstructed the response-space structure,
confirming dimensions of 26 and 37 for sequences of up to five and six
pulses, respectively. The PI further challenged the assumption that
pulse errors remain constant throughout a sequence by specifying a
numerical stress test involving hidden phase rotations between pulses.
Using the TSUBAME 4.0 supercomputer, the computational researcher
generated 252,620 exact certificates bounding calibration ambiguity and
prediction errors under these perturbations. Here, calibration ambiguity
is the largest difference in target means between ensembles that agree
on the short-sequence calibrations and remain statistically
indistinguishable under the full calibration. For the specified
six-pulse test, the certified prediction error remained at the
photon-noise level for hidden phase rotations of approximately
$`1.2^{\circ}`$ or less
(Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Discovery and verification of an ensemble-calibration law ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")c).
These calculations examined the robustness of the proposed law beyond
its ideal assumptions, while its empirical validity remained to be
established through measurements capable of distinguishing its
predictions from simpler calibration methods.

## 4 Collaborative design of a discriminating experiment

The experimental objective was to test the predictive value of the
calibration law using withheld sequence responses. The agents therefore
sought a design in which the law and specified interpolation methods
made different predictions, allowing the measurements to distinguish
between them. The initial design combined a complete grid of nine
three-pulse calibration sequences with five shorter sequences to predict
four withheld targets
(Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Collaborative design of a discriminating experiment ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")a).
However, the nine-point grid already contained sufficient information to
determine those targets through Fourier interpolation. Agreement between
the predicted and measured signals would therefore not distinguish the
proposed law from an ordinary interpolation method. The PI identified
this limitation and rejected the design as an inadequate test of the
theoretical result.

The rejection prompted further collaboration between the agents. The PI
proposed a different approach that combined measurements from pulse
sequences of different lengths, using five three-pulse calibrations
together with five shorter sequences. The shorter sequences supply the
information missing from the five three-pulse settings. The PI refined
the phase settings and verified that these ten calibrations spanned the
response space required by the law, while leaving four three-pulse
targets that could not be determined by the competing interpolation
methods
(Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Collaborative design of a discriminating experiment ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")b).
This cross-length design produced distinct predictions from the
discovered law and simpler interpolation rules
(Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Collaborative design of a discriminating experiment ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")c),
providing an experimental test capable of distinguishing the competing
explanations.

To ensure that the comparison could not be influenced by the observed
targets, the PI established the prediction rules and acceptance criteria
before data acquisition. The experimentalist was required to fix the
numerical predictions obtained from the calibration measurements before
analysing the withheld target data. The protocol also incorporated
independent experimental controls and predefined conditions for
classifying an outcome as supporting, contradicting or inconclusive, the
last including any acquisition that failed a control. Before
measurement, the experimental design was approved by a safety agent.
Consequently, agreement with the theoretical prediction alone would not
be sufficient to establish experimental support for the law.

## 5 Rejecting an apparently successful run and establishing experimental support

The first main acquisition met all ten precommitted accuracy criteria,
which assessed agreement for the four withheld targets and a contrast
formed from those responses (Methods). However, the global-phase and
reference-pulse controls failed
(Fig. [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5 Rejecting an apparently successful run and establishing experimental support ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")b).
These controls tested whether applying the same phase shift to every
pulse left the measured response unchanged. The principal-investigator
(PI) agent therefore classified the run as inconclusive.

The experimentalist traced the failed controls to changes in the signal
during acquisition. The signal rose by 7.6% over the morning and showed
a further 2.9% step at 11:48 a.m., coinciding with a laboratory member
switching on the room lights
(Fig. [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5 Rejecting an apparently successful run and establishing experimental support ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")a).
The experiment alternated between two programs containing different
global-phase replicas. Because these programs sampled the drifting
signal at different times, measurements expected to be equivalent
acquired systematic offsets.

The agents revised the acquisition protocol without relaxing the
acceptance criteria. The experimentalist shortened the alternation
between programs and tested different sequence orders in pilot
acquisitions (short runs used to test adjustments to the acquisition
procedure). The first pilot still failed the phase controls; a second,
with phase switching within a single program, passed. The PI retained
the inconclusive verdict for the original run and specified a new
six-hour acquisition with randomized sequence ordering and additional
diagnostics of the null pair. The manager conveyed the revised protocol
to the experimentalist, who implemented it.

The final acquisition yielded 21,600 valid one-second records and passed
all ten accuracy criteria and experimental controls
(Fig. [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5 Rejecting an apparently successful run and establishing experimental support ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")b).
Predictions from the calibration measurements were fixed before the
withheld targets were analysed. The four withheld responses were
summarized by the target contrast $`S=(-T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}-T_{4})/4`$,
which cancels the common fluorescence background. The measured target
contrast was $`(40.0\pm 0.4)\times 10^{-3}`$ weighted counts per shot,
consistent with the law’s prediction of $`(39.7\pm 1.3)\times 10^{-3}`$,
whereas the interpolation alternatives predicted zero
(Fig. [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5 Rejecting an apparently successful run and establishing experimental support ‣ Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins")c,d).
These measurements support the transfer of information from the shorter
calibration sequences to the four withheld three-pulse responses within
the predefined accuracy tolerances.

## 6 Discussion

Recent debate about AI scientists concerns whether automated research
can produce conceptual insight and reliable scientific
judgement \[[5](#bib.bib5), [21](#bib.bib21)\]. Our results demonstrate
both in a physical investigation. Critical evaluation shaped the
research question, strengthened the mathematical theory and determined
which experimental evidence could support a conclusion. The
principal-investigator agent rejected an experimental design because
conventional interpolation could predict the same targets. It
subsequently retained an inconclusive verdict for an acquisition that
met all ten precommitted accuracy criteria but failed independent
experimental controls. The agents revised the acquisition protocol
without relaxing the acceptance criteria. These decisions demonstrate
that collaborating AI agents can distinguish predictive agreement from
sufficient scientific support and enforce that distinction when
evaluating their own work.

The calibration law provides a concrete example of conceptual progress
during the investigation. The PI reformulated calibration in terms of
the dimension of the ensemble-response space and proved that $`L^{2}+1`$
measurements are both necessary and sufficient for sequences of at most
$`L`$ pulses under persistent pulse errors. Establishing necessity
required identifying physically admissible ensembles that agree on an
insufficient set of calibrations yet differ on unmeasured sequences.
This reasoning transformed a sufficient construction into an exact
characterization of the information required for prediction. The
experimental measurements supported this predictive capability on the
four withheld targets within the predefined tolerances, distinguishing
the law’s predictions from those of the two specified interpolation
methods. Together, the theoretical result and its experimental test show
how an AI laboratory can identify a precise information requirement,
turn it into a measurable prediction and assess the evidence supporting
that prediction.

Solid-state spin defects provide a particularly promising platform for
extending this approach. Nitrogen-vacancy centres combine optical spin
initialization and readout with programmable microwave control, allowing
many experimental protocols to be executed without manual intervention
once the apparatus is operational \[[8](#bib.bib8), [16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18)\]. Their sensitivity to control
imperfections and environmental fluctuations also provides demanding
tests of quantum-control theories. Such systems could enable AI agents
to investigate new pulse sequences, calibration strategies and sensing
protocols through repeated cycles of theoretical prediction and physical
measurement. Similar approaches may be applicable to other quantum
platforms.

Extending the laboratory towards greater autonomy will require
improvements in both the agent architecture and the experimental
infrastructure. Larger groups of parallel proposers could broaden the
search for research questions, while more specialized agents could
extend safety assessment or coordinate work across laboratories. Limited
diversity in generated research ideas has been identified in evaluations
of LLM research agents \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. Here, the manager used the
record of rejected proposals to revise the search strategy, after which
the proposers explored previously unrepresented research directions.
This adaptation demonstrates that an agent-led investigation can use
accumulated criticism to change the scientific questions it pursues.
Connecting multiple experimental setups, including cryogenic platforms,
would further expand the range of testable physical phenomena. Such
extensions would also require more reliable instrument diagnostics,
coordination and safety mechanisms as agents gain additional
experimental degrees of freedom.

A broader challenge is therefore to develop experimental testbeds in
other branches of science that allow AI-generated hypotheses to be
tested with comparable independence and rigour without human
involvement. Greater scientific autonomy will depend not only on
improvements in AI reasoning, but also on instruments that make
theoretical predictions experimentally accessible and competing
explanations distinguishable. As these capabilities develop, the role of
human researchers may shift towards identifying consequential questions,
expanding experimental possibilities and critically assessing the
significance of automated discoveries. The long-term opportunity is not
simply to automate scientific work, but to extend the range of questions
that can be investigated and answered with reliable evidence.

## 7 Methods

### 7.1 Agent implementation and human contributions

We implemented the laboratory using model conversations and coding-agent
sessions configured for different research roles. The principal
investigator (PI) used GPT-6 at the Pro reasoning setting, with Extra
High for reviews. Proposal generation used GPT-5.6 extra high. The
computational researcher ran GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort
through Codex, using CPUs and GPUs on the TSUBAME 4.0 supercomputer. The
experimentalist used Claude Fable 5 through Claude Code on the
instrument computer, and the manager used Claude Opus 5 on a separate
computer with browser and SSH access to the experimentalist and the
computational researcher on the supercomputer.

Mechanisms explored in earlier projects were incorporated into the
exclusion instructions for subsequent proposal searches to prevent new
proposals from overlapping with previously explored research directions.
Each role received instructions specifying its scientific task, expected
output and evaluation criteria.

For the NV investigation, human researchers assembled the apparatus and
supplied a description of its capabilities. During setup, they
configured and checked the safety agent using preset operating limits,
including maximum voltage and power.

### 7.2 Context allocation, document exchange and review

Agents exchanged proposals, manuscripts and research reports as written
documents relayed by the manager. Information was supplied according to
each agent’s task. Fresh proposers received a short description of the
experimental platform and were not shown one another’s proposals. Each
proposal batch was assessed in a new PI conversation. After selection,
the PI continued that conversation through theoretical development,
manuscript preparation and computational test design. A separate
feasibility reviewer received the manuscript and full apparatus
specification without the PI’s preceding exchanges.

An internal manuscript carried the research question, assumptions and
mathematical derivations between agents. The computational researcher
received its source files together with the PI’s test specification, and
returned verification results and a revised manuscript. Its working
directory persisted across tasks. The experimentalist received the
experimental protocol and task instructions, maintained local notes and
logs, and returned laboratory reports. Raw photon counts remained on the
instrument computer; the PI and computational researcher assessed the
measurements from these reports.

Assessments at decision points ended with an explicit verdict and
written reasons. Requests for revision specified numbered changes for
the agent responsible for the task. The decisive computational
experiment underwent one PI review followed by one correction pass by
the computational researcher. Final manuscript reviewers started in new
conversations and received only the manuscript, without the preceding
agent exchanges, source code or raw data. They returned a referee
report, recommendation and confidence score. After receiving the
reviews, the PI judged the experimental evidence sufficient and
instructed the computational researcher to add more information in the
manuscript as requested by the reviewers.

### 7.3 Proposal generation and adaptive selection

Each proposer was instructed to formulate one theoretical contribution
to information science in which physics was essential and that could be
applicable to NV centres. The selected proposal met this brief as a
learning problem: it asked how many calibration measurements are
necessary and sufficient to predict the responses of an exponentially
large family of control sequences, a question of identifiability and
sample complexity whose answer is set by the physics of persistent pulse
errors. Proposals had to address a significant problem, identify a
feasible theoretical approach and include at least one central
prediction specific to the idea and testable on the available NV
platform. Numerical validation and empirical benchmarks were excluded at
this stage. Proposers were instructed to develop candidates before
checking the literature, assess novelty by the underlying mechanism, and
exclude directions adopted from published future-work or open-problem
discussions.

Fresh batches launched ten proposer conversations to avoid overloading
the context of the PI. Each fresh batch was forwarded to the PI once
five proposals had arrived, and the remaining proposers were stopped. A
separate deduplication stage grouped proposals by central mechanism and
retained one representative from each group. The PI required proposers
to return the strongest idea reached, with remaining uncertainties
stated. The PI assessed novelty, logical validity, significance,
theoretical feasibility and experimental testability, and could reject
an entire batch.

Proposal instructions excluded mechanisms explored in earlier projects.
After twelve consecutive assessments without a winner, the manager added
exclusions derived from repeatedly rejected mechanisms in the PI’s
verdicts. Revision batches launched six proposers, each receiving a
rejected proposal verbatim and the PI’s full assessment of its batch.
They were instructed to address decisive objections and reassess
novelty, with permission to change the mechanism if the original could

each established and what remained new.

### 7.4 Spin-response model and calibration construction

The PI and the proposer who originated the research considered pulses
with fixed duration, amplitude and shape and independently programmable
phases. In the two-level model, a pulse of phase $`\phi`$ was
represented by

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       U_{\phi}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}a&\sqrt{q}\,e^{-i\phi}\\
       -\sqrt{q}\,e^{i\phi}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix},\qquad q=1-|a|^{2},
       ```                                                            |     |

where $`a`$ encodes the pulse action on an individual spin and
$`\bar{a}`$ denotes complex conjugation. Each spin retained the same
$`a`$ throughout a sequence. The ensemble was described by an arbitrary
unknown probability distribution $`\nu`$ on an admissible domain
$`K\subseteq\{a:|a|\leq 1\}`$ with nonempty two-dimensional interior.

For a sequence $`w=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\ell})`$ in time order, with
$`\ell\leq L`$, the return probability and mean fluorescence signal were

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p_{w}(a)=\left|\langle 0|U_{\phi_{\ell}}(a)\cdots U_{\phi_{1}}(a)|0\rangle\right|^{2},\qquad\mu(w)=B+\kappa\int_{K}p_{w}(a)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(a).
``` |  |

The empty sequence had $`p_{\varnothing}=1`$. The distribution $`\nu`$,
unknown offset $`B`$ and nonzero contrast $`\kappa`$ were common to all
sequence settings.

The PI and the proposer defined $`\mathcal{F}_{L}`$ as the real span of
the response functions for sequences of at most $`L`$ pulses. Uniform
identification of every target in this class required calibration
responses spanning $`\mathcal{F}_{L}`$, whose dimension was
$`d_{L}=L^{2}+1`$. The general construction used the five phases
$`2\pi k/5`$, $`k=0,\ldots,4`$, to resolve the phase Fourier components
and selected $`d_{L}`$ linearly independent sequence responses as a
basis. For each target, expansion in this basis determined real
coefficients $`h_{j}(w)`$ satisfying

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{w}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{L}}h_{j}(w)p_{b_{j}}(a),\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d_{L}}h_{j}(w)=1.
``` |  |

Target predictions were therefore computed as

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \widehat{\mu}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{L}}h_{j}(w)\widehat{\mu}(b_{j}),
       ```                                                               |     |

where $`\widehat{\mu}(b_{j})`$ denotes the measured mean for calibration
sequence $`b_{j}`$. The count $`d_{L}`$ refers to distinct calibration
settings; repeated acquisitions determine their precision. The specific
laboratory calibration design is described below.

For the theoretical photon-noise analysis, acquisitions were modelled as
independent Poisson counts with means bounded by $`M`$. A
determinant-maximizing acquisition design with $`N\geq d_{L}`$
observations, combined with least-squares estimation, gave worst-case
mean-squared prediction error at most $`Md_{L}/(N-d_{L}+1)`$.

### 7.5 Independent computational verification and robustness analysis

The computational researcher reproduced the symbolic checks and
independently reconstructed response-space coordinates for $`L=5`$ and
$`L=6`$. A complementary stress test, specified by the PI, examined
whether departures from persistent pulse errors could remain
statistically hidden in calibration while changing target predictions.
This test fixed

|     |                                                                   |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       L=6,\qquad K=[3/5,4/5]+i[-1/5,1/5],\qquad B=1,\qquad\kappa=1/100.
       ```                                                                |     |

Five phase settings with rational sine and cosine values were used, with
the first pulse phase fixed. The design comprised 192 calibration
sequences, including every sequence through three pulses, and 3,715
targets. A hypothetical budget of $`8.8\times 10^{10}`$ Poisson
acquisitions, with approximately one photon per acquisition, set the
statistical scale.

Perturbations consisted of at most one hidden rotation about each spin’s
$`z`$ axis, with angles $`+\delta`$ and $`-\delta`$ occurring with equal
probability. Rotations after pulse three, four or five were considered
separately, together with an unperturbed case. Calibration ambiguity was
defined as the largest target-mean separation between ensembles that
agreed on all short-sequence means and were statistically
indistinguishable under the full calibration. The indistinguishability
criterion required an optimal discrimination success probability of at
most 55%.

Candidate ensemble pairs were sought numerically, and bounds were
certified using exact rational arithmetic. The domain $`K`$ was
subdivided into rectangles, on each of which polynomial inequalities
were checked using the minimum and maximum Bernstein coefficients. This
procedure certified the inequalities across the continuous parameter
domain for each specified test case. A Berry–Esseen bound on sums of
photon counts supplied a necessary condition for the
indistinguishability criterion and was used to tighten the ambiguity
bounds.

Separate certificates bounded the calibrated predictor’s squared bias
and variance. Their sum defined the mean-squared prediction error, whose
square root was expressed relative to $`\sigma_{M}`$, the predictor’s
photon-noise standard deviation. Certification covered every target, the
three rotation positions and the unperturbed case at twelve evaluated
rotation strengths. Seven exact interval halvings refined the crossing
of the photon-noise reference. A control retained only the 32
calibration means through three pulses and sought ensembles agreeing on
those means but differing on a six-pulse target.

### 7.6 Experimental design and prediction locking

Experimental proposals underwent separate assessments of apparatus
feasibility and scientific discrimination. The feasibility reviewer
evaluated whether the available apparatus could test a central
prediction, and the PI assessed whether the proposed measurements
distinguished that prediction from specified alternative models.

The final design combined five three-pulse calibration sequences with
five sequences of at most two pulses, including the empty sequence. Four
additional three-pulse settings served as withheld targets. Phases were
selected in multiples of $`60^{\circ}`$, and an exact rank calculation
verified that the ten calibration responses spanned the $`L=3`$ response
space. The PI derived a $`4\times 10`$ transfer matrix $`H`$, with each
row summing to one, to obtain target predictions as
$`\widehat{\mathbf{T}}=H\mathbf{C}`$. Denoting calibration and target
means in weighted counts per shot by $`C_{0},\ldots,C_{9}`$ and
$`T_{1},\ldots,T_{4}`$, respectively, the test compared the measured
contrast

|     |                                      |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       S=\frac{-T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}-T_{4}}{4}
       ```                                   |     |

with the calibration prediction

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       P=\frac{3}{4}(C_{0}-C_{1}+C_{6})-\frac{1}{4}(C_{2}+C_{3}+C_{4}).
       ```                                                               |     |

Both combinations cancel the common fluorescence background.

Two interpolation baselines were specified. A nine-term Fourier
interpolant fitted the five three-pulse calibration means by minimizing
the squared coefficient norm. A six-term alternative incorporated the
symmetry of the repeated pulse. Both predicted $`S=0`$ for any
calibration record. The protocol additionally required a nine-parameter
model of coherent ensemble evolution to be fitted to all ten calibration
means.

A digital twin created by the computational researcher evaluated the
decision rule using 20,000 simulated runs per case, including intended
operation, a target phase misprogrammed by $`60^{\circ}`$, and cases at
the accuracy-tolerance boundaries. The laboratory packet contained the
protocol, data format and analysis code, and underwent a separate review
before execution. Its feasibility assessment used an approximately
12-hour budget on the HPC, including calibration, without relaxing the
prescribed thresholds.

Before acquisition, the PI fixed $`H`$, the definition of $`P`$, the
accuracy tolerances, controls and outcome criteria. Initial analysis was
restricted to the ten calibration means and their covariance. The
protocol required the numerical law predictions, $`P`$, both
interpolation predictions and the physical fit to be locked and hashed
before target analysis. For the final acquisition, the documented
prediction lock occurred after data collection and before any target
mean was computed. The statistical outcome rules are specified below.

### 7.7 NV apparatus and fluorescence readout

Measurements were performed at room temperature using a confocal
microscope and a single-crystal diamond (Element Six DNV-B14) with
dimensions of $`3.0\times 3.0\times 0.5`$ mm, $`\{100\}`$ faces,
approximately 4.5 ppm NV centres and natural-abundance
$`{}^{13}\mathrm{C}`$ (1.1%). Continuous-wave 532-nm excitation was
gated by an acousto-optic modulator and focused through a $`100\times`$
objective with numerical aperture 0.90. A three-axis piezoelectric stage
positioned the sample. Collected fluorescence passed through a 30-µm
pinhole and a filter centred at 650 nm with a 10-nm bandwidth before
detection by a single-photon counter. The detected rate was typically
$`2.8`$–$`3.1\times 10^{6}`$ counts s⁻¹, with an imposed operating limit
of $`3.4\times 10^{6}`$ counts s⁻¹.

The permanent-magnet field was determined by fitting the resonance
spectrum of all four NV orientations, giving a field magnitude of 4.18
mT directed $`3.8^{\circ}`$ from one NV axis. Microwave excitation
addressed the $`m_{s}=0\rightarrow m_{s}=-1`$ transition of this
orientation near 2.7503 GHz. The carrier was tuned to the central line
of the $`{}^{14}\mathrm{N}`$ hyperfine triplet, whose adjacent lines
were separated by 2.16 MHz.

An FPGA controller synchronized optical and microwave switching and
recorded photon arrivals. Programmed in-phase and quadrature waveforms
controlled microwave phase and frequency offset through a mixer. A fast
switch blocked microwave transmission between pulses, and an amplifier
drove a gold-wire antenna beside the diamond. Waveform offsets were
adjusted to suppress carrier leakage, and gain and phase imbalance were
corrected using a $`2\times 2`$ matrix, with parameters adjusted
individually against spectrum-analyser readings. Qualification
measurements compared Rabi oscillations at phases of $`0^{\circ}`$,
$`60^{\circ}`$ and $`120^{\circ}`$, and checked residual drive using a
zero-amplitude waveform with the microwave switch open. Each new
controller program was simulated before execution on the instrument.
Microwave pulses in the final calibration and target sequences lasted 64
ns, with 152-ns intervals between pulses.

Fluorescence was recorded during a 50-µs laser pulse following microwave
manipulation. Counts from three successive readout windows were combined
with fixed weights of 1, 0.45 and 0.10, respectively, to obtain the
weighted photon count per shot. Sequence means were expressed in these
units. The same laser pulse initialized the spin ensemble for the next
shot. A 1-µs dark interval completed an approximately 52-µs shot cycle,
and all sequence settings used the same total shot duration.

### 7.8 Acquisition protocol and amendments

A preliminary measurement of the predicted identity used 20-ns
inter-pulse gaps and 16 five-minute sub-runs. The first main acquisition
assigned equal exposure to each sequence and comprised 36 alternating
ten-minute sub-runs. Two controller programs contained complementary
sets of global-phase replicas: program A applied common phase shifts of
$`0^{\circ}`$, $`120^{\circ}`$ and $`240^{\circ}`$, and program B
applied $`60^{\circ}`$, $`180^{\circ}`$ and $`300^{\circ}`$, to all
pulses in each nonempty sequence. Following discussion between the
manager and PI, this schedule replaced the initially proposed randomized
66-s cycles.

Failures of the global-phase and reference-pulse controls, associated
with signal changes sampled at different times by the two programs,
prompted amendments to the acquisition. The first run retained its
inconclusive classification. A first pilot shortened the alternation to
30-s blocks and used three sequence orders. A second pilot implemented
phase alternation within a single controller program, adding
$`60^{\circ}`$ to every pulse phase on alternate sweeps through the
sequences. The pilots were classified as acquisition development, and
the original decision criteria were retained.

The amended protocol adopted the within-program phase toggle and
specified 720 blocks of 30 s, giving six hours of integration. Three
prespecified sequence orders were arranged in 120 randomized six-block
groups. Before the final acquisition, the protocol required a 20-minute
diagnostic comparing the null pair: the empty sequence and a shot with
microwaves disabled. These two settings exchanged positions during the
diagnostic to assess their sensitivity to acquisition order. The
protocol also required at least 20 min of warm-up and 240 s of operation
before recording, while maintaining the laser duty cycle. A signal-level
step was to be flagged if it exceeded both 1% and six standard errors.

A 32-bit integer overflow in the reference-diagnostic calculation
produced an undefined standard error. The experimentalist corrected the
calculation using floating-point arithmetic and reanalysed the same
diagnostic data against the unchanged cutoff. The final acquisition
accumulated 21,600 valid one-second records across eight segments,
completing the prescribed six hours of integration.

### 7.9 Statistical analysis and outcome classification

For each withheld target, the prediction residual was defined as
$`r_{i}=T_{i}-(H\mathbf{C})_{i}`$, where $`\mathbf{C}`$ contains the ten
calibration means and $`H`$ is the fixed transfer matrix. The primary
accuracy test used ten margins,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S-0.8P,\qquad 1.2P-S,\qquad 0.3P+r_{i},\qquad 0.3P-r_{i}\quad(i=1,\ldots,4).
``` |  |

Positive margins corresponded to agreement within 20% of $`P`$ for the
measured contrast and residual magnitudes below 30% of $`P`$ for each
target.

Standard errors were propagated using the complete coefficients of the
underlying weighted-count means. This propagation included uncertainty
in the calibration-derived predictions and the covariance between $`P`$
and each residual arising from their shared calibration data.
Poisson-based variance contributions were multiplied by an
excess-variance factor $`F^{*}`$, with a floor of 1.1. Values of
$`F^{*}>1.25`$ failed the variance-validity requirement.

Control measurements assessed global-phase invariance and consistency of
the null signal. A joint $`\chi^{2}`$ statistic compared the six
global-phase replica means for each of the 13 nonempty sequences, giving
65 degrees of freedom. A separate phase-invariance check used six
replicas of a single 128 ns reference pulse. The null control tested
equality between the mean signal of the empty sequence and that of a
shot with microwave emission disabled. Each control used a rejection
threshold corresponding to a tail probability of $`10^{-4}`$; the null
comparison was two-sided.

Acquisitions were classified using the fixed decision rule. Support
required all control and variance-validity criteria to pass, positive
individual one-sided 99% lower confidence bounds for all ten margins,
and a positive measured contrast $`S`$ at one-sided $`p\leq 0.001`$.
Contradiction required the same validity criteria, a predicted contrast
$`P`$ more than 3.09 standard errors above zero, and at least one margin
with a negative one-sided upper confidence bound at tail probability
$`10^{-4}`$. All remaining cases were classified as inconclusive,
including any acquisition that failed a control or the variance-validity
requirement.

The primary classification therefore assessed agreement within the
predefined tolerances. Later diagnostic analyses examined joint
consistency of the residuals with zero and the dependence of $`S`$ and
$`P`$ on acquisition order. These diagnostics were separate from the
fixed outcome-classification rule.
