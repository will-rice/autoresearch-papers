###### Abstract

Machine learning engineering (MLE) tasks require long-horizon decision
making over iterative solution debugging and refinement, under expensive
and feedback-driven environment interactions. Developing and training a
monolithic agent for such tasks is fundamentally challenging, as it must
simultaneously manage extremely long and noisy contexts, explore vast
solution spaces, and remain effective under limited model capacity and
computational budgets. To address these challenges, we propose
Matryoshka Agent, a unified hierarchical agent framework for complex
long-horizon tasks. Matryoshka Agent decomposes agentic problem solving
into a coordinated hierarchy of decision making and execution: a
high-level Orchestrator maintains compact, long-horizon exploration
states and issues strategic instructions, while lower-level Sub-Agents
execute concrete solution attempts through direct environment
interaction, mediated by standardized Tool interface. This design
decouples strategic exploration from costly execution, substantially
reducing the burden of long-context reasoning and enabling efficient
iterative refinement. We further develop an efficient training paradigm
for Matryoshka Agent. Experimental results on a broad range of MLE tasks
with diverse model types and scales demonstrate that Matryoshka Agent is
an effective and scalable paradigm for long-horizon MLE tasks and
complex agentic problem solving. Notably, Matryoshka Agent enables
Qwen3-4B-Instruct to reach Orchestrator performance comparable to
o4-mini. Applying Matryoshka Agent to Qwen3-30B-Coder results in at most
36.7% relative performance gain.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have developed from static
question-answering (QA) chatbots to autonomous agents that are capable
of solving complex, multi-step challenges through interaction with
real-world environments \[[6](#bib.bib6), [24](#bib.bib24),
[40](#bib.bib40), [63](#bib.bib63), [30](#bib.bib30), [52](#bib.bib52),
[58](#bib.bib58), [54](#bib.bib54), [37](#bib.bib37)\]. Within this
expanding landscape, a new class of performance-optimization agentic
tasks has emerged, where the goal is not merely to complete a single
executable objective, but to progressively improve solution quality
through feedback from the environment \[[36](#bib.bib36),
[39](#bib.bib39), [42](#bib.bib42)\]. These tasks are typically
characterized by several fundamental challenges. First, long-horizon
interaction naturally produces growing contexts, with execution logs,
intermediate outputs, and debugging feedback accumulated across
rounds \[[12](#bib.bib12), [52](#bib.bib52)\]. Second, these tasks
involve large, open-ended action and observation spaces, where diverse
observations can induce a broad range of subsequent refinement
choices \[[38](#bib.bib38), [30](#bib.bib30), [36](#bib.bib36),
[39](#bib.bib39)\]. Third, environment interactions often incur
substantial wall-clock and computational cost, making both
inference-time exploration and training-time data collection
expensive \[[44](#bib.bib44), [20](#bib.bib20), [42](#bib.bib42)\].
Machine Learning Engineering (MLE) tasks, characterized by their
dependency on task-specific datasets with diverse modalities (_e.g._,
images, text), schemas, data distributions, and evaluation objectives,
constitute a particularly challenging and representative instantiation
of these difficulties \[[32](#bib.bib32), [25](#bib.bib25),
[6](#bib.bib6), [41](#bib.bib41), [20](#bib.bib20)\]. Consequently,
effective MLE agents must combine strong coding and debugging skills
with deep domain understanding and the ability to systematically explore
and exploit diverse methodological alternatives \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[27](#bib.bib27), [57](#bib.bib57), [17](#bib.bib17), [32](#bib.bib32),
[48](#bib.bib48)\], which inevitably requires accumulating extensive
history contexts that include redundant information. Furthermore, the
practical execution of MLE code is often computationally intensive and
time-consuming, especially for large-scale datasets. The need to handle
long contexts under noisy and partially redundant histories, the
substantial time and computational costs of environment interaction, and
the requirement for sustained exploration and iterative refinement
jointly pose significant challenges for agents to effectively solve this
important yet highly complex class of tasks.

In response to these challenges, we propose Matryoshka Agent, a
hierarchical framework for long-horizon MLE tasks. Matryoshka Agent
separates iterative performance optimization into three coordinated
layers. Orchestrator serves as the high-level decision layer: it
maintains a compact cross-round state over previous instructions,
summarized outcomes, and evaluation scores, and decides which solution
direction to refine next. Sub-Agents serve as the execution layer: they
are instantiated with fresh contexts to implement, run, and debug
concrete solution attempts through direct environment interaction. Tools
mediate between these layers by formatting Orchestrator instructions,
injecting selected references from previous attempts, launching
Sub-Agents, and returning structured summaries and scores. This design
creates an efficient separation between strategic evolution and concrete
execution, reducing long-context burden while preserving key information
for iterative refinement. Beyond the inference-time framework,
Matryoshka Agent further develop a training paradigm tailored to
expensive performance-optimization tasks. During training, Solution
Refinement Tree–based sampling reuses shared execution prefixes and
collects structured comparisons among alternative refinements from
common states. These comparisons provide trajectory-level preference
signals for optimizing the Orchestrator with online ranking-based
reinforcement learning, while successful execution trajectories being
used to improve Sub-Agents.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  We propose Matryoshka Agent, a hierarchical MLE agent framework that
  decouples high-level orchestration from low-level code execution
  through structured tool- mediated feedback.
- •
  We introduce a training paradigm based on Solution Refinement
  Tree–based sampling and trajectory-level ranking optimization,
  enabling efficient Orchestrator learning from costly environment
  interactions while supporting Sub-Agent improvement from successful
  executions.
- •
  We evaluate and analyze Matryoshka Agent extensively across a wide
  range of MLE tasks with diverse model types, scales, and combinations.
  Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of both Matryoshka
  Agent and the training paradigm.

## 2 Related Works

#### Modularized LLM Agents

Recent advances in LLM-based agents increasingly emphasize modular
architectures, where specialized components coordinate to handle diverse
decision-making contexts \[[51](#bib.bib51), [18](#bib.bib18),
[55](#bib.bib55), [8](#bib.bib8), [61](#bib.bib61)\]. Interactions among
such modules have shown strong potential for improving reasoning,
exploration, and task execution \[[28](#bib.bib28), [45](#bib.bib45),
[9](#bib.bib9), [4](#bib.bib4), [60](#bib.bib60)\]. Early modular
frameworks primarily rely on advisory or controller-based guidance by
introducing trainable modules that steer frozen foundation models
through intermediate signals \[[1](#bib.bib1), [43](#bib.bib43),
[53](#bib.bib53)\]. Moving beyond this paradigm,
Matryoshka-Pilot \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] adopts a controller–generator
framework, where a lightweight trainable controller explicitly controls
a black-box LLM via structured guidance. Subsequent work explores
explicit decomposition into specialized planning, execution,
verification, and generation modules, while maintaining a subset of
trainable components to improve efficiency \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[60](#bib.bib60), [49](#bib.bib49)\]. More recent approaches adopt a
subagent-as-tool paradigm, enabling submodules to co-evolve through
self-generated tasks and rewards via dual-role and multi-role self-play
interactions \[[22](#bib.bib22), [19](#bib.bib19), [10](#bib.bib10)\].
Despite their promise, modular agent systems face substantial challenges
in system complexity, training cost, and stability \[[5](#bib.bib5),
[62](#bib.bib62), [55](#bib.bib55)\]. Extending the research to
long-horizon agentic workflows, we propose Matryoshka Agent, a modular
LLM agent framework that systematically orchestrates end-to-end solution
pipelines and achieves strong performance on complex MLE tasks.

#### Coding Agents for MLE

Leveraging rapid advances in LLM coding and reasoning
capabilities \[[21](#bib.bib21), [24](#bib.bib24)\], recent work has
explored LLM-based coding agents for MLE tasks. These efforts span
agentic solutions for ML tasks \[[6](#bib.bib6), [40](#bib.bib40),
[41](#bib.bib41), [46](#bib.bib46), [20](#bib.bib20)\], data science
problems \[[25](#bib.bib25), [3](#bib.bib3), [16](#bib.bib16)\], and
broader ML workflows such as data preprocessing, model selection, and
hyperparameter tuning \[[29](#bib.bib29), [59](#bib.bib59),
[31](#bib.bib31)\]. Prior work in this area can be broadly categorized
into agent scaffolding and specialized training approaches. Early
scaffolding-based methods structure in-context reasoning and tool use
via predefined agent frameworks, but often exhibit unstable prompting
gains \[[2](#bib.bib2), [50](#bib.bib50), [23](#bib.bib23),
[14](#bib.bib14)\] and susceptibility to local
optima \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. More recent research has shifted toward
LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS), which decompose MLE workflows
across interacting agents, tools, and memory to improve robustness and
performance \[[27](#bib.bib27), [20](#bib.bib20), [15](#bib.bib15)\].
Complementary approaches further incorporate explicit exploration
mechanisms \[[35](#bib.bib35), [57](#bib.bib57), [13](#bib.bib13)\] or
learning-based optimization, particularly reinforcement learning, to
directly improve agent performance on ML engineering
tasks \[[56](#bib.bib56)\]. Distinct from prior approaches, Matryoshka
Agent designs an orchestrator that dynamically selects refinement
targets and operations rather than coordinating fixed agent roles.
Optimized through an online reinforcement learning paradigm, this design
enables principled exploration and learning over long-horizon MLE
workflows.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Problem Setup: Iterative MLE with Executable Feedback

We consider iterative machine learning engineering (MLE) tasks where
progress is made through iterative code generation, execution,
debugging, and evaluation. A task instance $`x`$ provides a
natural-language specification, dataset files, a target metric, and a
required submission format. At each round, an executable solution
attempt is produced typically as code that generates a submission file
and evaluated by an execution environment $`\mathcal{E}`$. The
environment executes the code, validates the submission format,
evaluates the resulting prediction under the task-specific metric, and
returns a scalar score. If execution fails, $`\mathcal{E}`$ instead
returns structured error feedback that can be used for debugging. The
goal is to maximize the best score achieved within a finite budget of
solution attempts, i.e., $`\max\mathbb{E}[\max_{1\leq t\leq T}r_{t}]`$,
where $`r_{t}`$ denotes the score obtained by the $`t`$-th solution
attempt.

### 3.2 Matryoshka Agent Framework

Matryoshka Agent is a hierarchical framework that separates long-horizon
decision-making from low-level execution. It consists of three
interacting components: an _Orchestrator_, a set of on-demand
_Sub-Agents_, and a standardized _Tool_ interface connected to the
execution environment. The Orchestrator is responsible for deciding what
solution direction should be explored or refined next. Sub-Agents are
responsible for implementing and debugging concrete solution attempts.
Tools mediate the interaction between them by constructing Sub-Agent
contexts, executing code, collecting environment feedback, and returning
compact summaries and scores.

At initialization, the Orchestrator constructs a root state from the
task instance. This root state captures task-level constraints and
initial understandings and serves as the starting point for subsequent
refinement. After initialization, each turn in Matryoshka Agent
corresponds to one high-level solution refinement attempt. At each turn,
the Orchestrator issues a tool call specifying three pieces of
information: a previous attempt to refine (if present), detailed new
implementation instruction, and optionally a set of previous attempts to
use as references. The Tool interface converts this call into a fresh
Sub-Agent context, combining the task specification, the selected
previous attempt, the Orchestrator instruction, and the tagged
references. The Sub-Agent then implements the requested solution
direction through code generation, execution, and debugging within the
environment $`\mathcal{E}`$. After the Sub-Agent terminates, the Tool
interface returns a compact result to the Orchestrator, including the
evaluation score when a valid submission is produced, together with a
structured summary. If execution still fails after the debugging budget
is exhausted, the returned result records the failure status and
diagnostic summary instead of a valid score. This result is appended to
the Orchestrator history as a standard tool response and becomes
available for future refinement decisions. The process continues until
the time budget or maximum number of turns is reached, after which
Matryoshka Agent returns the best-scoring solution attempt observed
during the interaction.

### 3.3 Orchestrator as Decision Layer

The Orchestrator is the persistent decision-making component of
Matryoshka Agent. Across turns, it maintains a standard agent context
consisting of a sequential record of tool calls and tool responses. This
history gives the Orchestrator a score-annotated view of the solution
process, including which attempts were made, how they performed, and
what high-level diagnostics were observed. Unlike a monolithic agent,
the Orchestrator does not directly accumulate raw code, execution logs,
debugging traces, or full Sub-Agent conversations. It therefore reasons
over long-horizon progress at the level of strategic decisions and
outcomes. Each turn, the Orchestrator controls three aspects of the next
refinement. First, it selects previous attempts to be refined, allowing
it to exploit strong solutions or revisit promising but underdeveloped
directions. Second, it writes a concrete implementation instruction for
the next Sub-Agent, specifying modeling choices, preprocessing
strategies, hyperparameters, validation protocols, or ensembling plans.
Third, it may tag previous attempts as references, allowing the next
Sub-Agent to reuse useful information from selected prior solutions
without exposing the entire execution history. Thus, at inference time,
the Orchestrator dynamically decides what to refine, how to refine it
and receives results of the refinement.

### 3.4 Tools as Mediation Layer

Tools define the communication interface between the Orchestrator and
Sub-Agents. They translate high-level refinement decisions from the
Orchestrator into executable Sub-Agent contexts, and translate Sub-Agent
execution outcomes back into compact tool responses. This mediation
layer is central to Matryoshka Agent: it determines what information is
exposed to Sub-Agents for execution and what information is returned to
the Orchestrator for future decisions.

Tool Inputs. A tool call specifies the refinement attempt to execute.
Concretely, it contains three types of information: the parent attempt
to refine, the implementation instruction generated by the Orchestrator,
and optional reference tags pointing to previous attempts. The parent
attempt provides the immediate refinement target. The instruction
specifies what the Sub-Agent should implement. The reference tags allow
the Orchestrator to selectively expose useful prior attempts concisely.

Tool Execution. Given a tool call, the Tool interface constructs a fresh
Sub-Agent context from the provided inputs and manages the full
execution lifecycle of the corresponding Sub-Agent. It launches the
Sub-Agent on the allocated compute resources, mediates its interaction
with the environment $`\mathcal{E}`$, and monitors the process until the
Sub-Agent terminates and summarizes the execution. Multiple Sub-Agents
can be launched by multiple, parallel tool calls when parallel
exploration is desired, while their contexts and execution histories
remain isolated from one another.

Tool Outputs. After execution, the Tool interface converts the Sub-Agent
trajectory into a compact response for the Orchestrator. The response
contains the evaluation score if submission is valid and the structured
summary. Thus, Tools enforce the information boundary that allows the
Orchestrator to reason over long-horizon progress without accumulating
noisy low-level execution history.

### 3.5 Sub-Agents as Execution Layer

Sub-Agents constitute the execution layer of Matryoshka Agent. While the
Orchestrator decides what direction to pursue, each Sub-Agent is
responsible for turning one high-level instruction into an executable
solution attempt. A Sub-Agent is instantiated with a fresh context
prepared by the Tool interface, including the task information needed
for execution, the Orchestrator instruction, and any selected
references. It does not inherit the Orchestrator’s history or other
Sub-Agents’ contexts.

Within its local context, the Sub-Agent performs a bounded
execution-debugging loop. It first writes code intended to produce a
valid submission file. The environment $`\mathcal{E}`$ executes the code
and checks whether the submission is valid. If execution fails or the
submission is invalid, the environment returns structured feedback, such
as error messages or validation failures, which the Sub-Agent uses to
revise the code. This process continues until a valid submission is
produced or the debugging budget is exhausted. When a valid submission
is produced, $`\mathcal{E}`$ evaluates it under the task-specific metric
and returns a scalar score. If the Sub-Agent fails to produce a valid
submission within the budget, the attempt is marked as failed and no
valid score is assigned. In both cases, the Sub-Agent summarizes its
execution outcome. The Tool interface returns this summary together with
the score when available to the Orchestrator. This design keeps
low-level implementation details local to the Sub-Agent while exposing
only decision-relevant outcomes to the long-horizon decision layer.

### 3.6 Iterative Online Optimization

We propose an iterative online optimization framework for Matryoshka
Agent that tightly couples _tree-based trajectory sampling_ and _online
reinforcement learning_. The framework alternates between structured
trajectory collection and policy optimization, where tree-based sampling
enables efficient reuse of costly execution feedback and naturally
induces contrastive supervision signals for long-horizon
decision-making. Through iterative data collection, ranking-based
labeling, and online contrastive updates, Matryoshka Agent jointly
improves high-level orchestration and low-level execution, enabling
efficient training for complex MLE tasks in expensive execution
environments.

Training-Time Solution Refinement Tree. For each task instance, we first
construct an initial root state from the task specification. Starting
from this root, the current Orchestrator policy samples alternative
refinement instructions. Each sampled instruction is executed by a
Sub-Agent through the Tool interface, producing a new solution attempt
with a compact summary and score. The resulting attempt becomes a child
node in the refinement tree. Repeating this process for multiple levels
yields a tree whose nodes are score-annotated solution attempts and
whose edges are Orchestrator refinement decisions. Specifically, we use
binary expansion for tractable data collection. At each non-leaf node,
the Orchestrator samples two alternative refinement instructions from
the same parent state. Both instructions are executed independently,
producing two child attempts.

Preference Construction. The refinement tree is labeled using
branch-level downstream returns rather than immediate node scores. For
an internal node $`u`$, let $`c_{1}`$ and $`c_{2}`$ denote the two child
branches generated from the same parent state. Each child branch is
assigned a downstream return,
$`R(c)=\max_{v\in\mathrm{subtree}(c)}r(v)`$, where $`r(v)`$ is the score
obtained by the solution attempt at node $`v`$. The child branch with
the higher downstream return is treated as the preferred continuation,
while the other branch is treated as the less preferred continuation.

This construction preserves the long-horizon best-score objective while
producing local comparisons between alternatives that share the same
prefix. Unlike immediate-score comparisons, branch-level returns do not
penalize a refinement direction merely because its first attempt has a
lower score but later improves substantially. Unlike a global
winner-take-all labeling scheme, the tree provides multiple
common-prefix preference pairs rather than treating all non-winning
trajectories as equally negative. As a result, the preference signal
focuses on the divergent refinement decision at each branching state
while retaining downstream performance as the supervision signal.

Online Reinforcement Training. At online iteration $`m`$, the refinement
trees are collected using the current Orchestrator policy $`\pi^{(m)}`$.
The preference pairs extracted from these trees are then used to train
the next Orchestrator policy. For each branching state, let $`s_{u}`$
denote the Orchestrator context at node $`u`$, and let
$`\Delta\tau_{u}^{+}`$ denote the preferred continuation selected by the
downstream-return labeling above. Let $`\mathcal{C}(u)`$ denote the set
of candidate continuations compared at node $`u`$. For a
trajectory-level score $`r(s_{u},\Delta\tau)`$, the corresponding
optimal policy has the energy-based form

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\pi^{\star}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})=\frac{1}{Z(s_{u})}\pi^{(m)}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(s_{u},\Delta\tau)\right),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`Z(s_{u})`$ is the normalizer and $`\beta`$ controls the strength
of regularization toward the previous policy. We optimize this score
with a ranking-based NCE objective that assigns higher probability to
the preferred continuation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\small\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orch}}^{(m+1)}=-\mathbb{E}_{u}\log\frac{\exp\left(r_{\theta^{(m+1)}}(s_{u},\Delta\tau_{u}^{+})\right)}{\sum_{\Delta\tau\in\mathcal{C}(u)}\exp\left(r_{\theta^{(m+1)}}(s_{u},\Delta\tau)\right)}.
``` |  | (2) |

This energy-based
form ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.6 Iterative Online Optimization ‣ 3 Method ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering"))
implies that, up to a state-dependent normalizing constant, the
trajectory score can be represented by the $`\log`$-ratio between the
updated policy and the reference policy. Since the constant is shared by
all candidates in $`\mathcal{C}(u)`$ and cancels in the ranking
objective, we parameterize the score as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\theta}(s_{u},\Delta\tau)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})}{\pi^{(m)}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})}.
``` |  | (3) |

Substituting this score into the ranking objective yields the final
Orchestrator loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\small\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orch}}^{(m+1)}=-\mathbb{E}_{u}\bigg[\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{(m+1)}}(\Delta\tau_{u}^{+}\mid s_{u})}{\pi^{(m)}(\Delta\tau_{u}^{+}\mid s_{u})}-\log\bigg({\sum_{\Delta\tau\in\mathcal{C}(u)}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta^{(m+1)}}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})}{\pi^{(m)}(\Delta\tau\mid s_{u})}\right)^{\beta}\bigg)\bigg]}.
``` |  | (4) |

Here, $`\pi^{(m)}`$ serves as the reference policy for iteration
$`m+1`$. This objective optimizes Orchestrator decisions using
downstream execution outcomes, while the $`KL`$-like $`\log`$-ratio
parameterization keeps each online update anchored to the previous
policy.

Self-reinforcement for Sub-Agents. In addition to optimizing the
Orchestrator, we further improve the capability of Sub-Agents through
supervised fine-tuning. Specifically, for each refinement tree, we
extract Sub-Agent trajectories associated with the highest-scoring
refinement paths, and construct instruction-response pairs from the
Sub-Agents’ own successful trajectories. These data are used to
fine-tune the Sub-Agents, thereby improving their coding,
instruction-following ability, and robustness to execution feedback.
This self-reinforcement mechanism enables Sub-Agents to improve
alongside the Orchestrator, leading to more faithful execution of
high-level decisions.

Datasets and Tasks. We adopt the comprehensive
MLE-Dojo \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] dataset and benchmark, as it covers MLE
tasks for both training and evaluation. Specifically, we retain the
150:50 training-evaluation split used by MLE-Dojo. MLE-Dojo supports
interactive feedback, enabling agents to obtain exact feedback after
each interaction. This facilitates refinement of strategies and
performance improvement in complex, long-horizon processes. Both
training and evaluation tasks cover a wide range of modalities (*e.g.*,
tabular, computer vision, or natural language processing), domains
(*e.g.*, finance, medicine, or media), and objectives (*e.g.*,
classification, regression, or generation). We also follow the split of
evaluation tasks into MLE-Lite, Tabular, CV, and NLP. We adopt HumanRank
as the evaluation metric, which measures the relative performance by the
fraction of human competitors it outperforms on the leaderboard.
HumanRank is normalized to $`[0,1]`$, strictly aligned with the original
task-specific metrics, and averaged across public and private
leaderboards to ensure fair and comparable evaluation across diverse MLE
tasks. See
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Benchmark and Metric Details ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering")
for details about the dataset.

LLMs as Orchestrator and Sub-Agents. We consider a diverse set of
cutting-edge large language models (LLMs) within Matryoshka Agent,
spanning a wide range of model scales and encompassing both open-source
and closed-source models. Specifically, we use
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 \[[47](#bib.bib47)\] and
Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct \[[47](#bib.bib47)\] as open-source models
at different scales, and o4-mini \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] and
GPT-5-nano \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] as closed-source models of varying
sizes. To comprehensively demonstrate the effectiveness of Matryoshka
Agent, we conduct extensive experiments across a wide spectrum of model
combinations. For convenience of notation, we denote each configuration
in the form Model A + Model B, where Model A serves as the Orchestrator
and Model B acts as sub-agents. For open-source models, we adopt the
default optimal temperature of 0.7; for closed-source models, we use the
default temperature setting of 1.0. We keep all other configurations as
default.

Agent and Environment Design. We refer to the agent scaffold for
benchmarking in MLE-Dojo as Dojo Agent, and our method as Matryoshka
Agent. We adapt Dojo Agent to make it more capable of handling extended
time horizons and increased numbers of interactions with the execution
environment. Specifically, we introduce a maximum token limit for each
single-round feedback from the environment and apply an effective
truncation strategy. The context and maximum output lengths are
determined by the the underlying model and are not constrained in either
agent, allowing the agents to leverage their full capabilities during
long-horizon interactions. For each task and each run, both agents are
allowed up to a maximum of 12 hours of execution time. For each task and
each model configuration, we run three parallel trials and report the
best-of-three HumanRank score as the final performance correspondingly.
See implementation details of both agents in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Agent and Environment Design ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering").

Training Strategy. For Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, we train the model
to serve both as Orchestrator and Sub-Agents. We first perform
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 100 high-quality Matryoshka Agent
trajectories, each comprising one complete Orchestrator trajectory and
multiple complete sub-agent trajectories and obtain Qwen3-30B-Coder-SFT.
We also perform SFT on 100 high-quality Dojo Agent trajectories,
yielding Qwen3-30B-Coder-SFT-Dojo as a monolithic trained baseline. In
addition, we fine-tune Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 using only Orchestrator
trajectories, resulting in Qwen3-4B-SFT.¹¹ 1 We find that
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 exhibits significantly weaker performance when
used as a Sub-Agent due to model scale and applying SFT to Qwen3-4B as
Dojo Agent can even degrade the performance. See
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Failure Analysis of Qwen3-4B ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering")
for detailed analyses. We further apply reinforcement learning (RL) to
the SFT models. Specifically, we pair Qwen3-4B-SFT as the Orchestrator
with o4-mini as Sub-Agents and apply RL training, resulting in
Qwen3-4B-RL. We conduct RL training for Qwen3-30B-Coder-SFT to
simultaneously serve as both the Orchestrator and Sub-Agents, yielding
Qwen3-30B-Coder-RL.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experiment Setups ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering")
reports HumanRank scores across agent frameworks, model configurations,
and evaluation task categories. For Qwen3-30B-Coder, Matryoshka Agent
improves the model from 0.3302 as a monolithic Dojo Agent to 0.3652 when
the same model serves as both Orchestrator and Sub-Agent, showing that
the hierarchical framework provides gains even without additional
training. To further distinguish architecture gains from SFT gains, we
compare against Qwen3-30B-Coder-SFT-Dojo, a monolithic Dojo Agent
trained with the same SFT protocol. Although SFT improves this trained
monolithic baseline, Matryoshka Agent with Qwen3-30B-Coder-SFT + self
further reaches 0.4197, indicating that the hierarchical architecture
contributes beyond supervised fine-tuning alone. Finally, online RL
further improves the Matryoshka Agent model to 0.4515, resulting in at
most 36.7% relative performance gain over the Dojo Agent baseline,
demonstrating effectiveness of improving Orchestrator decisions and
Sub-Agents capabilities with Iterative Online Optimization.

o4-mini improves from 0.4832 as a Dojo Agent to 0.5465 under Matryoshka
Agent, suggesting that inference-only gains are also evident for
frontier closed-source models. For smaller models, Qwen3-4B performs
poorly as a monolithic Dojo Agent, achieving only 0.1878 HumanRank.
However, when served as the Orchestrator within Matryoshka Agent with
o4-mini Sub-Agents, it reaches 0.4613, and after SFT and RL further
improves to 0.5360. This nearly matches o4-mini as the Orchestrator
under Matryoshka Agent, showing that Iterative Online Optimization can
make a small model an effective high-level decision layer when paired
with stronger execution models.

Overall, these results suggest that models across a wide range of scales
can serve as effective Orchestrators within Matryoshka Agent. Moreover,
additional training on MLE tasks via SFT and RL consistently strengthens
their orchestration capability, in some cases enabling smaller models to
reach or even surpass the performance of substantially larger models. In
contrast, the effectiveness of Sub-Agents is more sensitive to model
scale. Small models, such as Qwen3-4B, struggle to reliably follow and
execute complex instructions, leading to a noticeable gap between
instruction generation as an Orchestrator and instruction execution as a
sub-agent, as indicated in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Failure Analysis of Qwen3-4B ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering").

### 4.5 Cumulated Context Length

Figure 2: Cumulated Context Length across rounds. X-axis denotes the

Dojo Agent and Orchestrator are averaged over tasks, while contexts of

A core advantage of Matryoshka Agent lies in its effective and clean
separation of context management between the Orchestrator and the
Sub-Agents. As interaction rounds with the environment increase, each
sub-agent maintains an independent context with a fixed upper bound that
does not grow with the number of interactions. At the same time,
although the Orchestrator’s context does grow over time, its growth rate
remains modest even as the interaction horizon becomes long. As a
result, Matryoshka Agent remains robust even under extreme settings with
very long time periods and a large number of interactions, whereas the
context of the Dojo Agent expands more rapidly.
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.5 Cumulated Context Length ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering")
illustrates the evolution of context length for the Orchestrator, the
Sub-Agents, and the Dojo Agent as the number of interaction rounds
increases. The figure shows that the Orchestrator’s context length grows
at a slower rate than that of the Dojo Agent, while the Sub-Agents’
context lengths remain stable across interaction rounds due to their
independence. We further discuss context length scaling in

## 5 Conclusion

We introduce Matryoshka Agent, a hierarchical agent framework that
decomposes long-horizon machine learning engineering into coordinated
decision-making and execution layers. Matryoshka Agent effectively
mitigates long-context reasoning challenges and improves scalability
under limited computation budgets. We propose a training paradigm for
further improving Matryoshka Agent. Extensive experiments across diverse
MLE tasks and model configurations demonstrate consistent and
substantial performance gains, as well as strong flexibility through
plug-and-play orchestration. These results suggest that hierarchical
sub-agent unfolding provides a principled and general paradigm for
tackling Machine Learning Engineering like complex, long-horizon agentic
tasks.

## Appendix A Benchmark and Metric Details

MLE-Dojo Benchmark. We conduct all experiments on
MLE-Dojo \[[40](#bib.bib40)\], a large-scale benchmark designed to
evaluate autonomous agents on realistic machine learning engineering
(MLE) tasks. MLE-Dojo consists of 200 end-to-end Kaggle-style MLE tasks,
including 150 training tasks and 50 held-out evaluation tasks, following
the official benchmark split. Each task requires agents to perform a
complete MLE workflow, including data preprocessing, feature
engineering, model selection, training, evaluation, and submission.

A key feature of MLE-Dojo is its interactive environment, where agents
can iteratively submit solutions to a leaderboard and receive exact
evaluation feedback after each interaction. This enables agents to
refine strategies over long horizons, closely resembling real-world
iterative MLE pipelines.

For evaluation, tasks are grouped into four categories following
MLE-Dojo: MLE-Lite (21 tasks), Tabular (10 tasks), CV (9 tasks), and NLP
(10 tasks), corresponding to lightweight benchmark tasks, structured
tabular problems, computer vision tasks, and natural language processing
tasks, respectively.

HumanRank Metric. We adopt HumanRank as the primary evaluation metric.
HumanRank measures the relative ranking of an agent submission against
human competitors on the leaderboard. Suppose a submission ranks at
position $`p`$ among $`N`$ total submissions; the HumanRank score is
defined as:

|     |                  |     |     |
|-----|------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s=1-\frac{p}{N}.
       ```               |     | (5) |

Thus, HumanRank reflects the fraction of human participants that the
agent outperforms, with higher values indicating stronger performance.

HumanRank is a normalized metric within the range $`[0,1]`$, strictly
aligned with the original task-specific leaderboard metrics (e.g.,
accuracy, RMSE, or BLEU). To mitigate potential bias between public and
private leaderboards, HumanRank is computed independently on both
leaderboards and averaged as the final reward score. This normalization
enables fair and comparable evaluation across heterogeneous MLE tasks
with different scoring scales.

## Appendix B Agent and Environment Design

Dojo Agent. The max time limit is set to 12 hours, with agents having
full access to their interaction histories. A unified, concise prompt
with clear instructions is provided at the beginning, without additional
extraneous information. The maximum input token length is not limited,
while each output round is also not limited. We do not restrict the
number of submission attempts to enable continuous improvement. To
generate valid submission files and scores, agents must explicitly use
the "execute_code" command; submissions are not automated. We
pre-install commonly used Python packages, though agents may install
additional packages within their generated code as needed. We make
several key modifications to the original MLE-Dojo Agent. First, we
remove the explicit cap on the model’s context length. While MLE-Dojo
typically restricts the context window to 50k tokens, we instead allow
each model to utilize its native maximum context capacity. Second, we
apply output truncation to environment feedback: when the environment
output exceeds 5k tokens, we retain the first 2.5k and the last 2.5k
tokens and concatenate them. Together, these changes enable the Dojo
Agent to operate effectively over substantially longer horizons,
alleviating premature context saturation while preserving critical
execution signals from the environment.

Matryoshka Agent. The max time limit is set to 12 hours. A unified,
concise prompt with clear instructions is provided at the beginning,
without additional extraneous information. As with the Dojo Agent, we do
not impose an explicit limit on the model’s maximum context length,
allowing it to fully utilize its native context capacity. We also adopt
the same environment output truncation mechanism: when the environment
feedback exceeds 5k tokens, the first and last 2.5k tokens are retained
and concatenated. Each Sub-Agent is allowed to perform up to 10
debugging iterations within a round. If this limit is exceeded, the
corresponding run is terminated and the resulting score is recorded as
N/A.

## Appendix C SFT Data Collection

We collect Matryoshka Agent supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data by running
the full Matryoshka Agent pipeline with
gemini-2.5-flash \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] serving simultaneously as both the
Orchestrator and the Sub-Agents. We also collect Dojo Agent supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) data by running Dojo Agent with the same model.
Specifically, we randomly sample 100 training tasks from the MLE-Dojo
benchmark and execute the agentic system on each task with a maximum
wall-clock budget of 12 hours. For each task, execution is early-stopped
once a perfect score is achieved.

This procedure yields 100 complete agentic trajectories, one per task.
Each complete trajectory consists of a single Orchestrator trajectory
spanning multiple rounds, together with multiple corresponding Sub-Agent
execution trajectories instantiated across rounds. We retain the 100
Orchestrator trajectories as SFT data to fine-tune
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. In addition, we use the same 100 Orchestrator
trajectories together with 300 selected Sub-Agent trajectories to
separately fine-tune Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, resulting in
SFT-initialized versions of both the Orchestrator and Sub-Agent models.

## Appendix D Mean and Variance of Evaluation

For each of the 50 evaluation tasks, we run three independent trials and
report the per-task best score before averaging across tasks. This
best-of-three protocol follows MLE-Dojo and is designed to reduce the
effect of random execution failures, such as malformed submissions
produced in otherwise reasonable solution attempts. It also reflects the
practical setting of MLE competitions, where multiple submissions are
attempted and the best valid result is selected.

## Appendix E Failure Analysis of Qwen3-4B

In this section, we analyze why we do not employ Qwen3-4B as both the
Orchestrator and Sub-Agent in our main experiments. We present
representative execution trajectories for the pretrained Qwen3-4B model
(Table [9](#A8.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix H Case Study on Instruction Dynamics ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering"))
and its SFT-enhanced counterpart
(Table [10](#A8.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Appendix H Case Study on Instruction Dynamics ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering"))
on the leaf-classification task from the MLE-Dojo evaluation set.

Qwen3-4B: Insufficient Instruction Granularity. As shown in
Table [9](#A8.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix H Case Study on Instruction Dynamics ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering"),
the pretrained Qwen3-4B Orchestrator issues relatively coarse and
high-level instructions, such as performing PCA-based feature
engineering and training a LightGBM classifier. However, these
instructions lack concrete procedural scaffolding (e.g., explicit data
schema handling, feature concatenation strategies, and submission
formatting constraints). As a result, the Sub-Agent fails to correctly
execute the pipeline, exhibiting syntax-level hallucinations and
repeated schema mismatches (e.g., missing margin_1 columns). Eventually,
the Sub-Agent collapses to a degenerate fallback strategy that outputs
uniform class probabilities, yielding near-random leaderboard
performance (Position 1546/1595). This indicates that the pretrained
Orchestrator does not provide sufficient operational detail for reliable
downstream execution.

Qwen3-4B-SFT: Improved Instructions but Weak Instruction Following
Ability. After supervised fine-tuning, the Qwen3-4B-SFT Orchestrator
produces substantially more detailed and structured instructions
(Table [10](#A8.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Appendix H Case Study on Instruction Dynamics ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering")),
including explicit data loading steps, feature concatenation procedures,
LightGBM hyperparameters, and submission constraints. This demonstrates
that SFT successfully transfers procedural instruction templates from
the teacher model. However, despite the improved instruction quality,
the Qwen3-4B-SFT Sub-Agent still fails to follow these instructions
effectively. Concretely, the Sub-Agent repeatedly encounters execution
errors such as missing feature columns and fragmented DataFrame
insertions, and ultimately fails to produce a valid submission. This
highlights that, at this model scale, instruction following and
execution grounding remain weak even when the Orchestrator provides
detailed guidance.

## Appendix F Analysis of Context Length Scaling

We analyze the empirical growth rates of context length across
interaction rounds to characterize the scaling behavior of the
Orchestrator & Sub-Agent framework. As shown in
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.5 Cumulated Context Length ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Matryoshka Agent: Unfolding Sub-Agents for Long-Horizon Machine Learning Engineering"),
the Orchestrator exhibits a moderate linear growth in context length,
with an estimated slope of 1201.93 tokens per round, while the Dojo
Agent grows substantially faster, with a slope of 1870.29 tokens per
round. In contrast, the Sub-Agents maintain an approximately constant
context length across rounds, due to their independent and resettable
execution contexts.

We further estimate the maximum number of interaction rounds that can be
supported under a fixed context budget. Assuming a base model such as
Qwen3-30B-Coder \[[47](#bib.bib47)\] with a natural maximum context
length of 256K tokens, the Orchestrator in our framework can support
approximately $`\lfloor 256{,}000/1201.93\rfloor\approx 213`$ rounds
before exceeding the context limit, whereas the Dojo Agent can support
only $`\lfloor 256{,}000/1870.29\rfloor\approx 137`$ rounds. This
indicates that the modular Orchestrator & Sub-Agent design significantly
improves long-horizon scalability by decoupling execution memory from
orchestration memory, enabling substantially longer interaction horizons
under the same context budget.
````
