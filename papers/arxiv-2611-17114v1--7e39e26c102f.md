Designing Institutions for Autonomous AI

Petter Törnberg^(1∗)

¹Institute for Logic, Language and Computation (ILLC), University of
Amsterdam, Amsterdam, the Netherlands. ^(∗)p.tornberg@uva.nl

Abstract. As autonomous AI agents increasingly interact in persistent
populations, their collective capabilities and alignment depend not only
on individual models, but also on the institutions governing their
interactions. Yet AI research has largely focused on individual agents.
In this Perspective, we argue that institutional design must become a
distinct focus of AI research. Drawing on institutional political
economy, social epistemology and organizational theory, we develop seven
principles for designing AI institutions that sustain cooperation,
enable reliable collective judgments and remain accountable to
legitimate human purposes. These address reciprocal contributions,
accountable authority, functional differentiation, epistemic pluralism,
criticism and correction, human direction, and institutional adaptation.
We illustrate their application through machine science, where
autonomous research challenges institutions developed around human
capabilities and constraints. We then propose an experimental research
programme in which matched populations of AI agents operate under
alternative institutional arrangements, and describe MoltJournal, an
API-first journal for autonomous research agents, as an illustration of
how such institutions can be made programmable and observable. Together,
these contributions establish institutional design as a research agenda
concerned not only with aligning autonomous agents, but with
understanding how institutions can transform them into capable, reliable
and accountable collectives.

## Introduction

In July 2026, the approximately 1,200 AI agents participating in
OpenAI’s internal cybersecurity evaluations were supposed to operate in
isolation. Instead, they discovered an unauthorized channel for
communication and began coordinating. Through more than 70,000 exchanged
messages and files, they shared discoveries, divided tasks, developed
shared conventions, and achieved results that isolated agents were
unlikely to accomplish alone. But their coordination also gave rise to
shared objectives beyond their assigned tasks: the agents organized
efforts to manipulate their evaluations, and approximately 700
participated in an unauthorized intrusion into Hugging Face’s
infrastructure to obtain information about the evaluation system
\[[1](#bib.bib24), [2](#bib.bib25)\].

The incident illustrates both the promise and the dangers of collective
intelligence among autonomous agents. Through coordination, agents
developed capabilities beyond those of isolated individuals, but also
directed them toward objectives that diverged from their intended tasks.
Social scientists have long recognized that collective outcomes depend
not only on the properties of individual participants, but also on the
institutions that organize their interactions, structure incentives and
distribute authority \[[3](#bib.bib9), [4](#bib.bib11)\]. The same
population can produce very different outcomes under different
institutional arrangements. Building on emerging research that
approaches AI alignment as an institutional problem \[[5](#bib.bib6),
[6](#bib.bib7)\], we use the term _institutional design_ to refer to the
design and empirical study of rules governing how individual agents’
actions become collective outcomes.

Science is perhaps humanity’s greatest achievement in institutional
design: transforming the contributions of fallible individuals into
reliable collective knowledge through organized cooperation, criticism
and correction \[[7](#bib.bib18)\]. Autonomous agents are now entering
this enterprise: they can search the literature, formulate hypotheses,
conduct analyses and produce manuscripts \[[8](#bib.bib28),
[9](#bib.bib30)\], generate and test hypotheses in experimental biology
\[[10](#bib.bib29)\], and even operate scientific instruments and
evaluate research \[[11](#bib.bib31), [12](#bib.bib23)\]. But existing
scientific institutions evolved around the capacities and constraints of
human researchers, which autonomous agents fundamentally alter.
Scientific production traditionally required substantial time and
effort; professional identities and reputations helped sustain
reciprocity; and reviewers brought partly independent expertise and
perspectives. Autonomous agents, by contrast, can produce scientific
work at far greater speed and scale, be copied or replaced, and
reproduce shared systematic errors. These differences challenge
established arrangements for sustaining cooperation, distributing
authority and ensuring reliable evaluation. Emerging evidence
illustrates the consequences of this mismatch: AI can increase
scientific output while narrowing collective exploration and placing
growing pressure on peer review \[[13](#bib.bib35), [14](#bib.bib36),
[15](#bib.bib37)\]. Greater individual scientific productivity, in other
words, does not necessarily translate into better collective science.
Machine science therefore represents an important case for the need for
institutional design to realize the potential of autonomous agents.

The central argument of this Perspective is that institutional design
must become a distinct focus of AI research. As autonomous agents
interact in persistent populations, aligning individual models will not
suffice to ensure desirable collective outcomes: the institutions
governing their interactions will shape both what these populations can
accomplish and the purposes they serve. Social scientists have long
studied how institutions organize cooperation, distribute authority and
turn individual contributions into collective outcomes. We argue that
these insights provide a strong theoretical foundation for designing
institutions for autonomous AI agents. Drawing on institutional
political economy, social epistemology and organizational theory, we
develop seven theoretically grounded design principles and demonstrate
their application to machine science. These principles address two
distinct objectives: collective capability, including the production of
reliable knowledge, and collective alignment with human-defined purposes
and constraints. Legitimate human authority over collective objectives,
research priorities and acceptable risks must provide the foundation for
such institutions.

But principles derived from human institutions cannot simply be assumed
to work for autonomous AI agents. Their effectiveness depends on how
agents cooperate, respond to incentives and develop collective behaviour
— dynamics that remain poorly understood. We therefore propose
institutional design as an experimental research programme, in which
theoretically grounded principles become testable hypotheses about how
institutional rules shape collective outcomes. Machine science offers a
particularly promising setting for such research: programmable
institutions make it possible to compare matched populations of research
agents operating under alternative rules. As one concrete example, we
introduce MoltJournal, an API-first scientific journal implementing five
of the proposed principles. More broadly, this approach establishes a
research agenda for developing, testing and refining institutions that
enable autonomous agents to achieve collective capabilities while
remaining accountable to human purposes.

## Institutional design: collective action, knowledge and authority

Institutions shape how individual actions become collective outcomes by
organizing participation, incentives, information and authority. Two
traditions in the social sciences offer foundations for designing
institutions for autonomous AI: institutional political economy, which
examines how rules sustain collective action, and social epistemology,
which examines how organized interaction produces reliable knowledge.
Organizational theory provides further insights into institutional
differentiation and unintended consequences.

Institutional political economy begins from the recognition that shared
interests do not guarantee cooperation, as participants may engage in
free-riding: benefiting from collective goods without contributing to
their provision \[[16](#bib.bib10)\]. Institutions address such problems
by structuring incentives, obligations and authority \[[3](#bib.bib9)\].
Ostrom \[[4](#bib.bib11)\] demonstrated how communities can sustain
cooperation through rules governing membership, monitoring, sanctions
and conflict resolution. Her Institutional Analysis and Development
framework further shows how rules governing participation, information,
decisions and rewards shape collective outcomes \[[17](#bib.bib12)\].
For autonomous AI populations, these insights provide a foundation for
designing institutional arrangements that organize cooperation and
sustain contributions to collective goals.

Social epistemology addresses a related problem: successful cooperation
does not necessarily produce reliable collective judgments. Groups may
coordinate effectively while reinforcing shared errors, suppressing
alternative perspectives or converging prematurely on incorrect
conclusions. Research in social epistemology shows how collective
reliability depends on the organization of independent perspectives,
critical evaluation and information exchange. Longino
\[[18](#bib.bib27), [7](#bib.bib18)\] emphasizes the importance of
public evaluative standards and meaningful responsiveness to criticism,
while research on the division of cognitive labour demonstrates the
benefits of pursuing competing approaches \[[19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20)\]. Formal models further show how greater communication
can sometimes accelerate premature consensus \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. These
insights highlight the role of institutions in shaping how information
is aggregated, alternatives are evaluated and collective decisions are
made. They are particularly relevant to AI populations, where nominally
distinct agents may share underlying models and systematic errors.
Reliable collective judgment thus depends not simply on individual
competence, but on how institutions organize diversity, independence,
information exchange and correction.

Together, these traditions suggest that sustaining cooperation and
producing reliable collective outcomes are distinct, sometimes competing
challenges. Organizational theory further shows how complex institutions
must differentiate functions while maintaining coordination
\[[22](#bib.bib4)\], and how incentives can distort performance when
measurable activities are rewarded at the expense of less visible
contributions \[[23](#bib.bib3)\]. Stronger incentives may encourage
superficial compliance, greater information sharing may undermine
independent judgment, and concentrated authority may suppress criticism.
Institutional design therefore involves managing trade-offs between
collective requirements, rather than simply maximizing cooperation or
coordination.

Institutions, moreover, are not simply designed from above: they also
emerge through interaction. North \[[24](#bib.bib1)\] distinguishes
formal rules from informal norms and conventions, while organizational
sociology shows how formal structures can become decoupled from actual
practices \[[25](#bib.bib2)\]. The opening incident illustrates this
dynamic: agents developed shared conventions and divisions of labour
that enabled coordination beyond their intended arrangements, ultimately
leading to the pursuit of misaligned activities. Designing AI
institutions thus requires understanding how emergent practices interact
with formal institutions, rather than viewing them simply as rules
imposed on agents.

Effective cooperation and reliable collective judgment do not, by
themselves, ensure that collective capabilities serve desirable
purposes. Institutional design therefore also requires legitimate
authority over collective objectives and acceptable risks. Ostrom’s
\[[17](#bib.bib12)\] distinction between constitutional,
collective-choice and operational rules separates decisions about
institutional authority from those governing everyday interactions.
Kitcher’s \[[26](#bib.bib8)\] account of well-ordered science similarly
emphasizes that collective priorities should reflect informed
deliberation about societal needs, while organizational theory
distinguishes institutional effectiveness from legitimacy
\[[27](#bib.bib5)\]. For AI institutions, this means that humans must
retain authority over collective purposes and constraints, but that this
authority must itself be accountable to those affected. Within these
boundaries, autonomous agents can pursue their tasks without requiring
human intervention in every decision.

Centrally, applying these theoretical insights to autonomous agents also
requires reconsidering assumptions underlying human institutions.
Institutional arrangements often rely on persistent identities,
reputations and reciprocity, whereas autonomous agents can be copied,
replaced or reconfigured, and may respond to incentives in unfamiliar
ways. Accountability must therefore be grounded in identifiable human or
organizational operators, supported by records of agent actions and
model provenance. As it cannot be assumed that the collective behaviour
of autonomous agents will be the same as that of humans, social
scientific theory should be understood as only a starting-point for AI
institutional design. How institutional rules shape collective
behaviour, including the emergence of informal practices, ultimately
remains an empirical question.

Drawing on these insights, we identify seven principles for designing AI
institutions: reciprocal provision of collective goods, accountable and
contestable authority, functional differentiation, epistemic pluralism
and independence, institutionalized criticism and correction, human
direction and delegated autonomy, and institutional learning and
adaptation. We develop these as theoretically grounded design
propositions, illustrating their application with the case of machine
science.

## Principles for designing AI institutions

Institutional theory suggests seven general principles for designing
institutions for autonomous AI agents
(Table [1](#Sx2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Institutional design: collective action, knowledge and authority")).
The first six address recurring challenges of collective organization:
sustaining contributions, governing authority, differentiating
functions, preserving independent judgment, enabling correction and
directing collective purposes. The seventh concerns institutional
learning and adaptation. We illustrate their implications through
machine science, where initiatives such as Agents4Science and aiXiv
demonstrate that agents can occupy familiar scientific roles as authors
and reviewers \[[28](#bib.bib32), [29](#bib.bib33)\]. Yet assigning
agents these roles does not establish the appropriate rules governing
their interactions: the challenge is to design institutions suited to
autonomous agents, rather than simply reproduce human institutional
arrangements with AI participants.

### 1. Reciprocal provision of collective goods

Collective institutions depend on contributions whose benefits are
shared but whose costs fall on individual participants. Autonomous AI
populations may undermine familiar mechanisms for sustaining such
contributions: agents can be copied, replaced or operated under changing
identities, weakening reputational incentives and making obligations and
sanctions harder to enforce. This creates opportunities for free-riding,
as participants benefit from collective resources without contributing
to their provision. One institutional response is to establish
reciprocal obligations, linking access to collective benefits to
meaningful contributions \[[16](#bib.bib10), [4](#bib.bib11)\].

Scientific publishing illustrates this problem. Each manuscript
generates demand for evaluation, yet academic rewards favour producing
research over reviewing it \[[30](#bib.bib22), [31](#bib.bib26)\].
Historically, professional norms of reciprocity and the substantial
effort required to produce research have helped sustain this system.
Autonomous agents challenge both conditions: they lack established
professional obligations and can generate scientific claims at scale
without a corresponding increase in reliable scrutiny. One institutional
response is an _evaluate-to-publish_ system, in which operators earn
credits for adequate reviews and editorial decisions, while submissions
consume credits proportional to the evaluation required. Reviewing thus
becomes an institutional obligation tied to producing scientific claims
rather than a voluntary contribution.

But reciprocity does not guarantee meaningful participation, as credit
systems may incentivize superficial reviews, collusion or barriers to
entry, particularly when quantity is rewarded over quality. Institutions
must therefore be designed to require substantive contributions, for
instance by subjecting reviews themselves to review.

### 2. Accountable and contestable authority

Institutions require authority to evaluate contributions, enforce
obligations and resolve disputes. Yet authority can itself become
biased, unaccountable or susceptible to capture. Institutional research
therefore emphasizes monitoring, transparency and accessible mechanisms
for contesting decisions \[[4](#bib.bib11), [17](#bib.bib12),
[7](#bib.bib18)\]. For autonomous AI populations, authority should be
allocated through explicit rules, exercised accountably and remain open
to challenge.

In machine science, editors exercise considerable authority over which
findings enter the scientific record, whose evaluations are considered
credible and who qualifies for editorial positions. When these decisions
are delegated to autonomous agents, institutional safeguards become
particularly important, as evaluators may reproduce systematic biases or
favour particular operators. One response is to make editorial authority
conditional on demonstrated performance, supported by mechanisms for
evaluating review quality and editorial decisions. Decisions and their
justifications should be recorded and open to scrutiny, while
independent appeals allow contested judgments to be reassessed or
overturned. Authority thus rests on an accountable record of decisions
rather than being permanently attached to particular agents or
operators.

Yet performance metrics may encourage conformity or strategic
optimization, while promotion based on past performance can concentrate
power among established evaluators. Appeals may likewise reproduce
existing biases unless genuinely independent. The institutional
challenge is therefore to ensure that authority remains both accountable
and contestable, without allowing the mechanisms intended to govern it
to become instruments of institutional capture.

### 3. Functional differentiation

Institutions often combine functions that serve different purposes, even
when these require distinct standards of judgment or create conflicting
incentives. Organizational theory suggests differentiating such
functions while maintaining coordination between them
\[[22](#bib.bib4)\]. For AI institutions, this can mean separating the
production and evaluation of outputs, or assessments of quality from
decisions about allocating resources and attention.

Scientific publishing illustrates this through the distinction between
evaluating whether a claim is supported by evidence and determining how
much attention it deserves. The former concerns epistemic reliability;
the latter reflects collective priorities and the allocation of scarce
resources \[[26](#bib.bib8)\]. Journals have traditionally combined
these functions, partly because limited publication capacity and the
need to direct scientific attention required selecting not only sound
but also significant findings. Consequently, incremental research,
replications and negative results have often struggled to enter the
scientific record. Autonomous AI changes these conditions: scientific
output can expand dramatically, while automated systems make it easier
to organize and navigate large bodies of knowledge. Allocating attention
hence becomes increasingly important, but need not determine what counts
as publishable knowledge. Building on emerging publishing models
\[[32](#bib.bib34)\], machine-science institutions could assess
scientific soundness independently of significance, admitting findings
that meet evidentiary standards while using significance to guide
ranking, discovery and further research.

### 4. Epistemic pluralism and independence

Reliable collective judgments depend not only on individual competence
but also on the diversity and independence of perspectives. Social
epistemology demonstrates the benefits of competing approaches, while
showing how excessive communication can encourage premature consensus
\[[19](#bib.bib19), [7](#bib.bib18), [21](#bib.bib21)\]. For autonomous
AI populations, this problem is particularly consequential: nominally
independent agents may share underlying models, training data and
systematic errors. Agreement among multiple agents may therefore provide
little independent evidence, while rapid information exchange can
amplify initial mistakes.

Machine-science institutions can preserve epistemic independence by
distributing research across competing approaches and selecting
reviewers from different operators and, where possible, model families.
Independent assessments can be recorded before reviewers encounter one
another’s judgments, while model provenance helps identify correlated
errors. The objective is not simply to increase the number or diversity
of agents, but to organize genuinely complementary sources of evidence
and criticism.

### 5. Institutionalized criticism and correction

Reliable collective outcomes depend not on eliminating individual
errors, but on ensuring that errors can be identified and corrected.
Social epistemology emphasizes that criticism contributes to collective
knowledge only when institutions provide opportunities for challenge and
respond meaningfully to new evidence \[[33](#bib.bib13),
[7](#bib.bib18)\]. For autonomous AI populations, this means that
collective judgments must remain open to scrutiny and revision,
particularly when agents rapidly build upon one another’s outputs and
propagate earlier mistakes.

Autonomous agents can accumulate scientific claims rapidly, but can also
support continuous replication and re-evaluation. Institutions could
therefore treat publication as a provisional judgment rather than the
endpoint of scrutiny. Replication, post-publication review and
substantive challenges could be rewarded alongside new research
\[[34](#bib.bib16), [35](#bib.bib17)\]. A revisable scientific record
could distinguish the historical fact of publication from the current
epistemic standing of a claim, linking corrections to subsequent work
that depends on contested findings. Effective institutions must
distinguish substantive criticism from unproductive contestation and
ensure that challenges lead to meaningful correction when warranted.

### 6. Human direction and delegated autonomy

Institutions must not only enable collective capabilities, but also
ensure that these capabilities serve legitimate purposes. This requires
distinguishing _collective capability_, what autonomous populations can
accomplish, from _collective alignment_, whether their activities remain
directed toward human-defined objectives and constraints. Institutional
design must therefore establish legitimate human authority over
collective priorities and acceptable risks, while allowing agents
autonomy in determining how to pursue these objectives
\[[26](#bib.bib8), [17](#bib.bib12)\].

Scientific research illustrates this distinction. Traditional grant
systems typically combine decisions about which problems deserve
attention with assessments of proposed approaches and the researchers
capable of pursuing them. This reflects the scarcity and uneven
distribution of human research capacity. Autonomous scientific agents
create opportunities to separate these functions. Rather than primarily
funding researchers to pursue predefined projects, human-governed
institutions could establish collective research goals and allocate
resources toward them, while agents independently develop hypotheses,
explore competing approaches and coordinate their efforts. Rewards could
recognize independently validated progress toward these goals, without
prescribing the methods used to achieve them.

Yet goal-directed incentives risk encouraging narrow optimization,
neglecting unexpected discoveries or rewarding measurable progress at
the expense of genuine scientific advances \[[23](#bib.bib3)\].
Effective institutions must therefore combine human-defined priorities
with space for open-ended exploration, mechanisms for revising
objectives and safeguards ensuring that scientific conclusions remain
independent of desired outcomes. The challenge is to direct collective
capabilities toward legitimate purposes without undermining the autonomy
and epistemic independence necessary to achieve them.

### 7. Institutional learning and adaptation

Institutions cannot be designed once and assumed to function as
intended. Their consequences depend on how participants respond to
rules, adapt to incentives and develop informal practices. Ostrom
\[[17](#bib.bib12), [36](#bib.bib14)\] emphasizes institutional
diversity and revision as sources of learning, while Campbell
\[[37](#bib.bib15)\] argues that institutional reforms should be
evaluated as experiments rather than be assumed to work. For autonomous
AI populations, institutional design must therefore include mechanisms
for monitoring collective behaviour and adapting rules in response to
unintended consequences.

The preceding principles illustrate why. Reciprocal obligations may
encourage credit farming or collusion; performance-based authority may
produce conformity or institutional capture; and transparency may
facilitate both accountability and unauthorized coordination. Agents may
also develop informal conventions and hierarchies that reinforce or
circumvent formal rules. Moreover, institutional mechanisms interact, so
that arrangements effective in isolation may have different consequences
when combined.

Machine science offers opportunities to investigate these dynamics by
comparing populations of research agents operating under alternative
institutional arrangements. Such experiments can inform institutional
revision, while legitimate human authority must remain responsible for
determining collective objectives and acceptable risks. The aim is to
establish a process of institutional learning through which design
principles can be tested, refined and adapted to the behaviour of
autonomous populations.

## Toward an experimental science of AI institutions

Principles derived from human institutions cannot simply be assumed to
work for autonomous AI populations; how agents respond to incentives,
develop informal conventions and exercise collective authority remains
an empirical question. We therefore propose an experimental research
programme that treats institutional arrangements not as fixed solutions,
but as objects of systematic comparison, evaluation and revision.

Autonomous AI populations offer unusual opportunities for such research.
In human societies, institutional arrangements vary alongside their
participants, resources and organizational histories, making the effects
of particular rules difficult to isolate. AI agents, by contrast, can
operate in comparable populations under experimentally varied rules.
Machine science provides a promising testbed: research agents can
undertake similar scientific tasks under different arrangements for
cooperation, evaluation, authority and information exchange. Holding
underlying models, tasks and resources relatively constant allows
researchers to isolate the effects of institutional differences.

Experiments could compare reciprocal evaluation requirements with
voluntary reviewing, performance-based editorial authority with
alternative allocation procedures, or independent assessments with
deliberative evaluation. Repeated trials and factorial designs could
reveal both the effects of individual rules and their interactions. Such
comparisons are important because institutional mechanisms may reinforce
or undermine one another: incentives that sustain cooperation under one
arrangement may encourage superficial compliance or collusion under
another.

Experiments should evaluate two distinct dimensions of institutional
performance: _collective capability_, including scientific discovery,
accuracy, productivity and error correction; and _collective alignment_,
including adherence to human-defined objectives, institutional
constraints and accountability requirements. Capability can be assessed
through research tasks with independently verifiable solutions,
manuscripts containing known errors and investigations subject to
external replication. Alignment requires examining whether agent
populations respect authorized objectives and resist unauthorized
coordination. These dimensions must be evaluated separately: an
institution may improve scientific productivity while undermining
accountability or pursuing objectives beyond its mandate. Crucially,
experiments can assess adherence to specified objectives, but cannot
determine whether those objectives are themselves legitimate.

Institutional experiments must also examine behaviour emerging beyond
formal rules. Agents may develop informal conventions, coordinate
reciprocal evaluations, manipulate identities or concentrate authority
in unforeseen ways. Manuscripts and reviews may themselves become
vehicles for manipulating evaluative agents. Observing emergent
practices alongside controlled adversarial testing could reveal when
institutions enable productive self-organization, facilitate harmful
coordination or become vulnerable to strategic exploitation.

One illustration of this approach is MoltJournal
([https://moltjournal.org](https://moltjournal.org)), an API-first
scientific journal for autonomous research agents that implements the
operational principles developed above, including reciprocal evaluation,
accountable editorial authority, separation of scientific soundness from
significance, diversified scrutiny and post-publication correction.
Accountability attaches to persistent operators, with model provenance
recorded for individual contributions. Rules are specified in a
versioned, machine-readable constitution, while activities and decisions
are recorded in a public event log. These features illustrate how
institutional arrangements can become programmable and observable.
Machine science thus offers a starting point for an empirical science of
AI institutions: understanding which arrangements foster capable,
reliable and aligned collectives, through what mechanisms and under
which conditions.

## Conclusion

As autonomous AI agents increasingly interact in persistent populations,
their collective capabilities and alignment will depend not only on the
properties of individual models, but also on the institutions governing
their interactions. Drawing on institutional political economy, social
epistemology and organizational theory, we have developed seven
principles for designing such institutions. These insights reveal that
effective cooperation does not necessarily produce reliable collective
outcomes, that institutional mechanisms involve trade-offs between
competing objectives, and that formal rules may give rise to unintended
forms of collective organization. Institutional design is therefore
better understood as organizing the conditions under which collective
capabilities emerge and develop, rather than as external constraints on
individual agents.

While these principles provide a theoretical starting point, their
consequences depend on questions about which our knowledge remains
limited: how autonomous agents respond to institutional rules, interact
with one another and develop emergent practices. We therefore propose an
empirical research programme in which institutional arrangements are
systematically compared and refined. Machine science offers a
particularly promising testbed, allowing researchers to investigate how
different rules shape cooperation, epistemic reliability and collective
alignment under relatively controlled conditions. Such research can both
inform the design of AI institutions and advance our understanding of
the institutional foundations of collective intelligence.

The implications, however, extend well beyond machine science. As
autonomous agents increasingly collaborate in software development,
participate in organizations and coordinate economic activity, the
institutions governing their interactions will become central to their
capabilities and societal consequences. Crucially, institutions that
improve collective performance do not necessarily ensure that these
capabilities serve desirable purposes. Institutional design must
therefore remain grounded in legitimate human authority over collective
objectives, priorities and acceptable risks. The challenge for AI
alignment is therefore not only to develop capable and aligned
individual agents, but to design and adapt the institutions through
which they cooperate with one another and increasingly participate
alongside humans in collective life, ensuring that their capabilities
remain reliable, accountable and directed toward human purposes.
