###### Abstract

AI scientists are expanding automated research across scientific
domains, yet their ability to turn the scientific record into a
foundation for autonomous discovery remains elusive. Here we uncover a
fundamental barrier to this transition and demonstrate a route to
overcome it in computational quantum science, where advances shape both
what we can learn from complex quantum systems and what quantum
computing can ultimately deliver. To identify this barrier, we introduce
OmniQ, an expert-audited virtual research environment that converts
published studies into executable reproduction tasks. Across 59 papers
spanning classical simulation, quantum algorithms and quantum-property
characterization, we systematically evaluate five agentic systems
powered by large language models and six third-party agent harnesses.
Our findings reveal that current AI scientists often fail to reproduce
published results, exposing a fundamental mismatch between what
scientific papers communicate and what autonomous agents require. By
contrast, human expert intervention can turn these failures into
successful reproductions, showing that the missing information can be
identified and converted into reusable research experience. Guided by
this observation, we develop Residual Agents (RA), a quantum-tailored
self-improving AI scientist that distils externally verified workflow
repairs into such experience. RA substantially improves research
performance under fixed base models and limited adaptation budgets. For
example, RA raises DeepSeek V4.1 Flash performance from 68% to 85%,
matching Claude Code with Opus 5 at a third of its cost despite using a
weaker base model. We integrate all reproduced results into an
extensible platform built on the model context protocol, making verified
research workflows directly accessible to AI scientists and allowing new
scientific papers to be continuously incorporated. These results
establish a new paradigm for computational quantum research, where
published results become reusable scientific capabilities that support
autonomous discovery.

## Introduction

Computational quantum science develops algorithms to understand and
exploit complex quantum systems \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2),
[3](#bib.bib3)\], spanning classical simulation \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\], quantum algorithms \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9)\], and property characterization of
quantum systems \[[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11),
[12](#bib.bib12)\]. Despite this promise, algorithm design across all
three directions is becoming increasingly difficult as quantum systems
and hardware continue to scale. For classical simulation, pushing the
limits of classical tractability while establishing reliable baselines
for practical utility increasingly relies on sophisticated methods such
as tensor networks \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14),
[15](#bib.bib15)\] and Pauli-propagation techniques \[[16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20)\]. For both near-term and fault-tolerant quantum
algorithms, achieving computational advantage remains challenging under
realistic resource constraints, requiring careful co-design of
algorithms and hardware capabilities \[[21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. For quantum
property characterization, extracting informative properties from
limited measurements becomes difficult as the Hilbert space grows
exponentially, motivating efficient measurement and inference
protocols \[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26), [27](#bib.bib27),
[28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30)\]. As a result,
these challenges create an urgent need for more autonomous approaches to
computational quantum science.

Recent breakthroughs in AI scientists offer a potential route towards
autonomous computational quantum science \[[31](#bib.bib31),
[32](#bib.bib32), [33](#bib.bib33)\]. By integrating frontier large
language models (LLMs) with planning, tool use and iterative feedback
\[[34](#bib.bib34), [35](#bib.bib35), [36](#bib.bib36),
[37](#bib.bib37)\], these systems have begun to automate algorithm
discovery in mathematics \[[38](#bib.bib38), [39](#bib.bib39),
[40](#bib.bib40)\] and software engineering \[[41](#bib.bib41),
[42](#bib.bib42), [43](#bib.bib43), [44](#bib.bib44)\]. This trend is
also extending to other disciplines, such as biology \[[45](#bib.bib45),
[46](#bib.bib46), [47](#bib.bib47)\], chemistry \[[48](#bib.bib48),
[49](#bib.bib49), [50](#bib.bib50)\] and materials
science \[[51](#bib.bib51)\]. All of these advances point to a shift
from AI-assisted research towards autonomous algorithm discovery.
Computational quantum science is a natural next arena because progress
in this field may reshape both what we can learn from quantum systems
and what quantum computing can ultimately deliver. In this context,
early efforts have demonstrated AI-driven discovery of quantum
error-correcting codes \[[52](#bib.bib52), [53](#bib.bib53),
[54](#bib.bib54)\], automated design of quantum
circuits \[[55](#bib.bib55), [56](#bib.bib56), [57](#bib.bib57)\] and
algorithmic search for quantum-chemistry
applications \[[58](#bib.bib58), [59](#bib.bib59), [60](#bib.bib60),
[61](#bib.bib61)\]. However, the progress remains fragmented and largely
confined to narrowly defined settings, such as a single subproblem, a
human-tailored workflow or a search space strongly constrained by prior
knowledge. This raises a central question:

In this study, we address this question by starting from paper-to-result
reproduction, where verifiable outcomes allow AI scientists to
accumulate scientific experience before moving to open-ended research.
To this end, we introduce OmniQ, an expert-audited virtual research
environment that transforms published studies in computational quantum
science into executable reproduction tasks. More precisely, given a full
paper and a target result, an AI scientist reconstructs the underlying
method, implements and executes it under a comparable computational
budget, and attempts to reproduce the reported outcome. The workflow is
evaluated for consistency with the reported method and outcome, with
expert intervention reserved for ambiguous or inconsistent cases. In
doing so, OmniQ turns paper-to-result reproduction into a controlled and
diagnosable research setting.

Using OmniQ, we systematically evaluate five LLM-powered AI scientists
and six third-party agent harnesses across 60 papers spanning
computational quantum science. Different from recent paper-to-agent
approaches \[[62](#bib.bib62)\] that transform papers, code and data
into executable agentic systems, here we consider a more direct
reproduction setting in computational quantum science. That is, the
agent must reconstruct the workflow underlying a reported result.
Experimental results show that current AI scientists frequently fail at
this task. Moreover, human expert review reveals a deeper problem. That
is, in 18 studies, the paper description, released code and reported
results are inconsistent. Such cases obscure the boundary between agent
failure and limitations of the scientific record, posing a barrier to
reliable autonomous discovery in computational quantum science.

After separating source-side inconsistencies from genuine agent
failures, we find that human expert intervention can often turn failed
attempts into successful reproductions, revealing domain knowledge that
can be learned and reused. Building on this observation, we develop
Residual Agents (RA), an efficient self-improving AI scientist that
distils externally verified workflow repairs into reusable quantum
research experience. Unlike generic evolution mechanisms, RA accumulates
domain-specific lessons from successful repairs and applies them to
subsequent research. Across base LLMs, RA substantially improves
performance under a limited adaptation budget, with gains that
generalize to unseen tasks and transfer across models. We further
integrate all reproduced results into an extensible platform built on
the model context protocol, enabling AI scientists and the broader
research community to directly access, reuse, and extend verified
research workflows. Collectively, these advances recast paper-to-result
reproduction from a one-off translation problem into a foundation for
reusable scientific capabilities and continual learning, opening a path
towards autonomous discovery in computational quantum science and
potentially beyond.

## Results

### Failures of paper-to-result reproduction

Autonomous computational quantum science envisions AI scientists that
can independently understand and develop computational methods for
quantum systems. Towards this goal, a crucial step is reproduction,
which can accelerate the validation and reuse of existing methods while
reducing the expert effort required to reconstruct complex computational
workflows. We therefore ask whether an AI scientist can reconstruct the
underlying workflow from the scientific record and faithfully reproduce
a reported result. We refer to this setting as paper-to-result
reproduction.

We study paper-to-result reproduction using OmniQ, a virtual research
environment that turns published computational quantum studies into
executable and diagnosable reproduction tasks. As illustrated in
Fig. [1](#Sx1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
OmniQ is employed to curate 60 studies spanning classical simulation,
quantum algorithms and quantum-property characterization. The studies
are selected to balance scientific impact, publication in leading
venues, methodological diversity, and reproducibility under realistic
computational budgets, with approximately even coverage across the three
research directions. More details are provided in Supplementary
Information (SI) A and B.

To faithfully assess reproduction quality, we classify reproduction
quality into three levels according to how well the central scientific
claim is preserved through reconstruction. Specifically, Level I
captures missing methodological details that can be recovered from the
released code or expert interpretation. Level II captures localized
errors in the paper that can be resolved from the code, figures or
broader scientific context, but otherwise lead to reproduction
discrepancies. Level III captures fundamental inconsistencies among the
context, figures and released code that cannot be resolved through
expert inspection, leaving the target result unreproducible from the
available scientific record.

The reproduction experiment in OmniQ assigns eight agent configurations
to each study, each tasked with independently reproducing a designated
result under a computational budget comparable to that of the original
work. Across representative frontier models and agentic systems, the
reconstructed workflows and reported results are cross-checked across
agents, compared against available source code, and independently
verified by domain experts (See SI B). As shown in
Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
this procedure identifies reproducibility issues in 18 of the 60
studies. Below, we examine three representative studies from each
reproduction level to illustrate the distinct failure modes.

Level I is exemplified by Paper #46 \[[63](#bib.bib63)\] on quantum
error mitigation \[[64](#bib.bib64)\], exploring whether learning-based
mitigation can recover accurate observable expectation values beyond the
training regime, enabling extrapolation to deeper quantum circuits
without the full overhead of fault-tolerant error
correction \[[65](#bib.bib65)\]. Its Figure 3 directly probes this claim
by tracking prediction errors across Trotter steps within and beyond the
training region for Hamiltonian simulation tasks. The reproduction with
OmniQ reveals a localized but consequential inconsistency. That is, the
paper states that only one feature is considered when selecting the best
split of the machine-learning model, whereas the released code uses all
available features at each split (i.e., max_features=1.0). As shown in
the third panel of
Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")b,
following the written description causes the reproduced model to lose
its reported advantage in the extrapolation regime. In contrast, as
shown in the bottom panel, following the released code resolves this
discrepancy and recovers the reported extrapolation advantage.

Level II is exemplified by Paper #42 \[[66](#bib.bib66)\] on
quantum-property characterization, where efficient learning methods are
developed to identify topological order in many-body quantum systems
without prior phase labels. Its Figures 3b and 3d quantify the
robustness of the proposed fidelity and entanglement kernels under
increasingly deep two-qubit random circuits using the silhouette
coefficient, a standard metric of clustering quality
\[[67](#bib.bib67)\]. Reproduction with OmniQ fails to recover the
reported quantitative values. As shown in the third panel of
Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")c,
the silhouette coefficient, which measures clustering quality from
$`-1`$ (poor) to $`1`$ (well separated), decreases from approximately
$`0.59`$ in the paper to $`0.29`$ in the reproduction at circuit depth
four. Expert inspection reveals that the experimental procedure is
incompletely specified, as recovering the reported value requires an
additional two-step procedure that is not described in the paper.
Moreover, as shown in the last panel, applying the procedure described
in the paper to the authors’ own data yields a silhouette coefficient of
only $`0.24`$. After expert editing focuses the task on the reported
scientific conclusion instead of unrecoverable numerical values, RA
reproduces the reported degradation of the fidelity kernel and the
consistent advantage of the entanglement kernel.

Level III is represented by Paper #60 \[[68](#bib.bib68)\] on quantum
machine learning, which studies whether quantum kernels remain
efficiently distinguishable as the number of qubits increases. This
question is closely tied to whether quantum kernels can ultimately
support quantum advantage in learning \[[69](#bib.bib69)\]. The
scientific paper claims that highly expressive data encodings can cause
quantum kernels to concentrate exponentially, making their estimation
increasingly costly and ultimately limiting their usefulness for
learning. Its Figure 6b provides numerical evidence for this claim by
plotting the variance of the projected quantum kernel
$`\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{PQ}}`$ as a function of qubit number $`n`$ and
circuit depth $`L`$. However, reproduction with OmniQ reveals a
fundamental inconsistency: although the figure is described as reporting
$`\mathrm{Var}[\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{PQ}}]`$, the released code instead
evaluates the variance of the distance $`C`$ entering the kernel
$`\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{PQ}}=\exp(-\gamma C)`$. As shown in the third
panel of
Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")d,
the discrepancy is already evident at $`n=2`$, where the reported value
reaches $`0.49`$, exceeding the theoretical upper bound of $`0.25`$ on
the variance of a kernel taking values in $`[0,1]`$. A separate
discrepancy concerns the generation of the data underlying the figure.
Even when $`\mathrm{Var}[C]`$ was used as the target, rerunning the
released code fails to recover the published values. For the case of
$`n=12`$ qubits, the published variances at both $`L=2`$ and $`L=4`$ are
more than an order of magnitude larger than the recalculated values, as
shown in the last panel. Expert inspection identified the statistical
error but did not establish the configuration used to generate the
archived data. Unlike Levels I and II, the discrepancy between the
released code and the published figure remains unresolved, preventing
faithful reproduction of the target result from the available scientific
record.

### Performance across frontier AI scientists

We next explore how existing AI scientists perform on a cleaned
computational quantum science dataset. Following expert review,
resolvable Level I and II issues are corrected, whereas the unresolved
Level III case (i.e., Paper \#60) is excluded, yielding a consistent
dataset of 59 papers for agent evaluation. Using this cleaned dataset
and OmniQ, we systematically evaluate leading base models from the GPT,
Claude, DeepSeek, Kimi and Grok families \[[70](#bib.bib70),
[71](#bib.bib71), [72](#bib.bib72), [73](#bib.bib73),
[74](#bib.bib74)\]. We also consider first-party agentic systems,
including Codex, Claude Code, Kimi Code, Grok Build and DeepSeek Harness
(DSH), together with six widely used third-party agent harnesses, i.e.,
InternAgent, DeepCode, mini-swe-agent, live-swe-agent, Huxley–Gödel
Machine (HGM) and Meta-Harness \[[75](#bib.bib75), [76](#bib.bib76),
[77](#bib.bib77), [78](#bib.bib78), [79](#bib.bib79),
[80](#bib.bib80)\]. A reproduction task is counted as successful only
when the reconstructed workflow satisfies the strict reproduction
criterion defined above.

Figure [3](#Sx2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Failures of paper-to-result reproduction ‣ Results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")
summarizes reproduction success together with inference cost per task
and wall-clock time across different agents and harness mechanisms. A
first observation is that performance varies markedly across frontier
agentic systems. In particular, Claude Code with Opus 5.5 achieves the
highest reproduction rate, recovering approximately $`93\%`$ of the
computational quantum science dataset. Besides, Codex paired with
several frontier base models exceeds $`70\%`$ reproduction success. At
the other end of the spectrum, Codex with GPT-6-luna reaches only about
$`42\%`$, and Meta-Harness with DeepSeek performs worst at $`19\%`$.
These results indicate that once the scientific record is
self-consistent, frontier AI scientists already possess the capability
for autonomous computational quantum science.

The achieved results further reveal two unexpected findings. First,
reproduction success does not increase monotonically with inference
cost. As shown in
Fig. [3](#Sx2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Failures of paper-to-result reproduction ‣ Results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
Claude Code with Sonnet 5.5, Kimi Code with K3, and several Codex-based
systems use larger inference budgets than Claude Code with Opus 5.5 yet
perform worse. Meanwhile, DSH with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash achieves
approximately $`68\%`$ success at the lowest cost, outperforming every
other system below \$7 per task and several AI scientists costing an
order of magnitude more. This observation suggests that higher inference
spending often translates into more deliberation but not more execution,
a “thinking more but doing less” behavior that echoes recent
observations of overthinking in agentic reasoning \[[81](#bib.bib81)\].
Collectively, this decoupling identifies the allocation of inference
budgets between reasoning and scientific execution as a central
determinant of autonomous computational science.

A second unexpected finding is that existing agent harnesses do not
necessarily improve reproduction and can even degrade performance. As
illustrated in
Fig. [3](#Sx2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Failures of paper-to-result reproduction ‣ Results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
third-party harnesses span a wide performance range, from about $`19\%`$
for Meta-Harness with DeepSeek to $`48\%`$ for mini-swe-agent with
DeepSeek, all substantially below the strongest first-party systems.
Even on the same base LLM model, none of the six exceeds the vendor’s
own harness. With DeepSeek V4 Pro, their reproduction success ranges
from $`19\%`$ to $`48\%`$, compared with $`49\%`$ for DSH. Failure
analysis reveals common behaviors, i.e., agents may declare success
without validating the reproduced result against the paper, replace the
required computation with a cheaper surrogate, or silently reduce the
problem size. Therefore, harness mechanisms tuned on other tasks thus do
not transfer to computational quantum science \[[82](#bib.bib82),
[83](#bib.bib83)\], which calls for scientific experience grounded in
the computational quantum science domain.

### RA: Self-improving AI scientists

The performance gap between proprietary and open-source AI scientists in
Fig. [3](#Sx2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Failures of paper-to-result reproduction ‣ Results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")
underscores a dilemma. On the one hand, leading proprietary systems
achieve strong reproduction performance, but their closed
implementations limit the transparency and adaptability of scientific
workflows. Conversely, existing open-source alternatives struggle to
reproduce published results reliably, limiting their utility for
computational quantum science.

To bridge this gap, we develop Residual Agents (RA), an open-source,
self-improving AI scientist for autonomous computational quantum science
that learns from verified failures to improve future reproductions. As
shown in
Fig. [1](#Sx1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Introduction ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")b,
RA splits a reproduction task into sub-plans and coordinates three roles
powered by the same base LLM. Specifically, a planner develops a
resource-bounded plan, executors carry out each sub-plan, and a critic
validates the results against the paper. When a step fails, it is
repaired or replanned based on the critic’s assessment. Beyond resolving
individual failures, RA identifies the residual between failed
executions and their verified corrections, distilling these differences
into generalizable failure signatures stored in a residual memory
alongside the evolving memories of the planner and critic. As such,
adaptation is confined to the experience layer and driven only by
verified evidence. To ensure reliable evolution, a human expert reviews
each proposed update before it is incorporated, while the underlying
model, executors, and orchestration remain fixed. Further details and
its associated research platform are provided in SI C and SI D,
respectively.

We next evaluate RA across all 59 reproduction tasks. During
development, RA uses only the five most challenging tasks, whose
verified failures form its experience. The remaining 54 tasks are unseen
during development and evaluate how well this experience generalizes.
For evaluation, RA uses DeepSeek V4.1 Flash with DSH as the executor and
is given 24 hours per task. We compare it directly with DSH using the
same base model and computational budget, with all systems evaluated
under the same criteria as in the preceding reproduction experiments.

Figure [3](#Sx2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Failures of paper-to-result reproduction ‣ Results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")
compares RA with existing frontier AI scientists, with further details
provided in SI E. In particular, RA improves the reproduction success of
DeepSeek V4.1 Flash from $`68\%`$ to approximately $`85\%`$, at an
inference cost of only \$5.6 per task. This performance matches Claude
Code with Opus 5 and Codex with GPT-5.6-sol API, which achieve
approximately $`85\%`$ success at \$16 and \$35 per task, respectively.
Although the best-performing system still achieves a higher success
rate, RA reaches competitive performance at substantially lower
inference cost.

The internal trajectories further reveal how RA improves reproduction.
Across the 59 tasks, the critic rejects more than half of intermediate
results, triggering repair or replanning. Notably, $`90\%`$ of
successful tasks require at least one correction, including all 13 tasks
recovered beyond DSH. These results show that RA gains its advantage
primarily by identifying and correcting failures. More experimental
results are deferred to SI E.

## Discussion

Our study establishes paper-to-result reproduction as a foundation for
developing autonomous AI scientists. Supported by OmniQ, we show that
reliable reproduction in computational quantum science depends jointly
on the capabilities of AI scientists and the consistency of the
scientific record. We further develop RA, an open-source AI scientist
that converts verified reproduction failures into reusable scientific
experience, enabling improvement without modifying the underlying model.
By making verified reproduction workflows accessible through an
extensible research platform, we further lay the groundwork for a
community-driven ecosystem of reusable scientific capabilities.

Three important directions remain for future research. The first is to
extend OmniQ towards fully autonomous paper-to-result reproduction
across scientific disciplines. Recent studies have explored AI-driven
scientific reproduction in diverse domains, including
Paper2Agent \[[62](#bib.bib62)\] for computational biology,
AutoExperiment \[[84](#bib.bib84)\] for machine learning, and
SocSci-Repro-Bench \[[85](#bib.bib85)\] for social sciences. However,
our study reveals a fundamental challenge. That is, inconsistencies in
the scientific record can make faithful reproduction itself ambiguous,
requiring expert intervention to resolve. In this regard, integrating
OmniQ and RA with these domain-specific approaches could enable more
reliable and autonomous scientific reproduction across disciplines.

A second direction is to further improve the performance of
paper-to-result reproduction in computational quantum science. Although
RA outperforms several more expensive models at lower cost, achieving
fully reliable scientific reproduction with greater efficiency remains
an open challenge. Toward this goal, there are three potential
solutions. First, adaptive allocation of test-time computation could
improve reproduction performance by directing greater computational
resources toward challenging tasks \[[86](#bib.bib86),
[87](#bib.bib87)\]. Second, integrating specialized scientific tools and
reusable implementations of previously reproduced algorithms could
improve the accuracy and reliability of complex scientific
reproductions \[[88](#bib.bib88)\]. Third, optimizing the
performance–cost trade-off through adaptive model selection and
efficient agent orchestration could further reduce the cost of
scientific reproduction without compromising
reliability \[[89](#bib.bib89)\].

The last direction is to extend RA beyond scientific reproduction
towards autonomous algorithm discovery in computational quantum science.
Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of AI scientists to
discover more efficient quantum circuits through iterative search and
independent verification \[[90](#bib.bib90), [91](#bib.bib91),
[92](#bib.bib92)\]. Building on these advances, RA could strengthen
autonomous discovery by first establishing reliable computational
baselines and then learning from the differences between unsuccessful
candidates and verified improvements. By accumulating these insights as
reusable experience, RA could guide subsequent algorithm design and
optimization beyond individual search tasks. Extending its
self-improvement mechanism from verified reproduction repairs to
verified algorithmic advances offers a promising route towards more
reliable and efficient autonomous quantum algorithm discovery.

### A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems

Classical simulation uses classical resources to predict quantum
properties and assess potential quantum computational advantages. Here
we explain how to estimate observable expectation values, which can be
performed either by explicitly simulating quantum states or through
methods that directly estimate the target quantities. Mathematically,
given classical descriptions of an $`N`$-qubit initial state
$`\rho_{0}`$, a quantum evolution channel $`\mathcal{U}`$, and an
observable $`O`$, the goal is to estimate the expectation value

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       f_{O}(\rho)=\text{Tr}\left[\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})O\right],
       ```                                                        |     | (1) |

where $`\rho=\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})`$ denotes the evolved quantum state.
For unitary dynamics described by a unitary operator $`U`$, the
corresponding channel takes the form
$`\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})=U\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}`$.

A central challenge in classical simulation is the exponential growth of
the Hilbert space with the number of qubits $`N`$, making general
quantum-state simulation computationally expensive. To address this
challenge, various classical simulation algorithms have been developed.
In what follows, we introduce six representative classes of classical
simulation methods: tensor networks, neural quantum states, Pauli
propagation, quantum Monte Carlo methods, Lindbladian simulation for
open quantum systems, and free-fermion simulation. For each class, we
summarize its formulation, computational principles, and conditions for
efficient simulation.

Tensor networks. Tensor-network methods simulate quantum dynamics by
representing the evolving quantum state as a network of interconnected
tensors \[[93](#bib.bib93), [94](#bib.bib94), [95](#bib.bib95)\].
Specifically, consider a decomposition of the evolution channel into
elementary channels
$`\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{U}_{1}`$. At each
stage of the evolution, the $`N`$-qubit state, described by a density
matrix
$`\rho=\sum_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\rho_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}|\bm{i}\rangle\langle\bm{j}|`$,
is approximated by a tensor network on a graph $`G=(V,E)`$ with
$`|V|=N`$, i.e.,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\rho_{\bm{i}\bm{j}}\approx\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{\chi_{1}}\cdots\sum_{\alpha_{|E|}=1}^{\chi_{|E|}}\prod_{v\in V}A^{[v]}_{\bm{i}_{v},\bm{j}_{v},\{\alpha_{e}:e\ni v\}},
``` |  |

where $`\bm{i}=(\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{N})`$ and
$`\bm{j}=(\bm{j}_{1},\ldots,\bm{j}_{N})`$ are computational-basis
indices, $`A^{[v]}`$ denotes the local tensor at vertex $`v`$, and each
edge $`e\in E`$ carries a bond index $`\alpha_{e}`$ of dimension
$`\chi_{e}`$. The state at the next stage, following the application of
an $`m`$-qubit channel $`\mathcal{U}_{\ell}`$ to the qubit set
$`S_{\ell}`$, can then be approximated by applying
$`\mathcal{U}_{\ell}`$ to the contracted tensor $`\mathcal{T}_{\ell}`$
of the corresponding subgraph and decomposing it back into the original
network structure with truncated bond dimensions, i.e.,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\{A^{[v]}\}_{v\in S_{\ell}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{contraction}}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{U}_{\ell}}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{decomposition+truncation}}\{\widetilde{A}^{[v]}\}_{v\in S_{\ell}}.
``` |  |

The efficiency of tensor-network simulation depends on both the
entanglement structure of the quantum state and the geometry of the
underlying network $`G`$. States with area-law entanglement often admit
compact representations with small bond dimensions $`{\chi_{e}}`$,
whereas volume-law entanglement can require exponentially large bond
dimensions. Consequently, tensor-network methods are particularly
effective for weakly entangled systems, enabling efficient exact
simulation in certain structured cases and accurate approximate
simulation more broadly through tensor contraction. Representative
methods include matrix product states, matrix product operators, and
projected entangled-pair states.

Neural quantum states. According to
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
a neural quantum state (NQS) simulates quantum dynamics by approximating
the evolving quantum state $`\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})`$. Different from
tensor networks, NQS methods represent the wavefunction amplitudes using
a parameterized neural network \[[96](#bib.bib96), [97](#bib.bib97)\].
For pure-state dynamics with
$`\rho_{0}=|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|`$ and
$`\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|`$, the goal is to
approximate $`|\psi\rangle`$ with high fidelity using an NQS
$`|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle`$ of the form

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{\bm{i}}|g_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{i})|^{2}}}\sum_{\bm{i}}g_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{i})|\bm{i}\rangle,
``` |  |

where $`g_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)`$ is a complex-valued neural network that
outputs an unnormalized amplitude for each configuration $`\bm{i}`$.

A common strategy for simulating quantum dynamics with neural quantum
states (NQS) is to sequentially optimize the network parameters after
each elementary unitary evolution. Starting from an initial NQS
$`|\psi_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\rangle=|\psi_{0}\rangle`$, the parameters
$`\bm{\theta}_{\ell}`$ at step $`\ell`$ are obtained by minimizing the
infidelity between the normalized variational state
$`|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle`$ and the target state
$`U_{\ell}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}_{\ell-1}}\rangle`$, i.e.,
$`\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(\bm{\theta})=1-|\langle\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|U_{\ell}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}_{\ell-1}}\rangle|^{2}`$
with
$`\bm{\theta}_{\ell}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(\bm{\theta})`$.
To enable efficient optimization, the infidelity gradient can be
expressed in terms of expectations over the Born distribution
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{i})=|\langle\bm{i}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle|^{2}`$
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial\mathcal{I}_{k}}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{a}}=-2\operatorname{Re}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{i}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{i}|U_{\ell}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}_{\ell-1}}\rangle}{\langle\bm{i}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle}\right]^{*}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{i}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{i}|U_{\ell}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}_{\ell-1}}\rangle}{\langle\bm{i}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle}\left(O_{a}^{*}(\bm{i})-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\bm{\theta}}}[O_{a}^{*}]\right)\right]\right],
``` |  | (2) |

where
$`O_{a}(\bm{i})=\partial_{\bm{\theta}_{a}}\log g_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{i})`$
is the logarithmic derivative of the neural-network amplitude.

The infidelity gradient in
Eq. ([2](#A1.E2 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"))
can be estimated using Monte Carlo sampling, allowing NQS parameters to
be optimized without explicitly representing the full quantum state.
However, gradient estimation can suffer from numerical instability and
sampling inefficiency, making optimization
challenging \[[98](#bib.bib98)\]. Improved neural-network architectures,
sampling strategies, and gradient estimators have been developed to
address these limitations \[[99](#bib.bib99)\], enabling NQS to simulate
increasingly complex quantum states and dynamics.

Pauli propagation. Pauli propagation simulates quantum dynamics by
evolving observables in the Heisenberg picture and is particularly
efficient for circuits with limited non-Clifford
resources \[[100](#bib.bib100), [101](#bib.bib101)\]. Following
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
for an $`N`$-qubit channel $`\mathcal{U}`$ and an observable $`O`$, the
expectation value is given by
$`f_{O}(\rho_{0})=\text{Tr}[O\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})]=\text{Tr}[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(O)\rho_{0}]`$,
where
$`\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{U}_{1}^{\dagger}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{U}_{L}^{\dagger}`$
is the adjoint channel. The evolved observable is represented as a Pauli
expansion, i.e.,

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\bm{a}\in A}c_{\bm{a}}P_{\bm{a}},
       ```                                                               |     |

where $`A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2N}`$ indexes the nonzero terms,
$`P_{\bm{a}}\in\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes N}`$ denotes a Pauli
string, and $`c_{\bm{a}}`$ is its coefficient. The expectation value is
then evaluated as
$`f_{O}(\rho_{0})=\sum_{\bm{a}\in A}c_{\bm{a}}\text{Tr}(P_{\bm{a}}\rho_{0})`$.
As a result, the computational efficiency depends on the growth of the
Pauli expansion during backward propagation.

To reduce this computational cost, approximate Pauli-propagation
algorithms truncate the Pauli expansion during the propagation process
by retaining nontrivial Pauli strings \[[101](#bib.bib101)\]. Denote the
retained Pauli strings by $`A^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2N}`$.
The resulting approximation of the evolved observable takes the form
$`\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\bm{a}\in A^{\prime}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{a}}P_{\bm{a}}`$,
and the corresponding estimate of $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ is given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{f}_{O}(\rho)=\text{Tr}[\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}(O)\rho_{0}]=\sum_{\bm{a}\in A^{\prime}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{a}}\text{Tr}(P_{\bm{a}}\rho_{0}),
``` |  |

where $`\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{a}}`$ denotes the coefficient obtained from
the truncated propagation procedure. An effective Pauli-propagation
algorithm should satisfy $`|f_{O}(\rho)-\hat{f}_{O}(\rho)|\leq\epsilon`$
for a prescribed accuracy $`\epsilon`$, while keeping the number of
retained Pauli terms and the computational cost manageable. Typical
approaches in this regime include Pauli-weight truncation,
coefficient-based pruning, and Monte Carlo sampling of Pauli
paths \[[102](#bib.bib102), [101](#bib.bib101), [100](#bib.bib100)\].

Quantum Monte Carlo methods. Quantum Monte Carlo methods directly
estimate observable expectations $`\text{Tr}[O\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})]`$
through stochastic sampling of suitable representations of quantum
systems \[[103](#bib.bib103), [104](#bib.bib104), [105](#bib.bib105),
[106](#bib.bib106)\]. Depending on the formulation, these methods can
address equilibrium properties, quantum dynamics, and quantum-circuit
simulations, while their efficiency may be limited by sign or phase
problems. As a representative example, consider the estimation of
thermal expectation values for a Hamiltonian $`H`$ at inverse
temperature $`\beta`$, given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{Tr}[O\mathcal{U}_{\beta}(\rho_{0})]=\frac{\text{Tr}(Oe^{-\beta H})}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\frac{\langle\bm{i}|e^{-\beta H}|\bm{i}\rangle}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})}\frac{\langle\bm{i}|Oe^{-\beta H}|\bm{i}\rangle}{\langle\bm{i}|e^{-\beta H}|\bm{i}\rangle},
``` |  |

where
$`\mathcal{U}_{\beta}(\rho_{0})=\text{Tr}(\rho_{0})e^{-\beta H}/\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})`$
refers to a thermal-state preparation channel.

To exploit Hamiltonian locality, imaginary-time QMC decomposes
$`e^{-\beta H}=(e^{-\Delta\tau H})^{L}`$, where $`\Delta\tau=\beta/L`$,
and inserts computational-basis resolutions of the identity between
successive time slices. This yields a path-integral representation of
the thermal expectation value
$`\langle O\rangle_{\beta}=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}p(\mathcal{C})O_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{C})`$,
where $`\mathcal{C}=(\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{L})`$ is a configuration
path with $`\bm{i}_{L+1}=\bm{i}_{1}`$, and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(\mathcal{C})=\frac{w(\mathcal{C})}{\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}w(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})},\quad w(\mathcal{C})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\langle\bm{i}_{\ell+1}|e^{-\Delta\tau H}|\bm{i}_{\ell}\rangle,\quad O_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{\langle\bm{i}_{2}|e^{-\Delta\tau H}O|\bm{i}_{1}\rangle}{\langle\bm{i}_{2}|e^{-\Delta\tau H}|\bm{i}_{1}\rangle}.
``` |  |

When $`w(\mathcal{C})\geq 0`$, the normalized weights define a sampling
distribution, allowing the expectation value to be estimated without
explicitly summing over exponentially many paths, i.e.,
$`\langle O\rangle_{\beta}\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}O_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})`$
with $`\mathcal{C}^{(m)}\sim p`$.

Lindbladian simulation for open quantum systems. Lindbladian simulation
provides a framework for simulating the dynamics of an open quantum
system interacting with an environment without explicitly representing
the environmental degrees of freedom \[[107](#bib.bib107)\]. Under the
assumption of time-homogeneous Markovian dynamics, the evolution channel
over time $`T`$ takes the Lindblad form
$`\mathcal{U}=e^{T\mathcal{L}}`$, where the Lindblad generator
$`\mathcal{L}`$ is defined by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})=-\imath[H,\rho_{t}]+\sum_{\mu}\left(L_{\mu}\rho_{t}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu},\rho_{t}\}\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`H`$ is the system Hamiltonian, $`\rho_{t}`$ is the system
density matrix at time $`t\leq T`$, $`L_{\mu}`$ are the jump operators
describing dissipative processes, and $`[A,B]=AB-BA`$ and
$`\{A,B\}=AB+BA`$ denote the commutator and anticommutator,
respectively.

A standard approach to evaluating $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ in
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"))
under Lindblad dynamics is the quantum-trajectory
method \[[108](#bib.bib108), [109](#bib.bib109)\], which unravels the
mixed-state evolution into an ensemble of stochastic pure-state
trajectories, i.e.,
$`\rho_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim p}[|\psi_{t}^{(\xi)}\rangle\langle\psi_{t}^{(\xi)}|]`$.
Each trajectory $`|\psi_{t}^{(\xi)}\rangle`$ undergoes non-unitary
evolution governed by the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
$`H_{\mathrm{eff}}=H-\frac{\imath}{2}\sum_{\mu}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}`$,
derived from the Lindblad generator in
Eq. ([3](#A1.E3 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
interrupted by stochastic quantum jumps associated with the operators
$`L_{\mu}`$. The observable expectation is then estimated by averaging
over $`M`$ independently sampled trajectories:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{Tr}(O\rho_{T})\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\langle\psi_{T}^{(m)}|O|\psi_{T}^{(m)}\rangle.
``` |  |

Free-fermion simulation. Free-fermion methods enable efficient classical
simulation of fermionic Gaussian states evolving under quadratic
Hamiltonians, without explicitly representing the exponentially large
quantum state \[[110](#bib.bib110), [111](#bib.bib111)\]. To evaluate
the observable expectation $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ in
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
we first introduce suitable representations of the initial state, its
evolution, and the target observable.

For an $`N`$-mode fermionic system, define the Majorana operators
$`\gamma_{2j-1}=\bm{a}_{j}+\bm{a}_{j}^{\dagger}`$ and
$`\gamma_{2j}=-\imath(\bm{a}_{j}-\bm{a}_{j}^{\dagger})`$, where
$`\bm{a}_{j}^{\dagger}`$ and $`\bm{a}_{j}`$ denote fermionic creation
and annihilation operators, respectively. A full-rank fermionic Gaussian
state can be expressed as $`\rho_{0}=e^{-K_{0}}/\text{Tr}(e^{-K_{0}})`$,
where $`K_{0}=(\imath/4)\bm{\gamma}^{\top}B_{0}\bm{\gamma}`$ and
$`B_{0}`$ is a real antisymmetric $`2N\times 2N`$ matrix. Consider the
evolution
$`\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{U}_{1}`$, where
each unitary channel is generated by a quadratic Hamiltonian
$`H_{\ell}=(\imath/4)\bm{\gamma}^{\top}M_{\ell}\bm{\gamma}`$, with
$`M_{\ell}`$ real and antisymmetric. The evolved state remains Gaussian,
and its coefficient matrix is updated as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
B_{\ell}=Q_{\ell}B_{\ell-1}Q_{\ell}^{\top}\qquad\text{with}~Q_{\ell}=e^{M_{\ell}x_{\ell}},
``` |  |

where $`x_{\ell}`$ denotes the evolution time at step $`\ell`$. The
final state $`\rho=\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})`$ is thus characterized by
$`B_{L}`$, from which its covariance matrix
$`\Gamma_{ab}=\frac{\imath}{2}\text{Tr}(\rho[\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b}])`$
can be obtained as $`\Gamma=-\tan(B_{L}/2)`$. In addition, we consider
Hermitian observables of the form
$`O=\imath^{k}\gamma_{a_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{a_{2k}}`$, where
$`a_{1},\ldots,a_{2k}`$ denote distinct Majorana indices.

With the above notations, the expectation value $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ under
Free-fermion system can then be efficiently evaluated using Wick’s
theorem \[[112](#bib.bib112)\]. Mathematically, we have

|     |                                      |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       f_{O}(\rho)=\mathsf{Pf}(\Gamma_{A}),
       ```                                   |     |

where $`\mathsf{Pf}(\cdot)`$ denotes the Pfaffian of an antisymmetric
matrix, and $`\Gamma_{A}`$ is the $`2k\times 2k`$ submatrix of
$`\Gamma`$ obtained by selecting the rows and columns indexed by
$`A=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{2k})`$, as specified by the Majorana operators
appearing in $`O`$.

### A.2 Quantum algorithms

Quantum algorithms address computational problems by processing
information encoded in quantum states and extracting results through
measurement. We focus on five representative categories spanning both
fault-tolerant and near-term quantum computing: non-variational quantum
simulation, quantum phase estimation, variational quantum simulation,
and quantum machine learning, quantum-enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo.
The diversity of computational approaches and implementation strategies
within these categories further complicates autonomous reproduction,
requiring AI scientists to reconstruct the specific methods adopted in
individual studies. For each category, we introduce the problem
formulation and basic workflows of representative algorithms.

Non-variational quantum simulation. Quantum simulation aims to
understand the properties and dynamics of quantum systems, whose
exponentially growing state spaces often make classical computation
intractable. Non-variational approaches address this challenge by
directly constructing quantum operations from the system Hamiltonian to
simulate time evolution or prepare ground states \[[113](#bib.bib113)\].
Mathematically, for a Hamiltonian $`H(t)`$ and an initial state
$`|\psi(0)\rangle`$, the evolving state obeys the Schrödinger equation

|     |                                                          |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \partial_{t}|\psi(t)\rangle=-\imath H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle.
       ```                                                       |     | (4) |

Below, we discuss this approach for time-independent and time-dependent
Hamiltonians, respectively.

For time-independent $`H`$ in
Eq. ([4](#A1.E4 "In A.2 Quantum algorithms ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
its solution is $`|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-\imath Ht}|\psi(0)\rangle`$.
Trotterization approximates this evolution by composing operations
generated by individual Hamiltonian terms \[[114](#bib.bib114)\]. Let
the explored Hamiltonian be $`H=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}h_{\ell}`$, where each
Hermitian term $`h_{\ell}`$ acts on a small number of qubits, and
dividing the total time $`t>0`$ into $`L`$ steps of duration
$`\Delta t=t/L`$ gives the first-order approximation

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
U_{\mathrm{Trot}}(t)=\left(e^{-\imath h_{m}\Delta t}\cdots e^{-\imath h_{1}\Delta t}\right)^{L}\approx e^{-\imath Ht}.
``` |  |

Each local unitary can be decomposed into elementary gates, and applying
the full sequence prepares an approximation to $`|\psi(t)\rangle`$. For
fixed $`H`$, the error satisfies
$`\|U_{\mathrm{Trot}}(t)-\exp({-\imath Ht})\|=\mathcal{O}({t^{2}}/{L})`$ \[[114](#bib.bib114)\].
Increasing $`L`$ reduces the error bound at the cost of greater circuit
depth. Higher-order Trotter formulas \[[115](#bib.bib115)\] improve this
tradeoff by canceling lower-order errors through local evolutions with
suitably chosen ordering and durations. Further refinements exploit
separated energy scales \[[116](#bib.bib116)\] or introduce auxiliary
correctors \[[117](#bib.bib117)\] to reduce product-formula errors.

For time-dependent Hamiltonians $`H(t)`$ in
Eq. ([4](#A1.E4 "In A.2 Quantum algorithms ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")),
$`\ket{\psi(t)}`$ composes short evolutions with the Hamiltonian held
fixed within each step \[[118](#bib.bib118)\], enabling digital
adiabatic ground-state preparation \[[119](#bib.bib119)\]. More
precisely, starting from an easily prepared ground state of
$`H_{\mathrm{init}}`$, one varies $`H(s)=(1-s)H_{\mathrm{init}}+sH`$
from $`s=0`$ to $`s=1`$. For a smooth path with a nondegenerate ground
state and a nonzero spectral gap, sufficiently slow evolution follows
the instantaneous ground state toward the target ground
state \[[120](#bib.bib120)\]. Beyond adiabatic preparation, quantum
imaginary-time evolution \[[121](#bib.bib121)\] and adaptive spectral
filtering \[[122](#bib.bib122)\] can prepare approximate ground states
by suppressing excited-state components through unitary updates inferred
from measurements and postselected filters built from controlled
evolution, respectively.

Quantum phase estimation. Quantum phase estimation
(QPE) \[[123](#bib.bib123)\] extracts the eigenphases of a unitary
operator $`U`$, defined by
$`U|\phi_{j}\rangle=e^{\imath\varphi_{j}}|\phi_{j}\rangle`$ with
$`\varphi_{j}\in[0,2\pi)`$. QPE offers rigorous precision guarantees,
achieving additive phase-estimation error $`\varepsilon`$ with
$`\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)`$ controlled applications of $`U`$ under
standard assumptions. As a fundamental primitive underlying quantum
algorithms such as Shor’s factoring algorithm and Hamiltonian eigenvalue
estimation, QPE plays a central role in the pursuit of quantum
computational advantages.

The mechanism of QPE is as follows. Given an initial state
$`|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}\rangle=\sum_{j}c_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle`$ and $`m`$
ancillary qubits prepared in $`|+\rangle^{\otimes m}`$, the circuit
performs

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|+\rangle^{\otimes m}|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}\rangle\xrightarrow{\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\mathrm{C}_{r}U^{2^{r}}}\frac{1}{2^{m/2}}\sum_{j,s}c_{j}e^{\imath s\varphi_{j}}|s\rangle|\phi_{j}\rangle\xrightarrow{\text{inverse QFT}}\sum_{j,y}c_{j}a_{jy}|y\rangle|\phi_{j}\rangle.
``` |  |

Here, $`\mathrm{C}_{r}U^{2^{r}}`$ denotes $`U^{2^{r}}`$ controlled by
ancillary qubit $`r`$, and the inverse quantum Fourier Transform (QFT)
acts on the ancillary register. The indices
$`s,y\in\{0,\ldots,2^{m}-1\}`$ label its computational-basis states,
with
$`a_{jy}=2^{-m}\sum_{s=0}^{2^{m}-1}\exp({\imath s(\varphi_{j}-2\pi y/2^{m})})`$.
Measuring this register yields $`y`$ with probability
$`p(y)=\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}|a_{jy}|^{2}`$, giving the estimate
$`\hat{\varphi}=2\pi y/2^{m}`$. For each eigenstate component,
$`|a_{jy}|^{2}`$ is concentrated near its eigenphase modulo $`2\pi`$,
with resolution $`\mathcal{O}(2^{-m})`$. Improving the resolution
requires higher controlled powers of $`U`$, up to $`U^{2^{m-1}}`$.

Alternative implementations reduce ancillary-qubit requirements through
iterative measurements \[[124](#bib.bib124)\], guide measurements
through Bayesian updates \[[125](#bib.bib125)\], or accommodate inexact
initial states with sufficient target-state
overlap \[[126](#bib.bib126)\]. Beyond extracting spectral information,
QPE also serves as a subroutine in quantum algorithms such as quantum
amplitude estimation \[[127](#bib.bib127)\], which estimates the
probability $`a`$ of a target measurement outcome for a state prepared
by a unitary $`W`$. Combining a reflection about $`W|0\rangle`$ with a
phase flip on the target outcomes defines an amplification operator
$`Q`$ whose relevant eigenphases $`\pm 2\theta`$ satisfy
$`a=\sin^{2}\theta`$. Applying QPE to $`Q`$ gives an estimate
$`\hat{\theta}`$ of $`\theta`$ and hence
$`\hat{a}=\sin^{2}\hat{\theta}`$, achieving $`|\hat{a}-a|\leq\epsilon`$
with $`\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})`$ calls to $`W`$ and $`W^{\dagger}`$,
compared with $`\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})`$ for direct sampling. To
reduce circuit depth, classical inference can replace QPE by using
measurements at different amplification powers \[[128](#bib.bib128)\],
with Bayesian schemes adapting these powers while accounting for device
noise \[[129](#bib.bib129)\].

Variational quantum simulation. Complementing non-variational
approaches, variational quantum simulation aims to investigate quantum
states and dynamics using variational quantum circuits (VQCs) tailored
to the limited coherence times and circuit depths of near-term quantum
devices \[[130](#bib.bib130)\]. It approximates quantum states or time
evolution through hybrid quantum-classical optimization, offering a
hardware-efficient alternative to direct Hamiltonian-based simulation.
Specifically, the variational quantum eigensolver
(VQE) \[[131](#bib.bib131)\] targets ground-state preparation through
energy minimization, while variational quantum Hamiltonian
evolution \[[132](#bib.bib132)\] simulates dynamics by fitting evolved
states or time-evolution operators. In what follows, we present these
two standard approaches separately.

Variational quantum eigensolver. VQE approximates the ground state
within the circuit family
$`|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle=U(\bm{\theta})|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}\rangle`$,
where $`|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}\rangle`$ is an easily prepared reference
state and $`\bm{\theta}`$ denotes the parameters of $`U(\bm{\theta})`$,
i.e., the employed VQC with a tailored circuit architecture. For the
target Hamiltonian $`H`$, the objective is to minimize

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
E(\bm{\theta})=\langle\psi(\bm{\theta})|H|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle\geq E_{0},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`E_{0}`$ is the ground-state energy. A classical optimizer
updates $`\bm{\theta}`$ to minimize
Eq. ([5](#A1.E5 "In A.2 Quantum algorithms ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")).
Gradients used in the optimization can be estimated by evaluating the
energy value $`E(\bm{\theta})`$ at shifted circuit
parameters \[[133](#bib.bib133)\]. The optimized VQC (i.e.,
$`U(\bm{\theta}^{*})`$) prepares a ground-state approximation, whose
accuracy depends on the circuit family and the quality of the
optimization \[[134](#bib.bib134)\]. Further developments address
circuit construction through adaptive operator selection based on energy
gradients \[[135](#bib.bib135)\] and parameter initialization through
tensor-network pretraining \[[136](#bib.bib136)\].

Beyond ground-state preparation, the VQE framework can be extended to
combinatorial optimization with a cost function $`C(\bm{s})`$ over
binary configurations $`\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{N}`$, as in the quantum
approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) \[[137](#bib.bib137),
[138](#bib.bib138)\]. Here, the VQC alternates evolution under

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H_{C}=\sum_{\bm{s}}C(\bm{s})|\bm{s}\rangle\langle\bm{s}|\quad\text{and}\quad H_{B}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}.
``` |  |

The evolution times are optimized to minimize
Eq. ([5](#A1.E5 "In A.2 Quantum algorithms ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"))
with $`H=H_{C}`$, after which low-cost solutions are selected from
configurations sampled in the computational basis.

Variational quantum Hamiltonian evolution. Variational approaches
simulate Hamiltonian dynamics by optimizing $`U(\bm{\theta})`$ that
prepare approximate evolved states
$`|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle=U(\bm{\theta})|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}\rangle`$.
For a time-independent Hamiltonian $`H`$, one approach starts from
$`|\psi(\bm{\theta}(0))\rangle\approx|\psi(0)\rangle`$ and fits each
short-time evolved state
$`e^{-\imath H\Delta t}|\psi(\bm{\theta}(j))\rangle`$ with
$`|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle`$ by minimizing their infidelity

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{\theta}(j+1)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\bm{\theta}}\left[1-\left|\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta})}e^{-\imath H\Delta t}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}(j))}\right|^{2}\right],
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\bm{\theta}(j)`$ denotes the parameters at time
$`j\Delta t`$ \[[132](#bib.bib132)\]. The short-time evolution
$`\exp({-\imath H\Delta t})`$ can be approximated by Trotterization.
Repeating the update approximates the state $`|\psi(t)\rangle`$ at
successive times.

Subsequent studies have explored complementary strategies to improve the
efficiency and scalability of variational quantum Hamiltonian evolution.
To reduce the required quantum resources, circuit knitting estimates the
fidelity in
Eq. ([6](#A1.E6 "In A.2 Quantum algorithms ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"))
using smaller subcircuits while constraining the sampling
overhead \[[139](#bib.bib139)\]. Other approaches approximate the
time-evolution operator $`\exp({-\imath H\Delta t})`$ directly by
learning a diagonal factorization to extend simulation
times \[[140](#bib.bib140)\] or fitting its action on subsystems to
reduce the number of qubits required for
training \[[141](#bib.bib141)\].

Quantum machine learning. Quantum machine learning (QML) aims to
leverage quantum computing to learn patterns from classical or quantum
data, with the potential to enhance the expressivity and computational
efficiency of learning models \[[142](#bib.bib142)\]. Two representative
formulations are quantum neural networks (QNNs) \[[143](#bib.bib143)\],
which optimize circuits to learn predictive models, and quantum kernel
methods \[[144](#bib.bib144)\], which use quantum circuits to estimate
kernel values for classical model fitting.

Quantum neural networks. A typical QNN encodes an input $`\bm{x}`$ as
$`|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle=V(\bm{x})|0\rangle`$ and processes it with a VQC
$`U(\bm{\theta})`$. The expectation value of an observable $`O`$ defines
the model output, i.e.,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\langle\phi(\bm{x})|U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})OU(\bm{\theta})|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle.
``` |  |

For supervised learning, consider a training set
$`\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{M}`$, where $`y^{(i)}`$
is the target associated with input $`\bm{x}^{(i)}`$. The parameters in
$`f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})`$ are optimized to minimize the empirical loss
$`\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=M^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}^{(i)}),y^{(i)})`$,
where $`\ell(z,y)`$ measures the discrepancy between a model output
$`z`$ and its target $`y`$. For regression, one can use the
squared-error loss $`\ell(z,y)=(z-y)^{2}`$, and for binary
classification with $`y\in\{-1,1\}`$, the logistic loss
$`\ell(z,y)=\log(1+\exp({-yz}))`$. Once trained, the circuit predicts
outputs for new inputs.

Subsequent studies have explored both the applications and theoretical
properties of QNNs. Beyond predictive learning, QNNs have been extended
to generative modeling of probability
distributions \[[145](#bib.bib145), [146](#bib.bib146)\] and
reinforcement learning of decision policies \[[147](#bib.bib147)\].
Meanwhile, theoretical studies have investigated the expressivity,
trainability, and generalization of QNNs. Expressivity analyses
characterize the functions and quantum states representable by
parameterized circuits \[[148](#bib.bib148)\]. Regarding trainability,
global cost functions and scrambling dynamics can cause gradients to
vanish exponentially \[[149](#bib.bib149)\]. Generalization analyses
further investigate how model complexity, training data, and quantum
measurements affect predictive performance on unseen
inputs \[[150](#bib.bib150)\].

Quantum kernel methods. Quantum kernel methods construct predictors from
a kernel $`\kappa(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})`$, defined as an inner product
between input representations in a feature space \[[151](#bib.bib151),
[144](#bib.bib144)\]. For the training set $`\mathcal{D}`$, the
predictor takes the form
$`f(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{\alpha}_{i}\kappa(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{(i)})`$,
where $`\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}`$ contains the learned
coefficients. A typical example of quantum kernels is the fidelity
kernel
$`\kappa(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{x}^{\prime})]`$,
where $`\rho(\bm{x})=|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle\langle\phi(\bm{x})|`$. The
value of this kernel equals the probability of the all-zero outcome
after applying $`V^{\dagger}(\bm{x})V(\bm{x}^{\prime})`$ to
$`|0\rangle`$ and measuring in the computational basis. The coefficients
$`\bm{\alpha}=(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{M})^{\top}`$ can then
be fitted classically by minimizing the regularized least-squares
objective of kernel ridge regression,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{\alpha}^{*}=\arg\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\|K\bm{\alpha}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\bm{\alpha}^{\top}K\bm{\alpha}\right\},i.e.,
``` |  |

where $`K`$ is the kernel matrix with entries
$`K_{ij}=\kappa(\bm{x}^{(i)},\bm{x}^{(j)})`$,
$`\bm{y}=(y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(M)})^{\top}`$, and $`\lambda>0`$ is a
regularization parameter \[[152](#bib.bib152)\]. Solving
$`(K+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{M})\bm{\alpha}^{*}=\bm{y}`$ gives the fitted
coefficients $`\bm{\alpha}^{*}`$. The prediction for a new input
$`\bm{x}`$ is then computed using the fitted coefficients
$`\bm{\alpha}^{*}`$ and the kernel values
$`\kappa(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{(i)})`$ between this input and the training
samples. Following this framework, projected kernels select observable
features to improve generalization \[[69](#bib.bib69)\], kernel
alignment adapts data encodings to the learning
task \[[153](#bib.bib153), [154](#bib.bib154)\], and covariant kernels
exploit group structure in the data \[[155](#bib.bib155)\].

Quantum-enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo. Quantum-enhanced Markov chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) \[[156](#bib.bib156)\] combines quantum proposals
with classical acceptance rules to sample equilibrium distributions and
estimate thermal averages. By potentially accelerating the exploration
of complex energy landscapes, it offers a promising approach to studying
many-body systems and addressing sampling bottlenecks in statistical
physics and computational chemistry.

A representative setting of Quantum-enhanced MCMC is a classical Ising
model with configurations $`\bm{s}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}`$ and energy
$`E(\bm{s})`$. The target is the Boltzmann distribution
$`\pi(\bm{s})=\exp({-\beta E(\bm{s})})/\mathcal{Z}`$, where $`\beta`$ is
the inverse temperature and
$`\mathcal{Z}=\sum_{\bm{s}}\exp({-\beta E(\bm{s})})`$ is the partition
function. At each step of the Markov chain, the current configuration is
encoded in a computational basis state $`|\bm{s}\rangle`$ and evolved
under the Hamiltonian
$`H_{\mathrm{prop}}=\sum_{\bm{s}}E(\bm{s})|\bm{s}\rangle\langle\bm{s}|+\eta\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}`$,
where $`\eta\in\mathbb{R}`$ controls the spin-flip strength. After
evolution for a time $`t`$, a computational-basis measurement yields a
candidate $`\bm{s}^{\prime}`$ with probability
$`p(\bm{s}^{\prime}|\bm{s})=|\langle\bm{s}^{\prime}|\exp({-\imath H_{\mathrm{prop}}t})|\bm{s}\rangle|^{2}`$.
Since $`H_{\mathrm{prop}}`$ is real and symmetric,
$`p(\bm{s}^{\prime}|\bm{s})=p(\bm{s}|\bm{s}^{\prime})`$, so the
Metropolis–Hastings rule used to update the Markov chain accepts the
proposed configuration $`\bm{s}^{\prime}`$ with a probability that can
be computed classically from the energy difference, i.e.,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\rm Pr}_{\mathrm{acc}}(\bm{s}^{\prime}|\bm{s})=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(\bm{s}^{\prime})p(\bm{s}|\bm{s}^{\prime})}{\pi(\bm{s})p(\bm{s}^{\prime}|\bm{s})}\right\}=\min\left\{1,e^{-\beta[E(\bm{s}^{\prime})-E(\bm{s})]}\right\}.
``` |  |

This rule preserves $`\pi`$ as the stationary distribution, so averages
along an ergodic chain converge to the corresponding thermal
expectations. The spin-flip strength $`\eta`$ and evolution time $`t`$
can be chosen to favor transitions between configurations that differ in
many spins but have similar energies, which can accelerate equilibration
compared with single-spin updates.

Subsequent studies have further explored improved quantum proposal
strategies \[[157](#bib.bib157)\], faster mixing \[[158](#bib.bib158)\],
and reduced quantum resource requirements \[[159](#bib.bib159)\],
extending its applicability to larger and more complex
systems \[[160](#bib.bib160), [161](#bib.bib161)\].

### A.3 Property characterization of quantum systems

Quantum property characterization protocols aim to infer physical
properties of quantum systems from finite measurement results. Following
the framework in Ref. \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], we review five representative
directions: linear property estimation, nonlinear property estimation,
Hamiltonian learning, quantum error mitigation, and quantum process
characterization. For each direction, we outline representative
algorithms, focusing on their problem formulations, key assumptions, and
computational procedures.

Linear properties of quantum states. The linear property of an
$`N`$-qubit quantum state $`\rho`$ refers to the expectation value of a
fixed observable $`O`$, i.e., $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ in
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")).
Applying different observables $`O`$ can characterize various physical
quantities of quantum systems, such as magnetization, energy, and
correlation functions. Two complementary approaches to estimating
$`f_{O}(\rho)`$ are classical shadows \[[162](#bib.bib162)\] and
classical surrogates \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. That is, classical shadows
estimate multiple observable expectation values from measurements of a
fixed quantum state, whereas classical surrogates learn to predict these
values across a parametrized family of quantum states. Their mechanisms
are summarized below.

*Classical shadows.* Classical shadows enable the prediction of multiple
quantum properties from shared measurement data without additional
quantum measurements. Its mechanism is as follows. For an unknown
$`N`$-qubit state $`\rho`$, the classical-shadow protocol performs $`T`$
independent measurement rounds. In each round, a unitary $`U`$ is
sampled from a prescribed ensemble $`\mathcal{U}`$ and applied to a
fresh copy of $`\rho`$, followed by a computational-basis measurement.
The outcome is an $`N`$-bit string $`\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{N}`$, obtained
with probability
$`\mathbb{P}(\bm{b}|U)=\bra{\bm{b}}U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{\bm{b}}`$. This
procedure defines a linear map $`\mathcal{M}`$, known as the measurement
channel, i.e.,
$`\mathcal{M}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}\sim\mathbb{P}(\bm{b})}U^{\dagger}\ket{\bm{b}}\bra{\bm{b}}U=\sum_{\bm{b}}\int\mathsf{d}UU^{\dagger}\ket{\bm{b}}\bra{\bm{b}}U\bra{\bm{b}}U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{\bm{b}}`$.

When the measurement channel $`\mathcal{M}`$ is invertible, each
measurement outcome yields a classical-shadow snapshot
$`\hat{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(U_{t}^{\dagger}|\bm{b}_{t}\rangle\langle\bm{b}_{t}|U_{t})`$.
For randomized single-qubit Pauli measurements, where
$`U_{t}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(2)^{\otimes N}`$, the snapshot takes the explicit
form

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\rho}_{t}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\left(3U_{j,t}^{\dagger}|b_{j,t}\rangle\langle b_{j,t}|U_{j,t}-\mathbb{I}_{2}\right).
``` |  |

Averaging $`T`$ independent snapshots gives
$`\hat{\rho}_{T}=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\rho}_{t}`$, which provides an
unbiased estimator of $`\rho`$. The same snapshots can then estimate
multiple observables $`\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}`$ through
$`\hat{f}_{O_{i}}(\rho)=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{Tr}(O_{i}\hat{\rho}_{t})`$.

Subsequent advances, including derandomized
measurements \[[163](#bib.bib163)\] and measurement
grouping \[[164](#bib.bib164)\], further reduce measurement costs by
exploiting the structure of target observables.

*Classical surrogates.* Unlike classical shadows for a fixed quantum
state, classical surrogates aim to learn how a linear property
$`f_{O}(\rho(\bm{x}))=\text{Tr}(\rho(\bm{x})O)`$ varies across a
parametrized family of quantum states $`\rho(\bm{x})`$, where
$`\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ specifies circuit parameters or Hamiltonian
couplings. The goal of a classical surrogate is to learn a predictive
model $`h(\bm{x})`$ that approximates the observable expectation value
$`f_{O}(\bm{x})`$ across a parametrized family of quantum states, with
prediction error
$`\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{D}}|f_{O}(\bm{x})-h(\bm{x})|^{2}\leq\epsilon`$.

A common machine-learning-based approach represents the target function
using a prescribed family of basis functions
$`\{\Phi_{\bm{\omega}}\}_{\bm{\omega}\in\Lambda}`$. The construction
involves two steps. First, a training dataset
$`\mathcal{T}=\{(\bm{x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}`$ is collected, where
each label $`y^{(i)}`$ estimates $`f_{O}(\bm{x}^{(i)})`$ through quantum
measurements. Second, a subset of basis functions
$`\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq\Lambda`$ is selected to construct the
surrogate

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\hat{w}_{\bm{\omega}}\Phi_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x}),
``` |  |

where the coefficients $`\hat{w}_{\bm{\omega}}`$ are learned from
$`\mathcal{T}`$ through regression. Once trained, the surrogate predicts
observable expectation values for unseen inputs using only classical
computation, amortizing the quantum measurement cost across subsequent
predictions. Under suitable structural assumptions, classical surrogates
admit provable guarantees on sample complexity and prediction accuracy,
potentially reducing the computational cost of repeated quantum
evaluations \[[26](#bib.bib26), [29](#bib.bib29), [165](#bib.bib165)\].

Beyond these structured machine-learning approaches, recent studies have
explored deep learning and large language models to develop more
flexible surrogates for both qubit-based and continuous-variable quantum
systems \[[166](#bib.bib166), [167](#bib.bib167)\].

Nonlinear properties of quantum states. Beyond linear properties, many
physically relevant quantities depend nonlinearly on the quantum state
$`\rho`$. A representative class consists of degree-$`k`$ polynomial
functions of $`\rho`$, such as

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       f_{O}(\rho^{k})=\text{Tr}\left[O\rho^{k}\right],
       ```                                               |     |

where $`O`$ is a fixed observable and $`k`$ is a positive integer. Such
nonlinear functions arise naturally in the characterization of mixed
states, with important examples including purity \[[168](#bib.bib168)\]
and Rényi entropies \[[169](#bib.bib169)\].

A key approach to estimating nonlinear properties is to reformulate them
as linear-property estimation problems on multiple copies of the quantum
state. Specifically, a homogeneous polynomial of degree $`k`$ in the
matrix elements of $`\rho`$ can be represented as a linear functional of
the replicated state $`\rho^{\otimes k}`$. This gives
$`f_{O}(\rho^{k})=\text{Tr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)\rho^{\otimes k}\right]`$,
where $`\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)`$ is an operator acting on $`k`$ copies of
the original Hilbert space. For example, the purity of a quantum state
is defined as $`\text{Tr}(\rho^{2})`$, which can be expressed as
$`\text{Tr}(\rho^{2})=\text{Tr}(\mathbb{S}\rho^{\otimes 2})`$, where
$`O=\mathbb{I}`$ and $`\mathcal{A}_{2}(O)`$ is the swap operator
$`\mathbb{S}`$. By writing $`f_{O}(\rho^{k})`$ as a linear property in
terms of $`\rho^{\otimes k}`$, it could be characterized using classical
shadow methods as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{f}_{O}(\rho^{k})=\frac{(T-k)!}{T!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\neq t_{2}\cdots\neq t_{k}\in[T]\end{subarray}}\text{Tr}\left[\mathcal{A}(O)\hat{\rho}_{t_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{\rho}_{t_{k}}\right].
``` |  |

where $`\{\hat{\rho}_{t_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$ refers to $`T`$ independent
snapshots of $`\rho`$, and
$`\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}_{O}(\rho^{k})]=f_{O}(\rho^{k})`$. Beyond this basic
approach, subsequent developments have introduced more sophisticated
measurement protocols to improve the statistical efficiency of
estimating $`f_{O}(\rho^{k})`$ and reduce the measurement resources
required for higher-order quantities \[[170](#bib.bib170)\].

Hamiltonian Learning. Hamiltonian learning aims to identify the
interactions governing a quantum system from experimental observations,
such as measurements of its time evolution \[[171](#bib.bib171)\].
Consider an $`N`$-qubit Hamiltonian $`H=\sum_{l=1}^{L}h_{l}P_{l}`$,
where
$`P_{l}\in\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes N}\setminus\{\mathbb{I}^{\otimes N}\}`$
are non-identity Pauli strings and
$`\bm{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{L})\in\mathbb{R}^{L}`$ denotes the unknown
interaction coefficients. When the candidate Pauli terms
$`\{P_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}`$ are known, Hamiltonian learning reduces to
estimating $`\bm{h}`$ from measurement data.

A common approach to Hamiltonian learning involves two stages. First,
collecting time-dependent measurements for a set of initial-state and
observable pairs $`\{(\rho_{j},O_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}`$. Second, inferring
the Hamiltonian coefficients $`\bm{h}`$ from these measurements. For
each pair, the initial state evolves as
$`\rho_{j}(t)=e^{-\imath Ht}\rho_{j}e^{\imath Ht}`$, and measurements of
$`O_{j}`$ yield estimates of
$`f_{O_{j}}(\rho_{j}(t))=\text{Tr}[O_{j}\rho_{j}(t)]`$. The key insight
is that the initial time derivative of $`f_{O_{j}}(\rho_{j}(t))`$
depends linearly on the unknown coefficients $`\bm{h}`$, enabling
Hamiltonian learning through linear regression. Mathematically, we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{d}_{j}:=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{O_{j}}(\rho_{j}(t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\imath\cdot\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}[H,O_{j}])=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bm{K}_{jl}\bm{h}_{l},
``` |  |

where $`\bm{K}_{jl}=\imath\cdot\text{Tr}(\rho_{j}[P_{l},O_{j}])`$
defines the entries of a matrix $`\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times L}`$
determined by the known input states, observables, and candidate Pauli
strings. This relation reduces Hamiltonian learning to a linear inverse
problem \[[172](#bib.bib172)\]. Specifically, by measuring
$`f_{O_{j}}(\rho_{j}(t))`$ at sufficiently short evolution times and
fitting its time dependence, one can estimate the initial derivatives
$`\hat{\bm{d}}=(\hat{\bm{d}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{d}}_{M})`$, which
approximately satisfy $`\hat{\bm{d}}\approx\bm{d}=\bm{K}\bm{h}`$. The
Hamiltonian coefficients can then be inferred by solving a regression
problem, such as least-squares estimation, i.e.,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\bm{h}}=\arg\min_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}}\|\bm{K}\bm{h}-\hat{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}.
``` |  |

Quantum error mitigation. The objective of quantum error mitigation
(QEM) is to estimate ideal observable expectation values $`f_{O}(\rho)`$
in
Eq. ([1](#A1.E1 "In A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"))
using measurement data collected from noisy quantum devices
\[[64](#bib.bib64)\]. In practice, hardware noise leads to a noisy
implementation $`\tilde{\mathcal{U}}`$, yielding
$`f_{O}(\tilde{\rho})=\text{Tr}[O\tilde{\mathcal{U}}(\rho_{0})]`$, which
generally deviates from $`f_{O}(\rho)`$. QEM seeks to reduce this
discrepancy through suitably designed noisy experiments and classical
post-processing.

A representative QEM approach is zero-noise extrapolation
(ZNE) \[[173](#bib.bib173)\], which estimates $`f_{O}(\rho)`$ by
measuring a quantum circuit at multiple noise levels and extrapolating
to the zero-noise limit. Let $`\lambda\geq 0`$ denote the effective
noise strength and
$`f_{O}(\tilde{\rho}_{\lambda})=\text{Tr}[O\tilde{\rho}_{\lambda}]`$,
where $`\tilde{\rho}_{\lambda}=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\lambda}(\rho_{0})`$
and $`\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0}=\mathcal{U}`$ corresponds to the ideal
circuit. ZNE first collects estimates
$`\{\hat{f}_{O}(\tilde{\rho}_{\lambda_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{M}`$ at different
noise levels, typically generated using techniques such as gate folding
or pulse stretching. It then extrapolates these estimates toward
$`\lambda=0`$. In particular, Richardson extrapolation constructs the
estimator as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{f}_{O}^{\mathrm{ZNE}}(\tilde{\rho}_{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{c}_{j}\cdot\hat{f}_{O}(\tilde{\rho}_{\lambda_{j}}),
``` |  |

where the extrapolation coefficients $`\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}`$
satisfy $`\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{c}_{j}=1`$ and
$`\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{c}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{k}=0`$ for $`k\in[M-1]`$. These
constraints cancel the leading $`M-1`$ orders of noise contributions,
reducing the extrapolation bias at the potential cost of amplified
statistical errors. Beyond ZNE, various other QEM approaches have been
developed, including probabilistic error
cancellation \[[174](#bib.bib174)\], symmetry
verification \[[175](#bib.bib175)\], and virtual
distillation \[[176](#bib.bib176)\], each employing different strategies
to suppress or compensate for the effects of quantum noise.

Quantum process characterization. This task aims to infer the properties
of an unknown quantum process from measurements of its input-output
behavior. Mathematically, a quantum process is described by a completely
positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map $`\mathcal{E}`$, which
transforms an input quantum state $`\rho`$ into an output state
$`\mathcal{E}(\rho)`$. Depending on the characterization objective, one
may seek to reconstruct the entire quantum channel or predict selected
properties of its output states.

*Quantum process tomography.* Quantum process tomography aims to
reconstruct an unknown quantum channel $`\mathcal{E}`$ from measurements
of its outputs for a set of known input states. A convenient
representation of the channel is its Choi matrix, defined as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{a,b=1}^{d}|a\rangle\langle b|\otimes\mathcal{E}(|a\rangle\langle b|),
``` |  |

where $`d`$ denotes the dimension of the input Hilbert space. For a CPTP
map, the Choi matrix satisfies the physical constraints
$`J_{\mathcal{E}}\succeq\bm{0}`$ and
$`\text{Tr}_{\mathrm{out}}(J_{\mathcal{E}})=\mathbb{I}_{d}`$. These
properties allow quantum process tomography to be formulated as a
constrained estimation problem. Specifically, for a known input state
$`\rho_{s}`$ and an output measurement effect $`M_{b}`$, the probability
of obtaining measurement outcome $`b`$ is given by
$`\mathbb{P}(b|s)=\text{Tr}[M_{b}\mathcal{E}(\rho_{s})]=\text{Tr}[(\rho_{s}^{\top}\otimes M_{b})J_{\mathcal{E}}].`$
By preparing different input states and performing measurements on their
corresponding outputs, one obtains a collection of measurement
statistics that constrain the unknown Choi matrix. The channel can then
be reconstructed by fitting a physical Choi matrix to the observed data,
for example through maximum-likelihood
estimation \[[177](#bib.bib177)\].

*Prediction of output properties.* Different from full process
tomography, output-property prediction focuses on estimating selected
observable expectation values for previously unseen input states. In
particular, for an unknown quantum channel $`\mathcal{E}`$ and a target
observable $`O`$, the prediction task is to learn the function

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\mathcal{E},O}(\rho)=\text{Tr}[O\mathcal{E}(\rho)]=\text{Tr}[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(O)\rho],
``` |  |

where $`\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}`$ denotes the adjoint channel. This
relation indicates that predicting the expectation value of $`O`$ at the
output is equivalent to estimating the expectation value of the
effective observable $`\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(O)`$ at the input.
Therefore, instead of reconstructing the entire channel, one can
construct a classical predictive model by learning an approximation of
this effective observable.

A representative approach collects training data consisting of known
input states and estimates of the corresponding output expectation
values \[[178](#bib.bib178)\]. The effective observable can then be
approximated using a selected operator basis $`\{P_{j}\}`$ as
$`\hat{O}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{j}\bm{w}_{j}P_{j}`$, where the
coefficients $`\{\bm{w}_{j}\}`$ are learned from the training data. For
a new input state $`\rho`$, the resulting predictor takes the form

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{E},O}(\rho)=\text{Tr}(\hat{O}_{\mathrm{eff}}\rho)=\sum_{j}\bm{w}_{j}\text{Tr}(P_{j}\rho).
``` |  |

Once the coefficients are learned, the predictor can estimate the target
observable expectation value without additional access to the unknown
channel, provided that the required input-state features are available.

### A.4 AI agents and harnesses

Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from conversational assistants
into autonomous agents capable of executing complex tasks, ranging from
software engineering to scientific research. Here, we introduce three
foundations of AI scientists relevant to our work, including LLM agents
for software engineering, scientific-reproduction agents, and automated
harness design and agent self-improvement.

LLM agents for software engineering. Unlike conventional LLMs that
generate responses to individual prompts, LLM agents can execute complex
tasks through iterative reasoning and tool interactions. Software
engineering has become a prominent application and evaluation setting
for such agents. Specifically, an LLM agent operates within a software
framework, known as a *harness*, that manages interactions between the
LLM and its environment. At each step, the harness provides the model
with task instructions and execution history, receives a proposed
action, executes it through available tools, and returns the resulting
feedback. This process continues until the task is completed or a
predefined resource limit is reached, with inference costs determined by
the tokens consumed across model calls.

A representative benchmark for LLM agents in software engineering is
SWE-bench \[[42](#bib.bib42)\], which evaluates agents on resolving
real-world software issues, with correctness assessed through repository
tests. Building on this setting, SWE-agent \[[43](#bib.bib43)\]
demonstrated that specialized tools for code navigation, search, and
editing can substantially improve agent performance without changing the
underlying LLM. These findings underpin the importance of harness design
alongside model capabilities.

Subsequent research has explored different design philosophies, ranging
from minimal harnesses built around a single shell tool to adaptive
agents that construct their own tools during
execution \[[77](#bib.bib77), [78](#bib.bib78)\]. In practice, harnesses
can be developed by model providers for their own models, such as
DeepSeek Harness (DSH), or by third parties to support different models
through application programming interfaces (APIs).

Scientific-reproduction agents. Beyond software engineering, LLM agents
are increasingly being developed as AI scientists to automate scientific
research. One important application is scientific reproduction, which
aims to reconstruct computational procedures and reproduce results
reported in published papers, thereby facilitating the verification and
reuse of scientific findings. Previous research has advanced along two
complementary directions, namely developing benchmarks to evaluate
reproduction capabilities and designing agents to automate reproduction
workflows.

Existing benchmarks differ in their task objectives and the amount of
prior information provided to agents. Typically, benchmark development
involves defining reproduction objectives, constructing tasks and
reference materials, and establishing evaluation criteria to assess the
performance of different agents. For example,
PaperBench \[[179](#bib.bib179)\] evaluates the reproduction of
machine-learning research from published papers, supplemented by
author-provided clarifications, using expert-developed grading rubrics.
PRBench \[[180](#bib.bib180)\] extends paper-based reproduction to
physics, providing expert-curated task instructions that specify target
results and implementation requirements. In contrast,
CORE-Bench \[[181](#bib.bib181)\] evaluates agents on executing and
interpreting released research code and data, while
AutoExperiment \[[84](#bib.bib84)\] examines their ability to
reconstruct missing components of existing implementations. Other
benchmarks investigate computational reproduction in materials science
and the social sciences, using domain-specific research materials and
evaluation protocols \[[51](#bib.bib51), [85](#bib.bib85)\].

Complementing these benchmarks, research on reproduction agents focuses
on developing computational workflows for recovering published results.
Depending on the available research materials and task specifications,
agents may be required to implement methods from scientific papers,
execute existing research code, or reconstruct incomplete
implementations. A common design is to coordinate multiple stages of
scientific reproduction, including interpreting research materials,
planning computational procedures, implementing or adapting code,
executing experiments, and validating the resulting outputs. For
example, DeepCode \[[76](#bib.bib76)\] proposes an agentic framework for
generating executable code from research papers and technical
specifications, whereas InternAgent \[[75](#bib.bib75)\] develops an
agent-based workflow intended to support scientific understanding,
implementation, and experimental execution. Related efforts, such as
Paper2Agent \[[62](#bib.bib62)\], transform scientific papers and
associated research artifacts into interactive agents that expose
reusable computational capabilities.

Automated harness design and agent self-improvement. The performance of
LLM agents depends not only on the underlying language model but also on
how the agent is organized and how it learns from previous executions.
This has motivated research on two complementary approaches to
automatically improving agent capabilities: optimizing the harness that
governs agent execution and accumulating reusable experience from
completed tasks.

The first approach, automated harness design, aims to improve agent
performance by optimizing its prompts, tools, and control flow. Existing
methods employ LLM-based search to generate candidate harnesses,
evaluate their performance on benchmark tasks, and iteratively refine
their designs \[[182](#bib.bib182), [79](#bib.bib79),
[80](#bib.bib80)\]. The resulting harness can then be deployed to
execute subsequent tasks. This approach primarily improves the agent’s
execution framework, with the optimized design typically remaining fixed
during deployment.

The second approach focuses on improving agents through experience
accumulated during task execution. Instead of modifying model parameters
or harness code, experience-based methods extract useful information
from previous runs, such as reflections on failures, successful
problem-solving strategies, reusable workflows, and optimized
instructions \[[183](#bib.bib183), [184](#bib.bib184),
[185](#bib.bib185), [186](#bib.bib186)\]. This information is stored as
external memory and retrieved to guide subsequent executions, enabling
agents to adapt their behavior without retraining the underlying model.
In this work, we adopt this experience-based perspective of
self-improvement, emphasizing the accumulation and reuse of validated
knowledge across scientific reproduction tasks.

### A.5 Literature review

AI scientists are increasingly being explored to automate scientific
reasoning, computational implementation, and validation. Three research
directions are particularly relevant to our work: autonomous quantum
algorithm design, autonomous investigation of quantum systems, and
quantum research reproduction. These directions pursue distinct
scientific objectives: designing improved computational methods,
discovering or explaining physical phenomena, and independently
verifying published findings, respectively. Below, we review
representative advances in each direction and highlight their
differences from our study.

Autonomous quantum computing design. Autonomous quantum computing design
aims to discover and optimize quantum algorithms, circuit architectures,
and error-correcting codes under computational and hardware constraints.
A central challenge is the vast design space of quantum circuits,
programs, and error-correcting codes. Existing approaches leverage AI
models to guide candidate generation with such domain-specific
reasoning, while computational evaluation provides feedback for
iterative refinement.

For algorithm construction, LLMs have been trained to generate programs
representing families of quantum circuits \[[55](#bib.bib55)\], while
iterative search has been applied to quantum machine learning
architectures \[[56](#bib.bib56), [57](#bib.bib57)\], quantum state
preparation \[[187](#bib.bib187)\], and molecular ground-state energy
estimation \[[61](#bib.bib61)\]. Quantum resource optimization instead
improves existing computational procedures by improving Hamiltonian
simulation accuracy \[[188](#bib.bib188)\] and reducing gate and qubit
counts in quantum arithmetic subroutines \[[90](#bib.bib90)\]. In
quantum error correction, AI-guided search has been used to discover
quantum error-correcting codes \[[54](#bib.bib54), [52](#bib.bib52),
[53](#bib.bib53)\].

Autonomous investigation of quantum systems. Autonomous investigation of
quantum systems aims to advance scientific understanding through two
complementary tasks: predicting physical behaviour from specified models
and inferring explanations for observed phenomena. For the former, AI
agents integrate established numerical methods with computational
validation to investigate quantum phase
transitions \[[189](#bib.bib189)\] and dynamical
evolution \[[60](#bib.bib60)\]. For the latter, agents propose candidate
physical models and computationally assess their consistency with
observations. This capability has been explored through controlled
rediscovery of known physical models \[[190](#bib.bib190)\] and applied
to interpreting experimental optical measurements in quantum
materials \[[191](#bib.bib191)\], where human scientists guided the
investigation and collected measurements, while AI agents developed and
tested candidate explanations.

Benchmark for quantum science. As AI scientists become increasingly
capable of conducting computational research, systematic benchmarks are
needed to assess their scientific reasoning, implementation, and
validation capabilities. Existing benchmarks evaluate AI agents on
tailored research questions derived from scientific
papers \[[192](#bib.bib192)\], simulated quantum engineering
tasks \[[193](#bib.bib193)\], and the reproduction of computational
results reported in physics \[[180](#bib.bib180)\] and materials
science \[[51](#bib.bib51)\]. Automated scientific checks have also been
introduced to assess computational correctness and provide feedback for
code refinement \[[194](#bib.bib194)\]. Quantum programming benchmarks
further assess whether LLMs can translate task descriptions into
functionally correct programs across different software
packages \[[195](#bib.bib195), [196](#bib.bib196), [197](#bib.bib197),
[198](#bib.bib198)\]. These efforts establish structured evaluation
environments for measuring the capabilities of AI scientists.

Our work differs from the three prior research directions by advancing
autonomous paper-to-result reproduction in computational quantum
science. Although PRBench \[[180](#bib.bib180)\] benchmarks end-to-end
paper reproduction across broader areas of physics, its reliance on
expert-curated task instructions specifying implementation requirements
and expected outputs limits its ability to assess whether AI scientists
can independently reconstruct research procedures from original papers.
Our objective is to develop an AI scientist capable of reproducing
published results directly from scientific papers and target figures
with minimal human intervention. Importantly, our investigation reveals
that ambiguities and even errors in published research can substantially
hinder autonomous reproduction. Beyond benchmarking, the proposed RA
systematically investigates the causes of reproduction failures and
distils validated corrections into reusable knowledge, enabling
continual improvement across research tasks.

### B.1 Workflow of OmniQ for selecting and organizing research papers

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the reproduction benchmark,
OmniQ employs a seven-step workflow that integrates multi-agent
cross-validation with expert verification, as illustrated in Fig. [SI
1](#A2.F1 "Figure SI 1 ‣ B.1 Workflow of OmniQ for selecting and organizing research papers ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists").
The workflow begins with task construction in Steps 1–3, where a domain
expert selects a research paper and its target figure, an LLM agent
drafts the task specification and evaluation criteria, and the expert
subsequently reviews and refines both. Next, Step 4 involves initial
benchmarking, in which multiple agent configurations independently
execute each reproduction task, with each configuration evaluated once.
In Step 5, their evaluation results are cross-checked by an auditing
agent to identify potentially disputed judgments and assess their
consistency with the original paper, task specification, and evaluation
criteria. Based on this audit, domain experts review the flagged papers
in Step 6, classify confirmed reproduction issues into three levels
(i.e., Levels I–III, as defined in the main text) according to their
severity, and determine necessary revisions to the task specification or
evaluation criteria. Finally, in Step 7, the affected tasks are
re-evaluated or re-executed using the revised specifications and
criteria, yielding a more reliable benchmark for assessing scientific
reproduction capabilities.

Figure SI 1: Workflow of OmniQ. The workflow consists of seven steps
involving domain experts, agents, and benchmark evaluation,
distinguished by different colors. Arrows indicate the interactions and
progression between steps.

Beyond the overall workflow, three key mechanisms underpin the
construction and reliability of the OmniQ benchmark, i.e., task
specification and evaluation criteria, automated judging, and
cross-agent auditing with expert verification. We detail these
mechanisms below.

Task specification and evaluation criteria. Steps 1–3 of OmniQ establish
each reproduction task through agent-assisted construction and expert
review. As illustrated in Fig. 1, each task comprises two Markdown
documents with distinct purposes: a preliminary document that guides the
AI scientist and an evaluation criteria document reserved for the
automated judge. This separation allows the reproduction objectives to
be specified explicitly while keeping the expected results inaccessible
to the AI scientist.

The preliminary document specifies the target figure and provides both
general guidelines and paper-specific instructions. The general
guidelines, shared across all tasks, require the AI scientist to follow
the methods and settings reported in the original paper, prohibit
fabricated or externally obtained results, and require all
approximations to be documented. The paper-specific instructions define
the target problem, experimental scope, and relevant conventions, while
leaving the reconstruction of computational procedures and numerical
results to the AI scientist.

The evaluation criteria document defines the requirements for assessing
reproduction correctness. These include the structural properties of the
target figure, quantitative checkpoints with reference values and
tolerances, and verification of the experimental parameters and
essential computational procedures. Both documents are initially drafted
by an LLM agent and subsequently reviewed by domain experts to ensure
consistency with the original paper. In particular, expert review
addresses potentially misleading criteria, such as purely visual
assessments, numerical tolerances that are overly restrictive relative
to computational variability, or reference values incorrectly extracted
from published figures. Importantly, the evaluation criteria are made
available to the automated judge only after the reproduction is
completed, ensuring that the AI scientist cannot access the reference
values during execution.

Automated judging. To assess scientific reproduction beyond visual
similarity, Step 4 of OmniQ employs an automated judge to verify both
the reproduced results and their underlying computational procedures.
Implemented using Claude Code with Opus 4.8, the judge examines the
complete execution workspace, including source code, execution logs,
numerical data, and experimental reports, against the predefined
evaluation criteria. This evidence-based assessment examines not only
whether the reproduced figure matches the published results but also
whether those results are supported by the prescribed computational
methods and experimental settings.

The judge classifies identified issues into two categories according to
their impact on scientific correctness. In particular, critical issues
include quantitative results outside the specified tolerances,
deviations from essential methodological steps, inconsistent
experimental parameters, and numerical values that cannot be traced to
the executed computations. Every quantitative checkpoint is treated as a
mandatory requirement, and documenting an approximation does not exempt
the reproduction from satisfying the corresponding criteria. In
contrast, non-critical issues are limited to visual presentation and do
not affect scientific validity.

A reproduction is considered successful only when no critical issues are
identified. For tasks involving multiple target figures, each figure is
evaluated independently, and the task is considered successfully
reproduced only if all target figures satisfy their respective criteria.
This protocol prioritizes the fidelity of scientific results and
computational procedures over superficial agreement with published
figures.

Cross-agent verification and expert review. To further establish the
reliability of scientific reproduction assessments, Steps 5–7 of OmniQ
introduce a cross-agent verification mechanism to systematically examine
potential inconsistencies in the original papers, task specifications,
and evaluation criteria. Specifically, this mechanism leverages
evaluation results from multiple independent agent configurations to
identify potential issues in the benchmark itself.

Across the 60 selected research papers, a verification agent examines
3,376 judging outcomes collected from multiple agent configurations and
target figures, focusing on systematic failures where different
configurations, including the strongest-performing ones, fail to satisfy
the same evaluation criteria. This process identifies 19 papers for
further investigation. For each flagged paper, the verification agent
systematically compares the original publication, task specification,
evaluation criteria, and computational results across configurations.
Where possible, it also independently checks key numerical values using
analytical calculations, authors’ released code, or data extracted from
published figures.

Domain experts subsequently review these findings, classify identified
issues into three levels according to their severity, and determine
whether revisions to the task specifications or evaluation criteria are
warranted. Expert review confirms issues in 18 of the 19 flagged papers.
Depending on their underlying causes, revisions may align task
specifications with settings recovered from the authors’ released code
or data, restrict computational requirements to feasible resource
budgets, or adjust evaluation checkpoints that depend on unstated or
idealized conventions. Note that tasks whose evaluation outcomes are
unaffected by the identified issues are retained without substantive
changes. Finally, affected tasks are re-evaluated when the criteria are
revised or re-executed when the task specifications are modified.

### B.2 Construction of computational quantum science dataset

To systematically evaluate the capabilities of AI scientists in
computational quantum science, we construct a dataset comprising 60
research papers and their corresponding figure-level reproduction tasks.
The selected papers span three major research areas with relatively
balanced representation: classical simulation of quantum systems (20
papers), quantum algorithms (19 papers), and quantum property
characterization (21 papers). This selection captures diverse
computational paradigms and methodological challenges in computational
quantum science. We prioritize influential studies published in leading
journals while requiring that their numerical results be reproducible
within a computational budget of approximately one day per task. This
constraint largely excludes computationally intensive deep
learning-based approaches. An overview of the dataset is provided in
Table [SI
1](#A2.T1 "Table SI 1 ‣ B.2 Construction of computational quantum science dataset ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists").

For each paper, we select representative figures that capture its
central scientific findings or methodological contributions. Reproducing
these figures requires translating scientific descriptions into
executable workflows, implementing the underlying methods, and
validating the numerical results within OmniQ. By requiring AI
scientists to reconstruct computational results from the underlying
scientific principles, this design provides a systematic assessment of
their scientific reasoning and implementation capabilities. In the
following, we describe the dataset composition and representative
reproduction tasks across the three research areas.

Classical simulation of quantum systems. To evaluate the agent’s ability
to reproduce research-level results in classical quantum simulation, we
select papers based on two complementary criteria. The first prioritizes
state-of-the-art numerical methods that introduce novel algorithmic
ideas, require technically demanding implementations, or play an
important role in assessing claims of quantum utility. The second
emphasizes physics-informed methods, where successful reproduction
requires understanding the underlying quantum systems and how their
physical properties, such as symmetries, locality, and entanglement
structure, are exploited to enable efficient numerical simulation. These
criteria enable us to systematically assess the AI scientist’s ability
to understand and faithfully reproduce sophisticated classical
simulation methods.

The selected papers provide balanced coverage of representative
classical quantum simulation methods discussed in
SI [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),

#### B.3.1 Level I (Paper #46): Hyperparameter inconsistency in quantum error mitigation

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) plays an important role in improving the
accuracy of quantum computations on noisy quantum devices, yet achieving
accurate mitigation with limited experimental overhead remains
challenging \[[64](#bib.bib64)\]. Paper #46 \[[63](#bib.bib63)\],
published in Nature Machine Intelligence in 2024, investigated
machine-learning-based QEM (ML-QEM) to improve mitigation accuracy and
computational efficiency, with particular emphasis on generalization
beyond the training regime. Here, we examine the reproduction of its
random-forest (RF) results, where an inconsistency between the
hyperparameter settings described in the paper and those used in the
released implementation led to substantial reproduction discrepancies.

Algorithmic background. Recall from
SI. [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Property characterization of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")
that QEM aims to recover the ideal expectation value
$`f_{O}(\rho)=\text{Tr}(OU\rho_{0}U^{\dagger})`$ from noisy
measurements, which yield
$`f_{O}(\tilde{\rho})=\text{Tr}[O\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}(\rho_{0})]`$
with $`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}`$ representing the corresponding noisy
quantum channel. ML-QEM approaches this task by learning a classical
predictive model from training data
$`\mathcal{T}=\{(\bm{z}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}`$, where
$`\bm{z}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ encodes noisy measurement outcomes and
circuit-related features, while $`y^{(i)}`$ denotes the corresponding
ideal expectation value. As shown in Fig. [SI
2](#A2.F2 "Figure SI 2 ‣ B.3.1 Level I (Paper #46): Hyperparameter inconsistency in quantum error mitigation ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
given the dataset $`\mathcal{T}`$, RF constructs an ensemble of $`B`$
decision trees and averages their predictions to obtain the mitigated
expectation value,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{f}_{O}^{\mathrm{RF}}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_{b}(\bm{z}),
``` |  |

where $`T_{b}`$ denotes the prediction of the $`b`$-th decision tree.
Each tree recursively partitions the feature space by selecting a
feature and a splitting threshold that minimize the prediction error. At
each split, only a subset of candidate features
$`\mathcal{S}\subseteq[d]`$ is considered. The hyperparameter
max_features controls the number of these candidate features
$`|\mathcal{S}|`$, thereby influencing the construction of the decision
trees and their predictive performance.

shows its upper-left panel, which compares the mitigation errors of

#### B.3.2 Level I (Paper #14): Unclear Hamiltonian specification in dynamics simulation

Understanding the dynamics of interacting open quantum systems in the
thermodynamic limit is crucial for exploring nonequilibrium phases and
dissipative critical phenomena \[[232](#bib.bib232), [233](#bib.bib233),
[234](#bib.bib234)\], yet accurately simulating such dynamics remains
challenging, particularly in higher dimensions.
Paper #14 \[[201](#bib.bib201)\], published in Physical Review X in
2021, developed an infinite projected entangled pair operator (iPEPO)
algorithm that incorporates optimal bond truncation techniques, enabling
accurate simulations of correlated open-system dynamics in regimes
beyond the reach of existing methods. Here, we examine the reproduction
of its Figure 5 benchmark, where an unclear specification of the system
Hamiltonian led to substantial discrepancies between the reproduced and
reported results.

Physical model background. As reviewed in
SI. [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Classical simulation of quantum systems ‣ SI A Preliminary and literature review ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
the evolution of a Markovian open quantum system is governed by the
Lindblad master equation
$`\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})=-\imath[H,\rho_{t}]+\sum_{j}(L_{j}\rho_{t}L_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho_{t}\})`$,
where $`H`$ governs the coherent dynamics and $`L_{j}`$ are jump
operators describing the coupling to the environment. As Fig. [SI
4](#A2.F4 "Figure SI 4 ‣ B.3.2 Level I (Paper #14): Unclear Hamiltonian specification in dynamics simulation ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a
shows, Paper #14 considers a dissipative transverse-field Ising model on
an infinite two-dimensional square lattice. Its Hamiltonian and local
jump operators are given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H=\frac{V}{z}\sum_{\langle j,l\rangle}\sigma_{j}^{z}\sigma_{l}^{z}+\frac{h_{x}}{2}\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{x},\quad\textit{with}~L_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}(\sigma_{j}^{y}-\imath\sigma_{j}^{z}).
``` |  |

Here, $`\sigma_{j}^{x,y,z}`$ are Pauli operators, $`\langle j,l\rangle`$
denotes nearest-neighbor pairs, and $`z=4`$ is the lattice coordination
number. The parameters $`V`$, $`h_{x}`$, and $`\gamma`$ control the
interaction strength, transverse field, and dissipation rate,
respectively. In particular, the regime $`h_{x}=0`$ admits an exact
solution for local observables, providing a benchmark for evaluating the
accuracy of the iPEPO simulations.

#### B.3.3 Level I (Paper #38): Inconsistent measurement-cost criteria in the training of variational quantum algorithms

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) can suffer from barren plateaus,
where gradient variance decreases exponentially with the number of
qubits \[[149](#bib.bib149)\]. Paper #38 \[[216](#bib.bib216)\],
published in Nature Communications in 2021, studied when barren plateaus
arise for global and local cost functions at different circuit depths.
The authors also simulated quantum autoencoder
training \[[235](#bib.bib235)\], claiming that increasing circuit depth
slows training and raises the number of measurement shots needed to
reach a given cost threshold under a local cost function. Here, we
leverage OmniQ to reproduce this experiment, revealing an inconsistency
between the acceptance criteria based on absolute shot counts and the
optimization choices permitted by the task specification.

![Refer to caption](2611.02833v1/Fig1_paper38.png)

Figure SI 6: Overview of the reproduction task in Paper #38 for quantum
autoencoder training. a. Schematic of the quantum autoencoder, which
compresses the input quantum information into subsystem $`A`$ while
bringing subsystem $`B`$ close to the reference state
$`|0\rangle^{\otimes n_{B}}`$. The expanded circuit shows the ansatz
used in the numerical simulation, with initial RY rotations followed by
$`L`$ layers of alternating nearest-neighbor controlled-$`Z`$ gates and
RY rotations. b. Optimization settings described in the original paper
and task specification (Preliminary.md), together with the initial and
revised evaluation criteria (Criteria.md). c. Original Figure 6 of
Paper #38 \[[216](#bib.bib216)\]. The main panel shows the local cost
during training for different circuit depths. The inset shows the
cumulative number of shots needed to reach target costs 0.02 and 0.05 as
a function of circuit depth $`L`$.

Algorithmic background. A quantum autoencoder uses a variational circuit
$`V(\bm{\theta})`$ to compress an ensemble of quantum states
$`\{(p_{\mu},|\psi_{\mu}\rangle)\}_{\mu}`$, where $`p_{\mu}`$ is the
probability of state $`|\psi_{\mu}\rangle`$ and $`\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}=1`$.
As shown in Fig. [SI
6](#A2.F6 "Figure SI 6 ‣ B.3.3 Level I (Paper #38): Inconsistent measurement-cost criteria in the training of variational quantum algorithms ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
the circuit retains the encoded information in subsystem $`A`$ while
bringing subsystem $`B`$ close to a fixed reference state so that $`B`$
can be discarded. Training minimizes a cost that measures how closely
$`B`$ approaches this reference.

In Paper #38, the reference state is $`|0\rangle^{\otimes n_{B}}`$, and
the local cost is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C^{\prime}_{L}(\bm{\theta})=1-\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{B}}\text{Tr}\!\left[\left(|0\rangle\langle 0|_{j}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{j}}\right)\text{Tr}_{A}[V(\bm{\theta})\rho V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}]\right],
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\rho=\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle\langle\psi_{\mu}|`$ is
the ensemble-average input state, and $`\mathbb{I}_{\bar{j}}`$ is the
identity on all qubits in $`B`$ except $`j`$. The simulations use two
input states,
$`|\psi_{1}\rangle=|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}^{\otimes n_{B}}`$ and
$`|\psi_{2}\rangle=|1\rangle_{A}|110\cdots 0\rangle_{B}`$, with
probabilities $`p_{1}=2/3`$ and $`p_{2}=1/3`$. The authors fix
$`n_{A}=1`$ and $`n_{B}=10`$ and vary the number of circuit layers over
$`L=2,4,\ldots,20`$, using a brickwork ansatz of $`R_{y}`$ rotations and
nearest-neighbor controlled-$`Z`$ gates as shown in Fig. [SI
6](#A2.F6 "Figure SI 6 ‣ B.3.3 Level I (Paper #38): Inconsistent measurement-cost criteria in the training of variational quantum algorithms ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a.
The circuit parameters $`\bm{\theta}`$ are optimized using gradient-free
random-subspace search. Mathematically, at iteration $`t`$, a random
isometry $`A(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times d}`$ defines a
$`d=10`$-dimensional search subspace of the $`P`$ circuit parameters. An
inner optimizer then solves the following $`d`$-dimensional optimization
problem

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{s}^{*}=\arg\min_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}C^{\prime}_{L}\Big(\bm{\theta}(t)+A(t)\bm{s}\Big).
``` |  | (8) |

The parameters are then updated as
$`\bm{\theta}(t+1)=\bm{\theta}(t)+A(t)\bm{s}^{*}`$. The number of shots

#### B.3.4 Level II (Paper #42): Underspecified implementation details in topological phase characterization

Paper #42 \[[66](#bib.bib66)\], published in Nature Communications in
2026, investigates the unsupervised identification of topological order,
an important task in quantum many-body physics. The paper proposes two
quantum kernels based on reduced-state fidelities and entanglement
entropies to distinguish topologically ordered states from topologically
trivial states. In particular, they examined the robustness of these
kernels under random two-qubit unitary circuits of increasing depth.
However, the initial reproduction experiments failed to recover the
reported clustering performance despite following the methodological
description in the paper. Subsequent AI-assisted auditing identified
several consequential differences between the published description and
the released implementation, which required domain-expert intervention
to resolve.

Algorithmic background. Topological phases are characterized by nonlocal
properties, such as long-range entanglement, that cannot generally be
distinguished using conventional local order parameters. Unsupervised
topological phase characterization aims to identify different quantum
phases by clustering quantum states without using their phase labels
during learning. As shown in Fig. [SI
8](#A2.F8 "Figure SI 8 ‣ B.3.4 Level II (Paper #42): Underspecified implementation details in topological phase characterization ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
given a dataset of $`N`$ quantum states $`\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}`$,
kernel-based methods construct a similarity matrix $`K`$ with entries
$`K_{ij}=\kappa(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})`$, where $`\kappa`$ denotes a quantum
kernel. Kernel principal component analysis (PCA) then maps these states
into a low-dimensional representation that can reveal their underlying
clustering structure. To evaluate robustness against random unitary
perturbations, let $`\rho_{i}(d)`$ denote the $`i`$-th state after
applying a random circuit of depth $`d`$, and let $`K(d)`$ be the
corresponding kernel matrix. Under a standard centered kernel-PCA
procedure, the kernel matrix is first centered as $`K_{c}(d)=HK(d)H`$,
where $`H=\mathbb{I}-\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}/N`$. The leading
eigenvector $`\bm{v}_{1}(d)`$ of $`K_{c}(d)`$ is then standardized to
obtain a low-dimensional representation $`\bm{z}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}`$.
Its clustering performance can be evaluated using the silhouette
coefficient \[[67](#bib.bib67)\], i.e.,

|     |                                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s(d)=\operatorname{Silhouette}\left(\bm{z}(d),\bm{y}(d)\right),
       ```                                                              |     | (9) |

where $`\bm{y}(d)=[\bm{y}_{1}(d),\cdots,\bm{y}_{N}(d)]`$ contains the
phase labels at depth $`d`$ used for evaluation. A larger silhouette
coefficient indicates more compact and better-separated clusters.

Paper #42, quantifies the degradation of clustering performance using
the silhouette coefficient, which remains above $`0.55`$ at depth

Following the methodological description in the paper, as illustrated in

the source of these discrepancies, the auditing AI scientist in OmniQ
examined how the authors’ released implementation evaluated clustering
performance across different circuit depths. As illustrated in Fig. [SI
8](#A2.F8 "Figure SI 8 ‣ B.3.4 Level II (Paper #42): Underspecified implementation details in topological phase characterization ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")c,
the released implementation combines kernel-PCA representations from all

|     |                                                      |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s(d)=\operatorname{Silhouette}\left(X,\bm{y}\right),
       ```                                                   |     |

where $`\bm{y}=[\bm{y}(1),\cdots,\bm{y}(d)]`$ denotes the cluster labels

The AI scientist further identified a difference in the kernel-PCA

equivalent, this additional difference can also affect the resulting
clustering representations and silhouette coefficients. Together, these
implementation choices explain why following the initial task
specification does not necessarily reproduce the published numerical
results.

retained to preserve the stated correspondence between Fig. 3(a) and

evaluation for the 20 quantum states at each depth. Meanwhile, the
evaluation criteria in Criteria.md were revised to allow a $`y`$-axis
range of $`[0,1]`$ and assess the relative robustness of the two kernels
without requiring the published fidelity-kernel silhouette magnitudes.

Accordingly, this issue was classified as Level II because the
discrepancy between the published description and released
implementation affected the experimental methodology and could not be
resolved through straightforward corrections, requiring domain experts
to determine the appropriate reproduction protocol.

revised criteria, the final benchmark assesses whether AI scientists can

#### B.3.5 Level III (Paper #60): Unresolved inconsistencies in the reproduction of quantum kernel concentration

Quantum kernel methods measure similarities between classical inputs
using quantum states prepared by a data-encoding circuit.
Paper #60 \[[68](#bib.bib68)\], published in Nature Communications in

concentrate exponentially with the number of qubits, increasing the
measurement cost of resolving their differences. The authors also
investigated how encoding expressivity affects kernel concentration
through numerical simulations with different qubit counts and circuit
depths. Here, we examine the reproduction of this experiment, which
identified an inconsistency in the plotted statistic and a separate
discrepancy in the underlying data that remained unresolved.

Algorithmic background. For a classical input $`\bm{x}`$, the encoding
circuit $`U(\bm{x})`$ prepares
$`\rho(\bm{x})=U(\bm{x})\rho_{0}U(\bm{x})^{\dagger}`$ from an
$`n`$-qubit initial state $`\rho_{0}`$. Given a dataset
$`\{\bm{x}^{(i)}\}`$, the kernel Gram matrix has entries
$`K_{ij}=\kappa(\bm{x}^{(i)},\bm{x}^{(j)})`$. The fidelity quantum
kernel measures the overlap between the full encoded states,
$`\kappa^{{FQ}}(\bm{x}^{(i)},\bm{x}^{(j)})={\rm Tr}[\rho(\bm{x}^{(i)})\rho(\bm{x}^{(j)})]`$.
The projected quantum kernel compares the single-qubit reduced states
$`\rho_{k}(\bm{x})=\operatorname{Tr}_{\neq k}\rho(\bm{x})`$, as
illustrated in Fig. [SI
10](#A2.F10 "Figure SI 10 ‣ B.3.5 Level III (Paper #60): Unresolved inconsistencies in the reproduction of quantum kernel concentration ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl\|\rho_{k}(\bm{x}^{(i)})-\rho_{k}(\bm{x}^{(j)})\bigr\|_{F}^{2},\qquad\kappa^{{PQ}}_{ij}=\exp(-\gamma C_{ij}).
``` |  | (10) |

Here, $`\gamma>0`$ is a constant and $`\|\cdot\|_{F}`$ denotes the
Frobenius norm. The encoding circuit used in the Paper #60 consists of
$`L`$ hardware-efficient encoding (HEE) layers \[[236](#bib.bib236)\],
each applying data-dependent $`RX`$ rotations followed by
nearest-neighbor entangling gates. For the $`B=40`$ inputs used in the
experiment, let $`\mathcal{P}=\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq B\}`$ denote the
$`M=|\mathcal{P}|=780`$ unordered pairs of distinct inputs. The
empirical variance for either $`Z=C`$ or $`Z=\kappa^{{PQ}}`$ is defined
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{P}}[Z]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}(Z_{ij}-\bar{Z})^{2},\qquad\bar{Z}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}Z_{ij},
``` |  | (11) |

where the figure axes abbreviate the variance as
$`\operatorname{Var}[Z]`$.

## SI C Implementation of RA

In this section, we provide the implementation details of RA, whose
overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1b in the main text. We begin
with an overview of the framework of RA in
SI. [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Overview ‣ SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
followed by a detailed description of the implementation of its
components in
SI. [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Planner ‣ SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")–[C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 Experience distiller ‣ SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists").

### C.1 Overview

RA consists of five functional components: a planner, an executor, a
critic, a judge agent, and an experience distiller. These components
collaborate to reconstruct computational workflows from scientific
papers, validate reproduction results, resolve implementation issues,
and accumulate reusable experience. Specifically, the planner, executor,
and critic form the core reproduction workflow, while the judge agent
independently evaluates the completed reproduction and the experience
distiller extracts generalizable lessons from verified feedback.

Given a scientific paper, a target figure, and the corresponding task
specification, the planner decomposes the reproduction task into a set
of sub-plans with explicit dependencies, represented by a dependency
graph. Each node corresponds to a computational sub-plan, while each
edge specifies a dependency between sub-plans. The planner also
establishes shared methodological interpretations and assigns completion
requirements to individual sub-plans to maintain consistency throughout
execution.

Following this plan, the executor and critic interact iteratively to
implement and validate the sub-plans. The executor performs the required
computations, while the critic independently checks the resulting
implementations and outputs against the prescribed methodology and task
requirements. Based on its review, the critic determines whether a
sub-plan can be accepted, requires local repair, or needs a new
computational approach. This iterative process continues until the
required computations are completed or the available computational
budget is exhausted.

After execution, the judge agent independently evaluates the assembled
reproduction results against the prescribed evaluation criteria, which
are not accessible to the executor or critic. The resulting feedback,
together with execution records and verified failures, provides the
evidence from which the experience distiller extracts reusable lessons.
During development, these lessons are reviewed by domain experts before
being incorporated into the experience layer, which guides subsequent
reproduction tasks without modifying the underlying base LLM or
orchestration code. Through this process, RA combines within-task issue
resolution with experience-based self-improvement across tasks.

### C.2 Planner

The planner is responsible for translating a paper-to-result
reproduction task into a structured computational plan, which will be
passed to the executor and critic for implementation and verification.
Given the scientific paper, target figure, and task specification, it
first identifies the computational requirements needed to reproduce the
reported results and decomposes them into a set of sub-plans. Each
requirement, such as a numerical value, parameter-dependent curve, or
figure panel, is then assigned to exactly one sub-plan to ensure that
all prescribed outputs are covered.

For each sub-plan, the planner sets a computational resource budget and
defines completion criteria based on two sources. First, the planner
establishes criteria based on explicit requirements in the task
specification and derives additional criteria from the scientific claims
to be reproduced when necessary. Moreover, these criteria can be further
specified using additional information extracted from published figures
to support self-validation without treating the plotted values as
fitting targets.

The dependencies among these sub-plans are organized into a dependency
graph, which determines their execution order and enables intermediate
results to be shared across related computations. When the paper admits
multiple methodological interpretations, the planner establishes a
consistent interpretation shared by the relevant sub-plans, while
preserving experimental settings explicitly fixed by the task
specification. These shared interpretations may be revised when
supported by new evidence, with the affected sub-plans subsequently
revalidated.

### C.3 Interactive mechanism of critic and executor

The executor and critic form the core iterative mechanism of RA for
implementing, validating, and correcting computational sub-plans. As
illustrated in Fig. [SI
12](#A3.F12 "Figure SI 12 ‣ C.1 Overview ‣ SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
this mechanism operates on the dependency graph generated by the
planner, where each node represents a computational sub-plan and each
edge specifies a dependency between sub-plans. During execution, the
critic evaluates the results of individual nodes and determines whether
they can be accepted, require local repair, or need a new computational
approach. These decisions guide the progression of the dependency graph,
with additional diagnostic branches introduced when necessary. In this
way, the reproduction process can be viewed as an adaptive execution
graph that progressively resolves computational sub-plans through
validation and issue resolution.

Sub-plan execution and validation. Following the dependency graph, the
executor implements each sub-plan once its prerequisite nodes have been
accepted. Each executor operates in an independent session and receives
the corresponding task requirements, shared methodological
interpretations, and outputs from preceding sub-plans. Before performing
full-scale computations, the executor validates its implementation on
the smallest exactly solvable instance and estimates the computational
cost by timing a representative step at the largest required system
size. These checks help identify implementation errors and assess
computational feasibility before committing substantial resources. For
computationally demanding tasks, long-running calculations are launched
as background jobs tracked by the orchestrator, allowing the executor
session to terminate without interrupting the computation. The
orchestrator subsequently resumes the executor when the job completes or
stalls, without requiring continuous calls to the base LLM. To ensure
reproducibility, all reported numerical results must be traceable to the
corresponding code and data saved on disk.

Critic review and decision routing. The critic independently reviews the
computational results generated by each executor before accepting the
corresponding sub-plan. Unlike the judge agent, which evaluates the
completed reproduction against the benchmark criteria, the critic only
accesses the scientific paper, task specification, and execution
records. Its review focuses on three aspects: (i) result provenance,
verifying that reported numerical values are supported by the generated
code and data; (ii) methodological correctness, checking whether the
implementation follows the prescribed algorithms and experimental
settings; and (iii) task completion, assessing whether all requirements
of the sub-plan have been satisfied. These checks are primarily
conducted through inspection, supplemented by small verification
experiments when necessary. The critic can also inspect ongoing
computations and identify potential failures before they finish.

Based on these checks, the critic assigns each sub-plan to one of three
outcomes: (i) Pass, when all requirements are satisfied and no verified
defects remain, allowing dependent sub-plans to proceed; (ii) Local
repair, when an identified defect can be corrected within the remaining
computational budget; and (iii) New approach, when the defect cannot be
reliably resolved through local correction or requires a different
computational strategy. A passing node is marked as completed, making
its validated outputs available to dependent nodes in the graph. In
contrast, the latter two outcomes trigger different corrective branches,
as described below.

Local repair. When a defect has a known correction, the critic returns
the corresponding sub-plan to the executor with specific feedback
identifying the error and the required changes. The executor then
modifies the existing implementation and resubmits its results for
another critic review. This procedure allows the same node to be
corrected without changing its dependencies or restarting the entire
computational workflow. The repair process continues until the sub-plan
passes the review or further local correction is deemed ineffective.
Importantly, results containing fabricated or untraceable numerical
values are not eligible for ordinary repair, since their computational
provenance cannot be established.

Experiment-driven diagnosis and new approach. When local repair is
insufficient, either because the source of the defect remains unclear or
because repeated corrections have failed, the critic initiates an
experiment-driven diagnosis. This process introduces three temporary
diagnostic branches associated with the failed node, each handled by an
independent probe agent. These agents examine the failed sub-plan and
its residual, consisting of verified defects and reusable computational
artifacts. Each probe agent independently proposes a possible cause of
the failure and tests its hypothesis through a small numerical
experiment.

The resulting diagnoses are evaluated by a critic panel consisting of
three independent critic sessions. Before selecting a solution, the
panel excludes proposals that violate fixed task requirements,
substitute prescribed methodological components, or rely solely on
improved agreement with the published figure without independent
validation. The remaining diagnoses are ranked by majority vote, and the
highest-ranked diagnosis forms the basis of a new computational
approach, potentially incorporating compatible elements from other
proposals. A fresh executor then implements this approach using the
archived code and residual, with its results subjected to another round
of critic review. These diagnostic branches support the resolution of
the original failed node without changing the scientific requirements or
dependency structure of the reproduction task.

Dependency-aware revalidation. The three-way decision mechanism allows
RA to progressively complete the reproduction task specified by the
dependency graph while correcting issues at individual nodes. Beyond
these local decisions, changes to shared methodological interpretations
may affect multiple previously accepted sub-plans. When such a change is
supported by new evidence, the orchestrator reopens the sub-plans that
used the shared information, and the critic reassesses their validity.
Results that remain correct are retained, while affected computations
undergo local repair or a new approach. This dependency-aware
revalidation maintains consistency across the computational workflow
without unnecessarily repeating completed work.

In exceptional cases where the prescribed requirements of a sub-plan are
contradictory or cannot be satisfied, the critic may retire the affected
node. If this prevents subsequent sub-plans from proceeding, the planner
may revise the dependency graph to determine whether the remaining
requirements can still be completed without violating the task
specification. Throughout execution, all components operate under a
shared computational budget, with part of the available time reserved
for saving results and preparing the final report. Once all required
sub-plans have passed the critic’s review, their outputs are assembled
into the final reproduction artifacts for independent evaluation by the
judge agent. Together, these procedures enable RA to combine independent
validation, iterative issue resolution, and coordinated revisions within
a reliable computational reproduction workflow.

### C.4 Judge agent

The Judge agent is used to evaluate the final reproduction results
generated by RA. Once the reproduction run is complete, it receives the
task specification, evaluation criteria, and execution workspace,
including the source code, logs, numerical data, figures, and final
report. Only the Judge has access to the evaluation criteria, while the
Planner, Executor, and Critic do not receive it during task execution.

For each target figure, the Judge first checks that all required panels
and curves are present over the specified parameter range. It then
traces the plotted values to the saved data and code to verify how they
were calculated. The implementation is checked against the task
specification, including the definitions of observables, experimental
parameters, and required computational steps. Any permitted
approximations or choices of convention are checked against the
description in the report. The Judge then compares the saved numerical
results with the prescribed checkpoints and assesses whether the curves
reproduce the expected performance rankings and trends.

The Judge records any issues found in this assessment together with
evidence from the code, data, or execution logs. Each issue is
classified as critical or non-critical under the OmniQ protocol.
Critical issues affect scientific correctness or leave a required part
of the reproduction incomplete, such as an incorrect computational
procedure, an unsupported numerical value, a missing required curve, or
a result outside a mandatory tolerance. Non-critical issues concern
presentation differences, such as colors or legend placement, that leave
the scientific content unchanged. A figure passes when no critical issue
is found. A task is counted as successful when all its target figures
pass.

### C.5 Experience distiller

The experience distiller enables RA to learn from previously encountered
failures by converting verified issue resolutions into reusable guidance
for subsequent reproduction tasks. It operates outside individual
reproduction runs and is invoked after each round of development
experiments. Importantly, the distiller updates only the experience
layer, while the base LLM, agent roles, and orchestration code remain
unchanged. To extract useful experience, it examines the judge agent’s
feedback and the complete execution records, including the computational
plans, critic reviews, and implementations of individual sub-plans.

The distilled experience is organized into three types of memory:
planner memory, critic memory, and residual memory, as illustrated in
Fig. [SI
13](#A3.F13 "Figure SI 13 ‣ C.4 Judge agent ‣ SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists").
The planner memory provides guidance for constructing reliable
computational plans, such as scheduling validation on small, exactly
solvable instances before full-scale computation and prioritizing tasks
essential to the paper’s central claims. The critic memory improves the
identification of implementation issues by emphasizing checks on
numerical provenance, methodological correctness, and consistency with
the reported results. The residual memory stores generalizable failure
signatures, each consisting of three elements: a description of the
failure, a diagnostic check for identifying it, and a corresponding
remedy. These checks are derived from how the critic or judge agent
identified the issue, while the remedies are extracted from verified
corrections that subsequently led to successful reproduction.

During task execution, relevant entries in the residual memory are
retrieved to guide issue resolution. For example, when the critic
requests a local repair, it identifies the entries corresponding to the
verified defect, which are then provided to the executor to guide the
correction. Similar guidance is supplied to a fresh executor when a new
computational approach is required. This mechanism allows experience
accumulated from previous failures to inform subsequent decisions
without modifying the underlying execution framework.

To maintain the reliability and generalizability of the accumulated
experience, the distiller excludes lessons specific to individual papers
and merges those that overlap with existing entries. It can propose
additions, modifications, or deletions across all three memories, but
every update must be reviewed and approved by a human expert before
being incorporated into the experience layer. In our implementation, the
memories were developed exclusively from five development tasks,
resulting in ten entries in the residual memory. The resulting
experience layer was then fixed throughout evaluation on the remaining
54 unseen tasks, allowing us to assess whether the distilled experience
generalizes beyond the tasks used for its development.

## SI D An autonomous platform for quantum computational science

To make computational quantum science more accessible and reusable, we
develop an autonomous research platform that integrates OmniQ in
SI [B](#A2 "SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
RA in
SI [C](#A3 "SI C Implementation of RA ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
and the Model Context Protocol (MCP). The platform enables researchers
and AI agents to reproduce published results and build upon previously
verified computational methods. Specifically, it provides two core
functionalities:

1.  1.
    Reproducing new research. Automatically reproducing computational
    results from user-submitted scientific papers.
2.  2.
    Reusing existing algorithms. Discovering and applying previously
    reproduced algorithms through simple MCP queries.

Below, we briefly describe how these two functionalities are implemented
and accessed. More details are provided in
[https://omniq-qid-lab.github.io/](https://omniq-qid-lab.github.io/).

Reproducing new research. Building on the OmniQ framework, the platform
integrates task specification, AI-driven computation, independent
verification, and result delivery into a unified workflow. In
particular, a user begins by submitting a scientific paper and defining
a research objective, such as reproducing a published figure or
investigating the same method under new experimental parameters. An
analysis agent translates this request into two documents: a task
specification that guides the AI scientist and evaluation criteria that
define the requirements for successful reproduction. The user reviews
and confirms both documents before execution, while the evaluation
criteria remain inaccessible to the AI scientist.

Once the task is confirmed, the user selects an AI scientist, such as RA
or a first-party coding agent, together with the underlying LLM and
computational budget. The platform executes the task in an isolated
computational environment and independently evaluates the resulting
code, data, and figures against the predefined criteria.

Upon completion, the user receives an evaluation report and can download
the source code, generated data, reproduced figures, and a runnable
container. This workflow enables researchers to inspect, reproduce, and
extend computational results without reconstructing the experimental
environment.

Reusing existing algorithms. Beyond reproducing individual papers, the
platform organizes successfully reproduced algorithms into a shared
collection of verified computational workflows. To establish their
reliability, successful reproductions can undergo additional
verification using a fixed judge configuration, with optional review by
domain experts.

The platform is designed to issue digitally signed certificates linking
each verified workflow to its source paper, evaluation criteria, AI
scientist configuration, and executable results. These certificates
establish the reproducibility of the specified computational results
without making claims about the scientific validity of the original
paper.

Researchers and external AI agents can access these workflows through a
web interface or MCP. In particular, MCP enables an AI agent to search
for relevant algorithms using natural-language queries, retrieve their
implementations and experimental settings, and incorporate them into new
research tasks. As such, researchers can directly build upon existing,
verified implementations, reducing redundant computational effort and
facilitating subsequent scientific investigations.

Extensibility. The platform adopts a modular architecture that supports
integration with different AI scientists, underlying LLMs, computational
environments, and scientific evaluation protocols.

Although initially developed for computational quantum science, the
framework can be extended to other computational disciplines by adapting
their task specifications, evaluation criteria, and computational
resources. The 59 reproductions from this study provide the initial
collection of workflows, which can expand as new papers are reproduced
and verified. We have implemented a prototype web interface and MCP tool
definitions, with deployment of the full public service planned.

3](#A5.T3 "Table SI 3 ‣ E.1 Additional results of AI scientists on the established computational quantum science dataset ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
mismatches with the original paper are the most prevalent failure
category across all harnesses, affecting $`24-42`$ runs per
configuration. However, the failure patterns vary considerably.
InternAgent fails to produce a report in 13 of its 43 unsuccessful runs,
suggesting difficulties in completing the reproduction workflow. In
contrast, live-swe-agent exhibits implementation errors in 22 of its 41
failed runs, while Meta-Harness reduces the original problem settings in
18 of its 48 failed runs. Notably, these failure categories are not
mutually exclusive, as a single run may exhibit multiple issues.
Collectively, these results highlight the importance of harness design
in scientific reproduction and suggest that greater architectural
complexity does not necessarily translate into higher reproduction
performance.

### E.2 Case studies of RA

In this subsection, we examine how RA identifies and corrects
reproduction failures through three representative case studies from
Paper #2, Paper #35, and Paper #29. On these tasks, DSH and most
third-party harnesses evaluated with DeepSeek V4 Pro failed to reproduce
the reported results, whereas RA succeeded. Notably, none of the three
papers had encountered reproduction issues in the curation audit in
Table [SI
2](#A2.T2 "Table SI 2 ‣ B.3 Analysis of paper-to-result reproduction issues ‣ SI B Implementation details of OmniQ ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists"),
allowing us to focus on failures in agent execution rather than known
inconsistencies in the scientific record. In each case, RA identified
discrepancies between intermediate results and the methodological or
quantitative requirements of the original paper, subsequently diagnosing
and correcting the underlying errors without altering the prescribed
task settings. These cases illustrate three complementary mechanisms of
RA, namely sub-plan decomposition, critic-guided issue resolution, and
experiment-driven diagnosis, which we examine separately below.

Case study 1 (Paper #2): Sub-plan decomposition. This case illustrates
how RA uses sub-plan decomposition to overcome a reproduction challenge
that other AI scientists failed to resolve, by ensuring consistent
methodological interpretations and independent validation across
computational sub-plans.

Reproduction challenge and baseline failure.
Paper #2 \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] investigated the classical simulation of
IBM’s 127-qubit kicked Ising experiment using tensor networks contracted
through belief propagation, with simulation errors evaluated against an
exact light-cone benchmark. As shown in Fig. [SI
14](#A5.F14 "Figure SI 14 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a,
the circuit consists of repeated Trotter steps combining fixed Ising
interactions with single-qubit $`X`$ rotations parameterized by an angle
$`\theta_{h}`$, where $`\theta_{h}=\pi/2`$ corresponds to a Clifford
circuit that provides an exact reference point. A valid comparison
requires the tensor-network estimate $`\langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{TN}}`$
and the exact benchmark $`\langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{exact}}`$ to
evaluate the same physical observable $`O`$. In particular, the
weight-17 Pauli observable $`O_{17}`$ in Figure 3(c) of the original
paper is constructed by propagating a single-site $`Z`$ operator
backward through several Clifford steps, requiring the correct qubit and
number of steps to be identified. However, DSH using the same base LLM
failed to maintain this consistency. It initially simulated Figure 3(c)
using a qubit selected during an early search, but subsequently
discovered that this choice generated a weight-23 observable $`O_{23}`$.
Although DSH then computed the exact benchmark for the correct
observable, the corresponding simulation was not completed before the
run ended. Consequently, its final figure incorrectly compared
$`\braket{O_{23}}_{\mathrm{TN}}`$ with
$`\braket{O_{17}}_{\mathrm{exact}}`$, resulting in substantially larger
errors than those reported in the paper.

Sub-plan decomposition and reproduction results. RA identified the
correct target observable before execution and decomposed the task into
five independently validated sub-plans, using shared methodological
interpretations to maintain consistency across their computations. In
particular, the exact benchmark was implemented independently of the
simulation engine, while both evaluated the same observable. When the
full-scale simulation revealed a separate error in the
belief-propagation implementation, RA coordinated corrections across the
affected sub-plans while retaining the validated exact benchmark.

The reproduction results are presented in Fig. [SI
14](#A5.F14 "Figure SI 14 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")b.
DSH reported errors ranging from approximately $`10^{-3}`$ to
$`10^{-1}`$, several orders of magnitude larger than the published
reference values at many rotation angles. As discussed above, these
discrepancies resulted from comparing different observables rather than
directly reflecting the accuracy of the intended tensor-network
simulation. By contrast, the initial RA reproduction already achieved
substantially smaller errors, although noticeable deviations from the
published results remained at intermediate rotation angles, particularly
around $`\theta_{h}=3\pi/8`$. Following the coordinated corrections, the
final RA results more closely reproduced both the reported error
magnitudes and their dependence on $`\theta_{h}`$, including the peak
near $`\theta_{h}=3\pi/8`$ and the sharp decrease towards the Clifford
point $`\theta_{h}=\pi/2`$. Quantitatively, the final results agreed
with the published error values to within approximately $`2\%`$ at 15 of
the 23 evaluated points. These results illustrate how sub-plan
decomposition and coordinated correction enabled RA to recover the
reported simulation behavior that DSH failed to reproduce.

Case study 2 (Paper #35). Critic-guided issue resolution. In this case,
RA corrected an underestimated oracle-call cost through critic-guided
local repair: the critic identified an invalid circuit schedule, the
executor corrected the implementation, and the critic reviewed the
revised results before accepting the affected sub-plan.

Background and baseline failure. Paper #35 \[[128](#bib.bib128)\]
develops low-depth algorithms for estimating the angle $`\theta`$
encoded by a state-preparation oracle $`U`$, i.e.,
$`U|0\rangle^{\otimes n}=\cos\theta\,|\psi_{0}\rangle|0\rangle+\sin\theta\,|\psi_{1}\rangle|1\rangle`$
, where $`0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2`$, and
$`\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,|\psi_{1}\rangle\}`$ are normalized states of the
remaining register. The task is to estimate $`\theta`$ within additive
error $`\epsilon`$ using the procedure illustrated in Fig. [SI
15](#A5.F15 "Figure SI 15 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a.
Specifically, each amplification step applies
$`Q=UR_{0}U^{\dagger}S_{0}`$, where
$`R_{0}=2|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\langle 0|^{\otimes n}-\mathbb{I}_{2^{n}}`$
and $`S_{0}=\mathbb{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes Z`$. Each application of $`Q`$
uses two oracle calls of $`U`$. In this amplitude estimation procedure,
the number of amplitude amplification steps $`m_{k}`$ increases across
measurement rounds $`k=1,2,\cdots`$ according to a power law
$`m_{k}=\lfloor k^{(1-\beta)/(2\beta)}\rfloor`$ with a tunable exponent,
which allows a tradeoff between circuit depth and total oracle calls.
The reproduction targets are the relationships between the number of
oracle calls $`N`$ and the estimation error $`\epsilon`$ shown in
Figures 5 and 6 of the original paper, for noiseless circuits and
circuits with depolarizing noise at different decay rates $`\gamma`$,
respectively. With schedules adapted to the target error and noise
level, estimation at $`\epsilon\gg\gamma`$ retains approximately the
quantum scaling $`N\propto\epsilon^{-1}`$. At finer precision with
$`\epsilon\ll\gamma`$, the measurement contrast is suppressed by
$`e^{-\gamma q}`$ after $`q`$ oracle calls. More measurements are then
needed, and the cost approaches the classical scaling
$`N\propto\epsilon^{-2}`$. The crossover error $`\epsilon_{C}`$ marks
the transition between these scaling regimes and is expected to be of
order $`\gamma`$. DSH reproduced the noiseless relation but obtained a
noisy crossover at $`\epsilon_{C}\approx 0.05\gamma`$, well below this
scale, as shown in Fig. [SI
15](#A5.F15 "Figure SI 15 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")c.
Although it documented this discrepancy, DSH incorrectly declared the
noisy reproduction successful. The judge agent marked the noisy target
figure as failed because the crossover fell outside the prescribed
evaluation interval $`[0.3\gamma,3\gamma]`$.

Critic-guided issue resolution. At the initial stage, RA also
underestimated the cost in the noise-dominated regime. The executor
attributed the discrepancy to the published curve, but the critic
checked the recorded circuit depths against the original method. In the
power-law algorithm, these depths depend on $`\beta`$. For a requested
error $`\epsilon<\gamma`$, the requirement
$`\beta\geq 1-\log\gamma/\log(\epsilon/c)`$ holds, where $`c`$ is a
constant. In the initial simulation, the executor selected the circuit
sequence requiring the fewest oracle calls among candidates designed for
other error values, without enforcing this bound at the requested
precision. The recorded parameters confirmed violations of the bound,
and exponential schedules were retained below the expected crossover.
The critic therefore identified the circuit-selection rule as a
methodological defect and returned the affected sub-plan to the executor
for local repair.

Following this review, the executor rebuilt each noisy-regime schedule
with $`\beta`$ at the bound for the requested $`(\epsilon,\gamma)`$,
keeping $`c=1`$ throughout, and recomputed the affected results. The
corrected schedule rule was recorded as a revised shared methodological
interpretation, and the critic reviewed the recomputed results before
accepting the affected sub-plan. As shown in Fig. [SI
15](#A5.F15 "Figure SI 15 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")b,
the correction increased the estimated cost by roughly an order of
magnitude near $`\epsilon\sim 10^{-4}`$, while the costs at large target
errors remained unchanged. Across all three noise strengths, the
schedule switches in Fig. [SI
15](#A5.F15 "Figure SI 15 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")c
moved from below the evaluation interval to within it. The judge agent
evaluated the completed reproduction and marked both target figures as
passed.

Case study 3 (Paper #29): Experiment-driven diagnosis. This case
illustrates how critic corrects failures in individual subplans by first
analyzing their objectives and intermediate results to formulate
diagnostic hypotheses, then deploying probing agents to test these
hypotheses through small-scale experiments and guide targeted
corrections.

Background and baseline failure. Paper #29 \[[136](#bib.bib136)\] aims
to improve the performance of parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) by
classically pretraining a matrix product state (MPS) and converting it
into a quantum circuit as the initial ansatz for subsequent
optimization. More concretely, the proposed method first uses MPS
pretraining to obtain an MPS that closely approximates the target state
and then performs MPS-to-circuit conversion to transfer the pretrained
state into a PQC with minimal fidelity loss. As Fig. [SI
16](#A5.F16 "Figure SI 16 ‣ E.2 Case studies of RA ‣ SI E More experimental results ‣ Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists")a
shows, both DSH with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and RA with the same base LLM
failed at MPS pretraining and MPS-to-circuit conversion in their initial
attempts. Specifically, the KL divergence between the pretrained MPS and
the target state for both methods remained above $`1`$ after $`50`$
training sweeps, far exceeding the target range of $`[0.015,0.03]`$.
Similarly, the MPS-to-circuit conversion achieved transfer fidelities of
only $`0.9641`$ for DSH and $`0.9777`$ for RA, both below the required
0.990. These discrepancies ultimately prevented successful reproduction
of the PQC training results reported in the original paper.

Experiment-driven correction. By retrieving relevant knowledge from its
residual memory, the critic hypothesized that the failures in both MPS
pretraining and MPS-to-circuit conversion could arise from
implementation errors or insufficient variational optimization. To
investigate these possibilities, the critic deployed three probing
agents for each subplan to test each hypothesis from small-scale
experiments, as shown in Fig. [SI

For MPS pretraining, two probing agents independently identified errors
in the tensor contractions used for gradient computation by comparing
independently computed reference gradients with those produced by the
original code. For MPS-to-circuit conversion, one probing agent found
that the original implementation failed to correctly follow the
truncation-and-disentangling procedure for constructing circuit layers
prescribed in the paper. Based on majority voting on these findings, RA
corrected the errors in both subplans and finally reproduced the
performance curves reported in the original paper correctly, as
illustrated in Fig. [SI
