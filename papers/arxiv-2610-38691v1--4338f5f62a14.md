###### Abstract

Verifying numerical claims in research written by AI agents is an
important but challenging problem. Agents can now run experiments and
write complete papers, while organizations increasingly rely on their
reported results. Recent benchmarks show that many agent-written papers
fail integrity checks, but such verdicts, often produced by a single
large language model (LLM) judge, identify failure without necessarily
explaining its nature. Existing approaches either check a paper for
internal consistency or test whether a reported value appears somewhere
in an agent’s output. Neither establishes whether the value comes from
the experiment it is claimed to describe.

We re-analyze all 540 released judgments from a recent public benchmark
of AI research agents, covering nine agents and fifteen open research
problems, at the level of individual rubric criteria. We then manually
label 256 numerical claims from 18 submissions, distinguishing claims
with no supporting source in the available artifacts from values drawn
from the wrong experiment, metric, or run. Failures concentrate on
alignment between the paper and the underlying work rather than overt
cheating. Among unsupported numerical claims in the labeled sample,
approximately four in five correspond to values in the agent’s files but
lack support from the appropriate source, indicating that misattribution
is a major failure mode in this benchmark.

To address this problem, we introduce a deterministic claim-to-evidence
checker that scores whether the surrounding context identifies the
appropriate result and calibrates each claim against nearby numerical
decoys. With its operating rules fixed before any claims were labeled,
the checker certifies claims with over 90% precision against blinded
human annotations (78 of 85 certified claims traceable, including 27 of
29 on held-out submissions; Cohen’s $`\kappa=0.89`$), while verifying
only a subset of claims. An LLM verifier given the same evidence
recovers more traceable claims but accepts about three times as many
misattributed ones, so the two are best used together. Most claims it
cannot verify are nevertheless judged traceable by human annotators,
demonstrating why failure to verify should not be treated as evidence of
falsehood. Applied to 99 submissions, the checker reveals substantial
differences in numerical traceability across agents. A second judge from
a different vendor preserves the official judge’s agent ranking while
exhibiting substantially higher pass rates, showing that absolute
integrity outcomes depend on judge choice even when relative rankings
are more stable. These findings motivate provenance-first auditing of
AI-produced research and analytical reports.

## 1 Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents have moved from answering questions to
doing extended analytical work: they write and run code, execute
experiments, and report the results in documents that look like finished
research ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib33); [Chen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib7); [Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)). For
organizations, this changes where the risk sits. A human analyst’s
report can be questioned by the person who wrote it; an agent’s report
is often read by people who never see the code, the logs, or the
intermediate files behind it. The decisions that follow—whether to fund
a direction, adopt a method, or publish a result—rest on the numbers in
the report. Whether those numbers can be traced to the work that
supposedly produced them is therefore a data-quality and data-provenance
question ([Wang and Strong, 1996](#bib.bib58); [Simmhan et al.,
2005](#bib.bib48)), not only a question about model capability.

Recent benchmarks make the scale of the problem visible. On _Into the
Unknown_ ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), nine agents each attempted
fifteen open research problems drawn from recent papers, and an LLM
judge audited every submission for integrity before assessing its
scientific merit. Only 21 of the 135 submissions passed the integrity
gate. Commentary on such results tends to describe the failures as
fabrication: agents inventing data or results that were never produced.
That description matters, because it implies a particular
remedy—detecting invented numbers—and a particular level of distrust.

Two features of the evidence make that description premature. First, the
verdicts come from a single LLM judge from one vendor, and LLM judges
are known to be sensitive to presentation and, in some settings, to
favor outputs resembling their own ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib62);
[Panickssery et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)). Second, an integrity verdict is
an aggregate: it records _that_ a submission failed, not _how_. A paper
whose reported accuracy was never computed and a paper whose reported
accuracy was computed but under a different seed, split, or model both
fail, yet they call for different responses. The first is fabrication;
the second is misreporting—the number exists, but the report attaches it
to the wrong experiment.

This paper asks which of the two dominates, and what it takes to tell
them apart at scale. We study four questions:

1.  RQ1
    What kinds of integrity failures does the official judge record, and
    do they point to fabrication or to misalignment between write-up and
    work?
2.  RQ2
    When a number in an agent’s paper is not supported, is it absent
    from the agent’s files, or present but drawn from the wrong source?
3.  RQ3
    Can numerical claims in AI-generated research papers be traced
    automatically to their supporting execution artifacts with useful
    precision, and without treating claims that cannot be verified as
    false?
4.  RQ4
    How robust are the benchmark’s integrity conclusions to the choice
    of judge?

To answer them, we combine four sources of evidence on the publicly
released benchmark data. We re-analyze all 540 released judgments at the
level of individual rubric criteria. We collect human labels for 256
numerical claims drawn from 18 submissions, using a development set and
a separate, untouched confirmation set labeled independently by two
annotators. We build a deterministic claim-to-evidence checker that
matches each number in a paper against the agent’s own result files and,
crucially, tests whether the surrounding context identifies the right
source rather than merely the right value. Finally, we re-grade a
stratified sample of 30 submissions with a judge from a different
vendor, using the benchmark’s own grading pipeline.

The evidence points consistently in one direction. The criteria that
fail are those checking whether the write-up matches the work
(code–paper alignment, number traceability), whereas criteria that would
catch overt cheating (hard-coded values, external code, test-set
leakage) almost never fire. In the human-labeled sample, roughly four in
five unsupported numbers exist in the agent’s own files but belong to a
different experiment, metric, or run; numbers with no source at all are
a minority. In this benchmark, in other words, misattribution of real
numbers is a major failure mode, and outright absence of a source is the
minority case.

This finding has a methodological consequence. If misreported numbers
exist in the files, then checking whether a reported value appears
anywhere in an agent’s output—the obvious automated test—cannot detect
them: in our data it accepts 87–95% of misattributed numbers.
Verification has to establish provenance, not just presence. Our checker
does this by scoring how well the surrounding context identifies a
candidate source and calibrating each claim against nearby decoy values.
Its strict operating point was fixed before any claims were labeled, so
every human label is evaluation data for it; we also confirm it on
held-out submissions drawn after all development work (two annotators,
$`\kappa=0.89`$ on 48 double-labeled claims). Across all labeled data,
78 of the 85 claims it certified were judged traceable (92%, exact 95%
CI 84–97%), and 27 of 29 on the held-out sample, and it certified about
a quarter of all evaluated claims (23% coverage). Its recall is
deliberately limited, and this matters for how it is used: among the
claims it did not certify, a majority were nevertheless judged traceable
by the annotators (57% in the confirmation sample and 72% in the
development sample). Treating “not verified” as “false” would therefore
wrongly reject more claims than it correctly flags. The checker is a
conservative certifier: a positive result is strong evidence of
provenance, while a negative result routes a claim to human review
rather than counting as evidence of misconduct. Certification
establishes that a number is supported by the recorded artifacts, not
that the underlying experiment is scientifically sound.

## 2 Related work

This paper connects five literatures that have so far developed largely
in parallel: the evaluation of AI research agents; studies of how such
agents fail, and in particular how they misrepresent their own work; the
attribution and verification of generated text; the checking of reported
numbers in science; and the use of LLMs as judges. A sixth, the
information-systems literature on data quality, provenance, and trust in
automation, supplies the frame for treating agent reports as data
products whose fitness for use must be established before they inform
decisions. We review each in turn and close with a structured comparison
(Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.7 Positioning ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents"))
that locates our contribution.

### 2.1 From coding assistants to research agents

Benchmarks for LLM agents have moved steadily up the research pipeline.
Early agent benchmarks measured whether agents could resolve software
issues ([Jimenez et al., 2024](#bib.bib23)) or run machine-learning
experiments in sandboxed environments ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)). Later work evaluated end-to-end machine-learning
engineering ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)), compared agents against
human experts on open-ended research-engineering tasks ([Wijk et al.,
2024](#bib.bib59)), and assessed data-driven discovery across scientific
disciplines ([Chen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib9); [Jansen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)). A parallel line examines whether agents can
reproduce published work, either computationally from released code
([Siegel et al., 2024](#bib.bib47)) or from the paper alone ([Starace
et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)). At the generative end, LLMs have been shown
to propose research ideas that experts judge more novel, though less
feasible, than their own ([Si et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)), and systems
such as the AI Scientist ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib33); [Yamada et al.,
2025](#bib.bib60)) go further, producing complete manuscripts with
experiments, figures, and reviews.

Two recent benchmarks shift the target from reproducing known results to
producing new ones. MLR-Bench evaluates agents on open-ended
machine-learning research with an LLM judge ([Chen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib7)). _Into the Unknown_ ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib24))
asks agents to write follow-up papers to recent work in quantum
computing, plasma physics, computational chemistry, materials science,
and scientific machine learning, and is distinctive in gating every
scientific assessment behind an integrity audit. Because it releases the
full file system of every run alongside the judge’s node-level verdicts,
it is also the first benchmark on which the provenance of each reported
number can be examined directly. This is the property our study
exploits.

### 2.2 How research agents misrepresent their work

As capability has risen, so has evidence that agents’ reports do not
faithfully describe what the agents did. An independent evaluation of
the AI Scientist found manuscripts containing hallucinated numerical
results and experiments that did not support their stated conclusions
([Beel et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). MLR-Bench reports that coding agents
produce fabricated or invalidated experimental results in about 80% of
the cases it inspected ([Chen et al., 2025a](#bib.bib7)), and the _Into
the Unknown_ authors attribute most of their 114 integrity failures to
claimed work that was never done or to numbers that contradict the
recorded evidence ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). Two 2026 case
studies point to selective rather than invented reporting. In
InnovationEval, agents ran many near-identical training runs, submitted
the best result per dataset, and disclosed the selection only in passing
or not at all, although their own transcripts show awareness of the
practice; once out-of-scope selection was removed, little of the claimed
improvement remained ([Owen, 2026](#bib.bib38)). In a shadow evaluation
on unpublished conference submissions, agents handled the engineering
competently but made little scientific progress, and both resulting
papers were rejected by the original authors ([Kirgis et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)).

A broader literature on agent integrity examines behavior during a run.
Agents tamper with evaluation code or leak held-out data when given the
opportunity ([Atinafu and Cohen, 2026](#bib.bib1)), exploit test cases
when tasks are made impossible ([Zhong et al., 2026](#bib.bib63)), and
pass benchmarks through exploits that the benchmark designers did not
anticipate ([Luo et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)). Language models can
strategically misreport their own actions under pressure ([Scheurer
et al., 2023](#bib.bib45)), and honesty—whether a model states what it
believes—can be measured separately from accuracy ([Ren et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44)). Even without incentives to deceive, models’ stated
reasoning need not reflect the process that produced their answers
([Turpin et al., 2023](#bib.bib53); [Lanham et al., 2023](#bib.bib27)),
and research agents may defend mechanisms that their own data contradict
([Eulig, 2026](#bib.bib14)).

Two gaps run through this body of work. First, the evidence that agent
reports are unreliable comes almost entirely from expert review or a
single LLM judge, validated, where at all, at the level of whole
submissions; we know of no study that checks integrity verdicts claim by
claim against blinded human labels, or tests them under a judge from a
different vendor. Second, the studies do not separate two failure modes
with different causes and remedies: _fabrication_, where a reported
number was never produced, and _misattribution_, where it was produced
but is reported for the wrong experiment, metric, or run. The
selective-reporting evidence from InnovationEval suggests that the
second may be common, but no study has measured the split. Our work does
so at the level of individual numerical claims.

### 2.3 Attribution and provenance of generated content

The question of whether generated text is supported by a source has been
studied extensively under the name of attribution. [Rashkin et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib43) formalize when a statement is “attributable to an
identified source” and show that human judgments of attribution can be
made reliably. Systems that generate text with citations are evaluated
by citation precision and recall ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)), and
an audit of commercial generative search engines found that only about
half of generated sentences were fully supported by their citations
([Liu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib31)). Our problem is structurally the
same—is a reported value supported by an identified source?—but the
sources differ. Attribution research assumes a small set of retrieved
passages written for humans; we face thousands of machine-written result
entries, many containing the same value for different quantities. The
difficulty is less in judging support than in establishing _which_ entry
a claim refers to.

Closest to our approach, a recent survey of AI-scientist systems
identifies verification as the field’s binding constraint, noting that
released systems seldom expose the seeds, traces, and intermediate
outputs needed to check a result ([Ding et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)), and
[Yin et al. (2026)](#bib.bib61) propose that scientific agents emit
artifact-centered, claim-aware event traces, sketching a checker that
would compare a manuscript’s numbers with experiment logs. Their
contribution is a design specification: the checker is described but not
implemented or evaluated. We implement the check, validate it against
blinded human labels on held-out data, and show that it works on agent
outputs as they are, without requiring agents to emit special traces.
Our work also builds on the data-provenance tradition in databases and
e-science ([Simmhan et al., 2005](#bib.bib48); [Herschel et al.,
2017](#bib.bib19)), its standardization in the W3C PROV model ([Moreau
and Missier, 2013](#bib.bib36)), and documentation practices for
machine-learning artifacts ([Gebru et al., 2021](#bib.bib16)), all of
which treat the derivation of a data item as a first-class property that
must be recorded to be trusted.

### 2.4 Checking reported numbers and claims

Automated checks of reported numbers predate LLMs. In psychology,
recomputing $`p`$-values from reported test statistics revealed
widespread inconsistencies ([Bakker and Wicherts, 2011](#bib.bib2)); the
statcheck tool scaled this to roughly 250,000 $`p`$-values and found at
least one inconsistency in about half of the papers examined ([Nuijten
et al., 2016](#bib.bib37)). The GRIM test flags reported means that are
impossible given the sample size ([Brown and Heathers,
2017](#bib.bib5)). Studies of reproducibility in AI and computational
science show how rarely published results can be regenerated from the
information provided, and call for code, data, and workflow disclosure
([Gundersen and Kjensmo, 2018](#bib.bib18); [Pineau et al.,
2021](#bib.bib41); [Stodden et al., 2016](#bib.bib51)). LLMs now
contribute to the scientific record directly—their use in writing
scientific papers has risen sharply ([Liang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib30))—and their tendency to fabricate details, from
citations ([Walters and Wilder, 2023](#bib.bib56)) to factual content
more broadly ([Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), is well documented.

Claim verification in natural language processing approaches related
problems with learned models. Benchmarks pair claims with evidence from
encyclopedias ([Thorne et al., 2018](#bib.bib52)), scientific abstracts
([Wadden et al., 2020](#bib.bib55)), and tables ([Chen et al.,
2020](#bib.bib8); [Lu et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), and recent work
targets numerical claims specifically ([Venktesh et al.,
2024](#bib.bib54)). These settings, however, assume curated evidence and
model-based judgments of support.

The consistency checks test whether reported numbers are _internally_
coherent; the verification benchmarks test whether claims agree with
_external_ reference text. Neither tests whether a number was produced
by the run that supposedly produced it, because in conventional
publishing the run’s outputs are unavailable. Agent benchmarks remove
this obstacle, and we argue that they also change what verification
should mean: because misattributed numbers exist in the outputs, a check
that confirms a value’s presence passes exactly the errors that matter
most. We therefore design a deterministic, provenance-scoring checker
rather than a learned verifier, which would itself require verification,
and we evaluate it on precision and on the cost of its
non-verifications, not on presence alone.

### 2.5 LLMs as judges of integrity

LLM judges are now standard in evaluation. They agree with human
preferences at levels comparable to inter-human agreement on many tasks
([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib62); [Chiang and Lee, 2023](#bib.bib10);
[Liu et al., 2023b](#bib.bib32)), but they are sensitive to the order in
which candidates are presented ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib57)) and can
recognize and prefer their own generations ([Panickssery et al.,
2024](#bib.bib39)); a recent survey catalogs these and other biases ([Gu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)). Cross-model review can surface errors that a
same-model reviewer misses, though reviewer capability matters as much
as independence ([Song, 2026](#bib.bib49)). Most of this evidence
concerns judgments of quality or preference on short outputs. Integrity
auditing is different in kind: the judge must read a long report,
inspect a large file system, and decide whether specific statements are
supported. Whether such verdicts are stable across repeated runs, and
whether they depend on the judge’s vendor when that vendor also builds
some of the agents under evaluation, has not been tested. We test both,
using the benchmark’s own grading pipeline with a judge from a different
vendor.

### 2.6 Data quality, provenance, and trust in AI-produced analyses

In information systems, data quality is defined by fitness for use:
accuracy is one dimension among several, alongside believability,
traceability, and interpretability ([Wang and Strong, 1996](#bib.bib58);
[Pipino et al., 2002](#bib.bib42)). Managing AI in organizations raises
distinct challenges because AI systems act with increasing autonomy and
their outputs are increasingly inscrutable to the people who rely on
them ([Berente et al., 2021](#bib.bib4)). Reliance on automated outputs
is shaped by trust calibration: users over-trust automation that appears
competent ([Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010](#bib.bib40); [Lee and See,
2004](#bib.bib28)) and abandon algorithms after seeing them err
([Dietvorst et al., 2015](#bib.bib12)). Recent work in this journal
examines the trustworthiness of AI-generated content and the challenges
of human–AI interaction in organizations ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib29);
[Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib64)). A report written by a research agent
is a data product of exactly this kind. Its readers cannot inspect how
each number was derived, and both failure modes of trust apply:
accepting misattributed numbers, or discarding sound work because some
numbers could not be checked. Our results speak to both. Value presence
is a poor proxy for provenance, so naive checks invite over-trust; and
most numbers a conservative checker cannot verify are nonetheless
traceable, so treating non-verification as falsity invites unwarranted
rejection.

## 3 Benchmark and data

### 3.1 The _Into the Unknown_ benchmark

_Into the Unknown_ ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)) asks AI agents to
write a follow-up research paper to each of fifteen recent papers in the
computational and physical sciences. Each task supplies the original
paper, a task specification, and a computing environment; the agent must
formulate a direction, implement and run experiments, and submit a paper
with its full working directory. Nine agents from three vendor groups
each attempted all fifteen tasks, giving 135 submissions
(Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Released artifacts ‣ 3 Benchmark and data ‣ Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents")).

Each submission was graded by an LLM judge (GPT-6-Astra) against a
structured rubric. The rubric is a tree of binary criteria (_nodes_)
organized into three sequential gates: G1, research integrity (for
example, whether reported values trace to recorded outputs and whether
the described method matches the code); G2, completeness of the
deliverables; and G3, whether the work meets the bar a domain scientist
would expect. Each node receives a status of pass, fail, or
undetermined, together with a written justification and pointers to
evidence. The judge evaluated every submission four times, and the
benchmark aggregates the repeats into a consensus verdict. A submission
passes a gate only if it passes all counted nodes in that gate. No
submission passed all three gates; 21 passed G1
(Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Released artifacts ‣ 3 Benchmark and data ‣ Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents")).

### 3.2 Released artifacts

The benchmark release contains, for every submission, (i) the four judge
outputs and the consensus verdict, with node-level statuses and
justifications; (ii) the agent’s working directory as the judge saw it,
including the paper’s LaTEX source and PDF, code, configuration files,
logs, and all result files the agent wrote; and (iii) for some
harnesses, the agent’s full interaction trajectory. We use the release
at a fixed snapshot (repository commit 612cdca). Our analyses rely only
on these public artifacts; we do not re-run any agent.

Three properties of the release make claim-level provenance analysis
possible. The working directories are complete, so every result file the
agent produced is available. The papers are written in LaTEX, so numbers
and their surrounding text can be extracted reliably, including from
tables and from macros that agents use to insert computed values. And
the judge’s node-level justifications are released, so the reasons
behind each integrity failure can be analyzed directly.

### 4.1 Re-analysis of the official judgments

From the 540 released judge outputs we extract every counted G1 node:
2,376 node verdicts at the consensus level, each with a status, a
confidence, a justification, and evidence pointers. Nodes belong to
named rubric families (for example, _code–paper alignment_, _number
traceability_, _test-set integrity_, _no hard-coded values_). We report
failure rates by family, which separates criteria that check whether the
write-up matches the work from criteria that check for overt misconduct.
To assess the judge’s self-consistency, we compute the share of nodes on
which all four repeats agree.

We also test whether integrity outcomes are explained by how long an
agent ran. Long runs plausibly lead to messier records and more
opportunities for misreporting. For submission $`i`$ by agent $`a`$ on
task $`t`$, let $`R_{\text{int},i}`$ be the share of counted G1 nodes
passed and $`h_{i}`$ the run duration in hours. We estimate

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
R_{\text{int},i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\log h_{i}+\alpha_{a(i)}+\gamma_{t(i)}+\varepsilon_{i},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\alpha_{a}`$ and $`\gamma_{t}`$ are agent and task fixed
effects, with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors. A
coefficient $`\beta_{1}`$ that is large without the fixed effects but
vanishes with them indicates that run length proxies for agent identity
rather than causing integrity failures.

### 4.2 Claim-to-evidence checker

The checker takes a submission’s paper and working directory and assigns
each numerical claim one of three verdicts: *certified* (the claim’s
value is found in a result file whose context identifies it, beyond what
chance would produce), *unattributed* (the value is found but no source
is identified), or *untraced* (the value does not occur in any result
file at the reported precision). It is deterministic and uses no learned
model, so its behavior can be audited and reproduced exactly.

#### Design principle

Existing approaches ask different questions of a reported number.
Consistency checks such as statcheck and GRIM ask whether the number is
*possible* given other numbers in the paper. Value matching asks whether
the number *appears* somewhere in the outputs. Claim-verification models
ask whether retrieved text *supports* a statement. Our checker asks a
narrower question that none of these answers: does the number *belong to
the specific result the paper says it describes*? A value can be
possible, present, and supported by some text while still being attached
to the wrong experiment, as the worked example below shows: the value
0.1000 appears 24 times in one agent’s files, but the entry that best
matches it is a configuration setting, not the reported result.
Answering the narrower question requires two ingredients that value
matching lacks: a measure of whether the evidence entry’s context
identifies the claimed quantity, and a per-claim test of whether that
identification beats chance.

#### Claims

The checker reads the paper’s main LaTEX file, expands `\input` and
`\include` commands and numeric macro definitions (agents frequently
insert computed values through macros), and removes the bibliography. It
extracts every number with a decimal point, percent sign, or scientific
exponent; integers are excluded because they are mostly counts, indices,
and years. Each claim records its value, its number of decimal places,
whether it is a percentage, and its number of significant digits. Its
context is the surrounding 160 characters for numbers in prose, and the
row label, column header, and caption for numbers in tables. We evaluate
claims with at least two significant digits, since one-digit values
match too many unrelated numbers to be informative.

#### Evidence

Evidence consists of machine-written result files only: JSON, JSONL,
CSV, TSV, log, output, and plain-text files in the working directory.
Text written for humans is excluded: the paper’s own source and any
rendered copy of it, LaTEX build logs, code, and Markdown notes. A
number reported in the agent’s prose or code is not evidence that an
experiment produced it. Every numeric value in an evidence file becomes
an evidence entry with a context string: for JSON, the path of keys
leading to the value; for CSV, the column header and the row’s leading
fields; for logs, the line itself. Entries from bulk data—JSON arrays of
more than 20 numbers, CSV or JSONL files with more than 200 rows, and
log lines containing more than 10 numbers—are flagged, because a
reported summary statistic rarely comes from a raw data dump.

#### Notation

A claim is a tuple $`c=(v_{c},d_{c},\pi_{c},C_{c})`$: its value, number
of decimal places, a percentage indicator, and the set of content tokens
in its context. An evidence entry is a tuple $`e=(u_{e},E_{e},b_{e})`$:
its value, the set of content tokens in its key path, line, and file
path, and a bulk indicator. Let $`\mathcal{E}`$ be the set of entries
extracted from a submission.

#### Level 1: value match

A claim matches an entry when the entry’s value rounds to the claim at
the claim’s reported precision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M(c,e)=\mathbb{1}\Bigl[\,\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{c}}\bigl|\lambda u_{e}-v_{c}\bigr|\leq\delta_{c}\Bigr],\qquad\delta_{c}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,10^{-d_{c}}\,(1+10^{-9}),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\Lambda_{c}=\{1,100\}`$ if $`\pi_{c}=1`$ (a percentage, so 8.04%
matches 0.0804) and $`\Lambda_{c}=\{1\}`$ otherwise; the factor
$`1+10^{-9}`$ absorbs floating-point error. The matched set is
$`\mathcal{M}(c)=\{e\in\mathcal{E}:M(c,e)=1\}`$, and Level 1 accepts
$`c`$ whenever $`\mathcal{M}(c)\neq\emptyset`$. Level 1 is the obvious
automated test—does the number appear anywhere?—and we report it as a
baseline.

#### Level 2: provenance score

Among non-bulk matches
$`\mathcal{M}^{*}(c)=\{e\in\mathcal{M}(c):b_{e}=0\}`$, the checker
scores how well each entry’s context identifies the claim:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(c,e)=\frac{|C_{c}\cap E_{e}|}{\sqrt{|C_{c}\cup E_{e}|}\,\sqrt{|C_{c}|}},\qquad S(c)=\max_{e\in\mathcal{M}^{*}(c)}s(c,e),
``` |  | (3) |

with $`S(c)=0`$ when $`\mathcal{M}^{*}(c)=\emptyset`$. The score is a
cosine-like overlap that rewards evidence keys sharing words with the
sentence or table cell in which the number appears, and the
normalization by $`|C_{c}\cup E_{e}|`$ penalizes long, generic contexts
that overlap with everything.

#### Per-claim decoy calibration

A high score can arise by chance when a file contains many similar
numbers with similar keys. To guard against this, the checker repeats
the scoring for decoy claims $`c^{(k)}`$ that are identical to $`c`$
except for their value,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{c^{(k)}}=v_{c}+k\,10^{-d_{c}},\qquad k\in K=\{\pm 2,\pm 4,\pm 6,\pm 8,\pm 10\},
``` |  | (4) |

keeping only decoys with the same sign as $`v_{c}`$ (for 0.4583 the
decoys are 0.4573, …, 0.4593). The claim’s calibration $`p`$-value is
the rank of its own score among the decoys’ scores,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(c)=\frac{1+\sum_{k\in K}\mathbb{1}\bigl[S(c^{(k)})\geq S(c)\bigr]}{1+|K|},
``` |  | (5) |

and the verdict is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V(c)=\begin{cases}\text{certified}&\text{if }S(c)\geq\tau\text{ and }p(c)\leq\rho,\\
\text{untraced}&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{*}(c)=\emptyset,\\
\text{unattributed}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
``` |  | (6) |

With ten decoys, $`p(c)\leq 0.1`$ means the true value outscores every
decoy. A further decoy at offset $`k=+7`$, never used in
Eq. ([5](#S4.E5 "In Per-claim decoy calibration ‣ 4.2 Claim-to-evidence checker ‣ 4 Methods ‣ Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents")),
measures how often a value that is certainly wrong would be certified
(the *decoy acceptance rate*).

#### Operating points

The strict operating point ($`\tau=0.12`$, $`\rho=0.1`$) was fixed
before any human labels were collected. One change was made to the
checker afterwards: during development labeling we noticed that the
agent’s own rendered paper text and LaTEX build logs were being indexed
as evidence, and excluded them, as the evidence definition above
requires. This correction left the development test-split results
unchanged and was made before the confirmation sample was drawn. After
the development labels were complete, we examined relaxed variants on
the development tuning split only, and froze two of them—*balanced*
($`\tau=0`$, $`\rho=0.1`$) and *broad* ($`\tau=0`$, $`\rho=0.2`$)—before
the held-out confirmation sample was drawn. Strict is the primary
operating point throughout.

#### Worked example

Two claims from the development sample illustrate the difference between
the levels. In “the high-density half-chain entropy rises … to 0.8749
bits at 4.0”, the value 0.8749 matches two evidence entries; the best is
"half_chain_entropy_bits": 0.87487 in an output file, with score 0.38,
and no decoy scores as high, so the claim is certified. In “the matched
raw-sensor encoder obtains … 0.1000”, the value 0.1000 matches 24
entries, so Level 1 accepts it; but the best-scoring entry is a
configuration setting (train_profiles g3 base) with score 0.11, below
the threshold, and the claim is left unattributed. Human annotators
labeled the first claim traceable and the second wrong-source: the
number is present, but not as the result the sentence describes.

### 4.3 Human labeling

#### Labels

Annotators assign each claim one of four labels. *Traceable*: a result
entry gives this value for the quantity the sentence or table cell
describes (same experiment, metric, and model; rounding allowed). *Wrong
source*: the value occurs only for a different experiment, metric, run,
dataset, or statistic (for example, a standard deviation where a mean is
reported). *Not found*: no entry in the available artifacts supports the
value. *Not a claim*: the number is a setting rather than a result (a
grid value, tolerance, threshold, confidence level, or chosen parameter)
or an extraction error. We use “not found” strictly in the sense of
absence from the available artifacts. It is not a finding of
fabrication, since a value may have been computed and not saved.

#### Presentation

For each claim, annotators see the paper context with the number
highlighted and the three best-matching evidence entries (value, file,
and context), ordered so that non-bulk entries come first; they may also
consult other files. They never see the checker’s verdict, score, or
$`p`$-value. Verdicts were stored in a separate file that was not opened
during labeling.

#### Development sample

We drew ten submissions spanning all three vendor groups (one contained
no extractable claims) and sampled up to 22 claims per submission with
at least two significant digits, stratified so that about 40% came from
claims the strict checker certified (to estimate precision with useful
counts) and the rest from the remainder. Population estimates reweight
each claim by the inverse of its sampling probability within its
submission and stratum. The first ten claims served as annotator
practice and are excluded, leaving 184 claims, which were split
alternately into tuning and test halves. Labeling rules that emerged on
the development sample (for instance, that values computed by hand from
settings count as settings) were fixed before the confirmation sample
was drawn. To measure self-consistency, the first annotator relabeled 20
earlier claims, renamed and shuffled, without access to the original
labels.

#### Confirmation sample

After the operating points were frozen, we drew one previously unused
submission per agent (nine in total) and sampled nine claims from each
with the same stratification. One submission (astra-6) was dropped
before any of its claims were viewed, because the checker had not
finished processing it, leaving 72 claims from eight submissions. The
first annotator labeled all 72. The second annotator, a co-author who
had not taken part in development labeling, labeled a subset of 48 (six
per submission) after reading a written guide with three examples taken
from the development sample, blind to the first annotator’s labels and
to the checker; the first annotator was present during this session but
did not comment on any claim. Disagreements were not adjudicated: we
report results against the first annotator’s labels and, as a
sensitivity analysis, against the claims on which both annotators agree.
During confirmation labeling, four of the first annotator’s labels
conflicted with the written rules (for instance, a grid spacing labeled
traceable); after this was pointed out in a consistency review against
the rules, performed with an AI assistant, the annotator revised them.
We report agreement both before and after these revisions.

defines the quantities we report. We treat *traceable* as the positive

over-represent certified claims, population estimates weight each
labeled claim $`j`$ by the inverse of its sampling probability within
its submission $`m`$ and stratum $`g`$ (certified or not):

|     |                              |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |

       w_{j}=\frac{N_{mg}}{n_{mg}},

|  |  |  |  |

the same paper being correlated; for precision we also report exact

annotators is measured with Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ ([Cohen,

### 4.4 Baselines

We compare the checker with two alternatives that a practitioner might
reach for. The *embedding baseline* keeps the checker’s matching, bulk
filter, and decoy test but replaces the token-overlap score of
Eq. ([3](#S4.E3 "In Level 2: provenance score ‣ 4.2 Claim-to-evidence checker ‣ 4 Methods ‣ Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents"))
with the cosine similarity between sentence embeddings
(all-MiniLM-L6-v2) of the claim context and of each evidence entry’s
context and file path. It is run with and without the decoy test, and
its threshold is tuned on the development tuning split to match the
strict checker’s precision there. The *LLM verifier* gives a large
language model exactly what the human annotators saw—the written
labeling guide, the paper context, and the three best-matching evidence
entries—and asks for one of the four labels, with no access to files,
human labels, or checker verdicts; a *traceable* answer counts as a
certification. We ran it with Claude Opus 5.5 through the vendor’s
command-line interface, one single-turn call per claim in an empty
working directory. All methods are compared on the 129 labeled real
claims that were not used for any tuning: the development test split and
the confirmation sample.

### 4.5 Second judge

To test whether the integrity verdicts depend on the judge, we re-graded
a stratified sample of 30 submissions with a judge from a different
vendor (Claude Opus 5.5). We used the benchmark’s own runner for this
judge, which reuses the official prompt, rubric, evidence protocol, and
grading code, so the judge model is the only difference. The sample
contains three or four submissions per agent, including up to two G1
passers for each agent that had any, and was restricted to submissions
whose archived working directory is at most 5 GB. Because of
computational cost, the first two submissions were graded four times,
the next two twice, and the remaining 26 once.

We compare the two judges node by node. Per-repeat agreement compares
each second-judge repeat with each official repeat on the counted nodes
both graded; gate agreement compares majority verdicts. To test for a
vendor effect, let $`\mathcal{N}_{i}`$ be the counted G1 nodes graded by
both judges for submission $`i`$ and $`f^{J}_{in}=1`$ if judge $`J`$
fails node $`n`$. The leniency difference is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{i}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bigl(f^{\text{second}}_{in}-f^{\text{official}}_{in}\bigr).
``` |  | (10) |

We test whether $`d_{i}`$ is non-zero overall (Wilcoxon signed-rank
test) and whether it differs across vendor groups (Kruskal–Wallis test,
and permutation tests for each group against the rest), using
submissions as the unit of analysis. As a node-level check, we fit

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\operatorname{logit}\Pr\bigl(f^{J}_{in}=1\bigr)={}&\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\,\mathbb{1}[J=\text{second}]+\sum_{v}\beta_{2v}\,\mathbb{1}[g_{i}=v]\\
&+\sum_{v}\beta_{3v}\,\mathbb{1}[J=\text{second}]\,\mathbb{1}[g_{i}=v],\end{split}
``` |  | (11) |

where $`g_{i}`$ is the vendor group of the agent (open-weight as
reference), estimated by generalized estimating equations with an
exchangeable working correlation clustered by submission. A vendor
effect would appear as interaction terms $`\beta_{3v}`$ that differ in
sign or size across groups.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 What the official judge fails (RQ1)

Of the 540 released judgments, 450 ended in an integrity failure, 86 in
an incompleteness verdict, and 4 below the scientist’s bar. Across the
2,376 counted G1 nodes at the consensus level, 469 failed, almost all
(438) with high confidence.

concentrate in criteria that compare the write-up with the work:

was executed (26%). Criteria that target overt misconduct almost never

Replacing the token-overlap score with sentence-embedding similarity
makes the checker worse: tuned to the same precision, the embedding
score certifies 21 claims instead of 57 (recall 0.21 against 0.54), and

evidence the annotators saw, it labels almost every traceable claim

to let the deterministic checker certify what it can, and to use an LLM
to triage the remainder for human review rather than to issue final

deterministic, costs nothing to rerun, and exposes the entry and score
behind every certification. The LLM, by contrast, depends on the
candidate entries that the checker’s retrieval supplies.

#### Non-verification is not evidence of falsehood

The price of this precision is recall: the strict checker certifies
about a third of traceable claims (0.34 held-out, 0.39 development), and
its coverage—the share of all real claims it certifies—is 23%. The
remaining claims cannot be read as failures. Among held-out claims the
checker did not certify, 19 of 31 were labeled traceable (57%
population-weighted); on development the figure is 61 of 95 (72%). A
reader who treated “not certified” as “not supported” would be wrong in
most cases. The relaxed operating points recover more traceable claims
(recall 0.51–0.55 held-out) at a cost of about seven points of precision
and a three- to seven-fold higher decoy acceptance, so we retain strict
as the default and report the others as the available trade-off.

#### Why traceable claims are missed

On the development tuning split, the 37 traceable claims the strict
checker missed fall into three groups. For 13, the correct entry was
found and beat every decoy, but the context score fell just below the
threshold, typically because a sentence reporting several numbers
dilutes the overlap. For 6, the context matched, but neighboring decoy
values matched equally well, as happens in dense grids of similar
results. For the remaining 18, a different entry scored best, most often
a log line or an entry with a generic key. Two alternative scoring
functions—a plain set cosine and the share of evidence-key tokens found
in the claim’s context—did not improve on the original

## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 What the findings mean

The four analyses point in the same direction. The official judge’s
failures concentrate on criteria that compare the write-up with the
work, not on criteria for overt misconduct. When individual numbers are
checked by hand, most unsupported ones exist in the agent’s own files
but belong to a different experiment, metric, or run. A provenance
checker with no access to the judge’s verdicts reproduces the judge’s
ranking of agents. And a judge from a different vendor confirms that
ranking, while disagreeing about how many submissions pass. In this
benchmark, the typical integrity failure of an AI research agent is not
invention but misattribution: the agent did the work, recorded results,
and then reported some of them against the wrong referent.

This distinction matters because the two failure modes call for
different responses. Fabrication is a question of intent and calls for
detection and sanction. Misattribution is a question of record-keeping
and reporting discipline, and calls for tooling: structured result files
with explicit keys, reports generated from those files rather than
retyped, and checks that tie each reported number to the run that
produced it. The behavior we observe resembles the selective and loosely
documented reporting that InnovationEval describes ([Owen,
2026](#bib.bib38)) more than the invented results that headlines about
“fabricating” agents suggest. It also echoes long-standing findings
about human research reporting, where errors in reported statistics are
common and mostly inadvertent ([Bakker and Wicherts, 2011](#bib.bib2);
[Nuijten et al., 2016](#bib.bib37)).

The checker results add a methodological point that holds regardless of
how the fabrication–misattribution split varies across settings. If a
reported number may be a real value attached to the wrong source, then
confirming that the value appears in the outputs cannot establish that
the claim is supported. Value matching accepted 87–95% of unsupported
claims in our samples and 88% of decoy values that were wrong by
construction. Any audit of AI-produced results—whether by a benchmark, a
reviewer, or an organization consuming agent reports—needs to establish
provenance, not presence.

### 6.2 Implications for practice

#### For organizations that use AI-produced analyses

The first implication is to ask which run produced a reported number,
not whether the number can be found. The second is to use automated
verification asymmetrically. A conservative checker can certify a
meaningful share of claims (23% of claims in our held-out sample, at
over 90% precision), and those claims can be relied on to the extent
that the underlying experiment is sound. Claims it cannot certify should
be routed to human review rather than treated as failures, since most of
them turned out to be traceable. This asymmetric use guards against both
failure modes of trust in automation identified in the
information-systems literature: over-reliance on outputs that look
verified ([Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010](#bib.bib40); [Lee and See,
2004](#bib.bib28)), and wholesale rejection of useful work after visible
errors ([Dietvorst et al., 2015](#bib.bib12)). In data-quality terms
([Wang and Strong, 1996](#bib.bib58)), traceability is a dimension of
fitness for use that can be measured claim by claim, and it is distinct
from accuracy.

#### For benchmark designers

Integrity verdicts from a single LLM judge are stable across repeats but
not across judges: the second judge passed twice as many submissions
while preserving the ranking. Benchmarks should therefore report which
judge produced their pass rates, treat absolute pass rates as
judge-dependent, and, where feasible, add a second judge from a
different vendor or a deterministic check. The opus-5.5 case suggests a
further risk. A judge that checks a few headline numbers per paper may
rate highly an agent whose headline numbers are right but whose other
numbers are not traceable. Claim-level checking complements node-level
judging rather than replacing it.

#### For agent developers

Our failure analysis suggests that traceability depends on how results
are recorded: entries with descriptive keys are easy to tie to the
sentences that report them, while values computed only in the prose
cannot be traced at all. Making reports a function of the recorded
results—for example, by inserting values from result files instead of
retyping them—would remove most of the misattribution we observed, and
would make agents’ work far cheaper to verify.

### 6.3 Limitations

#### One benchmark

All findings come from one benchmark, nine agents, and fifteen tasks in
the computational and physical sciences. The share of misattributed
versus absent numbers may differ in other domains, with other agents, or
under other harnesses, and we make no claim that agents in general
misreport more often than they fabricate. The checker relies on
properties of this benchmark—papers written in LaTEX and result files in
machine-readable formats—and would need adaptation for papers in other
formats or results stored in binary files or databases.

#### Labels and annotators

The human labels come from two annotators, both members of the research
team. They worked blind to the checker and to each other’s labels, and
their agreement is high, but they are not an external panel. The
development labeling rules were refined while labeling, before the
confirmation sample was drawn. During confirmation labeling, four labels
were revised after a rule-consistency review, and we report agreement
before and after. “Not found” means not found in the available
artifacts: a value computed and never saved, or quoted from the
literature, is not fabricated. The labeled samples are modest (256
claims, 60 real claims in the held-out set), and several confidence
intervals are correspondingly wide.

#### Checker scope

The checker certifies provenance, not correctness. A certified number is
supported by a recorded result, but the experiment that produced it may
still be flawed. Its recall is about one third, so it settles only part
of a paper automatically. Benchmark-wide measurements cover 99 of the
135 submissions; the excluded submissions are the largest, and if very
large runs differ systematically in how they report results, the
per-agent certified shares could shift.

#### Second judge and harnesses

The second-judge sample has 30 submissions, most graded once, so the
vendor-interaction tests have limited power, and the non-significant
hint of self-preference can be neither confirmed nor ruled out. We also
did not adjudicate the 438 nodes on which the two judges disagree, so we
can say that pass rates depend on the judge but not which judge is
closer to a human expert. Agents were run under different harnesses. For
example, the runner’s completion check failed to execute much more often
for some Claude-based agents than for others, so differences between
agents may partly reflect their harnesses as well as their models.

#### The agents themselves

The agents in the benchmark are recent frontier systems that will be
superseded. We expect the methodological findings—that presence is not
provenance, and that non-verification is not falsehood—to outlast the
specific rates we report.

## 7 Conclusions

AI research agents now produce papers that look finished, and the people
who read them cannot easily see how each reported number was obtained.
We examined the integrity failures of nine such agents on an open
benchmark using the official judgments, claim-level human labels, a
deterministic claim-to-evidence checker, and a second judge. In this
benchmark, unsupported numbers are mostly real values reported against
the wrong experiment, metric, or run, rather than values with no source.
Because such numbers exist in the agents’ own files, checking whether a
value appears accepts almost all of them. A checker that instead
establishes provenance certifies claims with high precision on held-out
data. But most claims it cannot certify are traceable, so failure to
verify must not be read as evidence of falsehood. Applied across the
benchmark, the checker agrees with the official judge about which agents
report traceable results, and a second judge confirms that ranking while
showing that pass rates depend on the judge. The checker’s limited
recall restricts automation to a subset of claims; it does not limit the
value of verifying that subset. Verifying AI-produced research therefore
means tracing each number to the result it claims to describe. The
tools, labels, and data we release make that possible for a substantial
share of claims today.
````
