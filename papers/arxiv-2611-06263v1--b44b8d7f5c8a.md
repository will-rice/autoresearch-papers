###### Abstract.

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly capable of automating
machine learning (ML) research engineering, including code development,
experiment execution, and research reproduction. However, existing
systems typically couple research interpretation directly with code
generation and execution, making them vulnerable to cascading errors and
costly end-to-end repair. We identify a fundamental cause of this
limitation: _research artifacts are not executable specifications_. They
describe models, data, algorithms, and evaluation protocols through
heterogeneous and often implicit representations, leaving critical
implementation decisions to be resolved during generation.

We propose CLEAR, a specification-centered framework for controllable
long-horizon ML research engineering. CLEAR introduces an explicit
intermediate layer that transforms heterogeneous research artifacts into
structured and human verifiable execution specifications before
executable generation. These specifications provide persistent states
for decomposing workflows into localized implementation units, enabling
specification-conditioned generation and repair rather than repeated
end-to-end regeneration. Building on this representation, CLEAR combines
a strong planning model with a dynamically adjusted number of low-cost
coding workers according to task decomposition complexity, and employs a
lifecycle-oriented harness to monitor workflow states, localize
failures, and perform cost-aware intervention. Experiments on PaperBench
and MLE-Bench Lite demonstrate that CLEAR achieves a favorable
performance–efficiency trade-off. The code is available at
[https://anonymous.4open.science/r/CLEAR-VSS](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/CLEAR-VSS).

## 1. Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly being used to
automate machine learning (ML) research workflows, ranging from code
development and experiment execution to research reproduction and
scientific discovery ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4)). Recent systems demonstrate that LLMs can perform
substantial portions of ML research engineering with limited human
intervention ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Mohammadshahi et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). These advances suggest a shift from automating
individual research tasks toward automating long-horizon workflows that
transform research artifacts into executable ML systems.

However, ML research engineering remains substantially more difficult
than isolated code generation. A research artifact typically specifies a
system only implicitly through a mixture of natural-language
descriptions, equations, pseudo-code, implementation details, datasets,
and evaluation protocols. An agent must first infer the intended
computational structure, translate it into executable components,
coordinate their dependencies, and repeatedly diagnose and repair
failures during execution. Consequently, the quality of the final system
depends not only on code generation, but also on whether the agent can
maintain a consistent representation of what should be implemented
throughout this long-horizon process.

The Missing Layer: Structured Execution Specifications. Existing systems
largely operate without such an explicit intermediate representation.
They directly transform heterogeneous research artifacts into plans,
code, and execution traces within a tightly coupled agent loop. This
design creates a fundamental problem: _research artifacts are not
executable specifications_. Their contents often leave implicit
implementation assumptions, while different parts of a paper may specify
model architecture, data processing, training procedures, and evaluation
criteria at different levels of abstraction. When these ambiguities are
resolved implicitly during generation, errors introduced during early
interpretation can become embedded in downstream code and execution
states. Once failures occur, existing systems commonly recover by
regenerating large portions of the workflow, further increasing
computational cost and making error recovery unstable.

We identify three consequences of this missing intermediate
representation. First, _representation ambiguity_ makes it difficult to
distinguish what the research artifact explicitly specifies from what
the agent infers during implementation. Second, _execution coupling_
entangles interpretation, planning, coding, and debugging, allowing
early mistakes to propagate across dependent components. Third, _global
repair_ forces the system to repeatedly regenerate already-correct parts
of the workflow when only a localized component has failed. These issues
reveal a common underlying limitation: existing agents lack a
persistent, inspectable state that separates _what should be
implemented_ from _how it is implemented and repaired_.

CLEAR: Specification-Centered and Controllable Automation. To address
this limitation, we propose CLEAR, a Controllable Long-horizon
Engineering system for Automating ML Research workflows. CLEAR
introduces a structured specification layer between research artifacts
and executable implementations. Instead of using papers and other
artifacts as direct prompts for code generation, CLEAR first converts
them into structured and human verifiable execution specifications that
explicitly describe implementation units, dependencies, interfaces,
execution requirements, and evaluation procedures. These specifications
serve as intermediate states that can be referenced at any time
throughout the entire workflow, allowing downstream generation and
repair to operate on localized, well-defined objectives rather than
repeatedly interpreting the original artifacts.

This intermediate representation also enables two forms of
controllability that are difficult to achieve in end-to-end agent loops.
First, CLEAR performs _specification-conditioned model routing_: large
models are used for global interpretation, planning, and integration,
while smaller models handle specification-bounded code generation and
localized repair. Because each generation task is constrained by an
explicit specification, the number of smaller models used in generation
can be allocated according to the task complexity. Second, CLEAR
introduces a _lifecycle-oriented execution harness_ that treats research
engineering as a sequence of observable workflow states rather than an
opaque generation loop. The harness tracks the specification,
generation, execution, failure, and repair states, enabling targeted
intervention, runtime observability, and cost-aware control.

As illustrated in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering"),
CLEAR therefore follows a specification-centered workflow consisting of
three stages. _(1) Document-to-specification parsing_ converts
heterogeneous research artifacts into structured execution
specifications that expose implementation requirements and dependencies.
_(2) Specification-conditioned generation and repair_ decomposes the
workflow into specification-bounded units and assigns number of smaller
coding models according to the complexity of each unit, enabling
localized generation and recovery. _(3) Lifecycle-oriented
orchestration_ maintains the state of the workflow during execution,
monitors failures and costs, and triggers targeted repair instead of
indiscriminate end-to-end regeneration. Together, these components
transform ML research engineering from a tightly coupled generation loop
into a controllable sequence of specification-grounded state
transitions.

The central design principle of CLEAR is therefore to introduce an
explicit intermediate representation between research understanding and
executable realization. This separation provides a common foundation for
both _reliability_ and _efficiency_: specifications reduce ambiguity and
localize failures, while specification-bounded tasks enable selective
model amount allocation and targeted repair. Rather than increasing
model size to compensate for increasingly complex workflows, CLEAR uses
structured intermediate states to control where model capacity and
computation are needed.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.  (1)
    We identify the lack of an explicit intermediate representation as a
    fundamental bottleneck in long-horizon ML research engineering. We
    formulate structured execution specifications as a persistent state
    that separates research interpretation from executable realization
    and enables localized downstream control.
2.  (2)
    We propose CLEAR, a specification-centered framework that converts
    heterogeneous research artifacts into human verifiable execution
    specifications and uses them to support specification-conditioned
    generation, and specification-conditioned repair.
3.  (3)
    We develop a lifecycle-oriented execution harness that provides
    fine-grained control and observability over specification,
    generation, execution, failure, and repair states, enabling
    cost-aware intervention throughout long-horizon research workflows.
4.  (4)
    Extensive experiments on PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite show that
    CLEAR achieves a favorable performance–efficiency trade-off. On
    PaperBench, CLEAR obtains a score of 29.87 at an average cost of
    \$7.91 per paper, reducing cost by 35.16% relative to _AiScientist_
    while sacrificing only 3.86 points in performance. On MLE-Bench
    Lite, CLEAR achieves 77.27% Any Medal while substantially reducing
    repair iterations on representative tasks.

## 2. Task Formulation

Problem Definition. We formulate ML research engineering as the problem
of transforming heterogeneous research artifacts into executable and
evaluable computational artifacts through a sequence of interpretation,
implementation, and execution decisions. Unlike conventional code
generation, the input to ML research engineering may contain
natural-language descriptions, equations, pseudo-code, implementation
details, datasets, and evaluation protocols, which jointly specify the
intended system at different levels of abstraction.

Formally, given a task type $`T`$, a research artifact $`P`$, optional
task data $`D`$, and user instructions $`S`$, an agent system
$`\mathcal{A}`$ produces an executable artifact $`C`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

             ``` math
             C=\mathcal{A}(T,P,D,S).
             ```                      |     |

The generated artifact $`C`$ may include a runnable code repository,
experiment pipeline, trained model, evaluation results, or valid
competition submission, depending on the task type.

A valid solution must satisfy two requirements: (i) _executability_,
meaning that the artifact can be successfully executed or submitted
under the task environment; and (ii) _task performance_, measured by a
task-specific evaluation function. We therefore formulate ML research
engineering as the following constrained optimization problem:

|     |     |                              |                                                           |     |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| (2) |     | $`\displaystyle C^{\star}`$  | $`\displaystyle=\arg\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{T}}Q_{T}(C),`$ |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\text{s.t.}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Exec}_{T}(C)=1,`$                  |     |

where $`\mathcal{C}_{T}`$ denotes the artifact space for task type
$`T`$, $`Q_{T}(\cdot)`$ is the task-specific evaluation metric, and
$`\mathrm{Exec}_{T}(C)`$ indicates whether $`C`$ can be executed or
submitted successfully.

Task Instantiations. In this work, we consider two representative task
types:

1.  (1)
    _Replicate AI Research (RAR)_, where the input is an AI research
    paper $`P_{r}`$ and the goal is to construct a runnable reproduction
    artifact:

    |     |     |     |     |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | (3) |     |

                 ``` math
                 C_{r}=\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{RAR},P_{r},\varnothing,S_{r}).
                 ```                                                       |     |

2.  (2)
    _Machine Learning Engineering (MLE)_, where the input consists of a
    task description $`P_{m}`$ and task data $`D_{m}`$, and the goal is
    to construct a runnable ML pipeline or a valid competition
    submission:

    |     |     |     |     |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | (4) |     |

                 ``` math
                 C_{m}=\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{MLE},P_{m},D_{m},S_{m}).
                 ```                                                 |     |

Although RAR and MLE differ in their input artifacts and evaluation
protocols, they share the same underlying structure: both require an
agent to interpret heterogeneous task information, translate it into
implementation decisions, and iteratively construct and execute a
multi-step computational workflow. This shared structure makes them
suitable settings for studying long-horizon ML research engineering.

To capture this common structure, we distinguish the _research artifact_
provided as input from the _execution specification_ required for
implementation. A research artifact describes the intended research or
task objectives, often implicitly and across heterogeneous formats. In
contrast, an execution specification provides an explicit representation
of what needs to be implemented and how its components are related. In
CLEAR, this specification consists of two complementary views: a
machine-oriented specification $`P^{\prime}`$ and a human-readable
method summary $`S^{\prime}`$. We therefore formulate the workflow as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (5) |     |

````math
(P,D,S)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{spec}}}(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exec}}}C,
``` |  |

where $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ serves as the intermediate
representation between research understanding and executable
realization.

This formulation separates *what should be implemented*, represented by
$`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$, from *how it is realized*, represented by
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exec}}`$.

## 3. Our Method

Given the formulation above, CLEAR operationalizes
$`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ as an intermediate state throughout
long-horizon ML research engineering. The system follows a
*specification-first* design principle: research artifacts are first
transformed into structured execution specifications, after which
planning, generation, execution, and repair are performed with respect
to these specifications. This decomposition replaces the repeated
interpretation of raw research artifacts with specification-bounded
implementation and repair decisions. As shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering"),
CLEAR consists of three stages:

1.  (1)
    *Document-to-Specification Parsing*, where the system receives the
    task type $`T`$, research artifact $`P`$, optional task data $`D`$,
    and optional user instruction $`S`$, and transforms these
    heterogeneous inputs into the structured execution specification
    $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$. The machine-oriented specification
    $`P^{\prime}`$ captures implementation requirements, component
    dependencies, interfaces, execution constraints, and evaluation
    procedures, while the human-readable summary $`S^{\prime}`$ provides
    a concise abstraction of the intended research method for downstream
    planning and coordination.
2.  (2)
    *Specification-Conditioned Generation*, where a strong planning
    model uses $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ to decompose the workflow
    into localized, specification-bounded subtasks and coordinates
    lower-cost coding models to generate the initial repository
    $`C_{0}`$. The explicit specification allows the number of
    lower-cost coding workers to be adjusted according to task
    complexity and enables independent subtasks to be generated in
    parallel while preserving their specified dependencies and
    interfaces.
3.  (3)
    *Lifecycle-Controlled Execution and Repair*, where the generated
    repository $`C_{0}`$ is executed and evaluated under the task
    environment. When failures occur, CLEAR identifies affected subtasks
    and performs targeted, specification-conditioned iterative repair
    rather than regenerating the entire workflow. The process iterates
    through execution, diagnosis, and repair until a runnable and
    evaluable artifact $`C`$ is obtained.

A *visual lifecycle harness* serves as the control layer across all
three stages. Rather than treating LLM agents as a single black-box
generator, the harness explicitly controls when and how agents parse,
plan, generate, execute, and repair. It exposes intermediate
specifications and execution artifacts for inspection and user
intervention, while tracking runtime, token usage, and estimated cost.
This lifecycle-oriented control makes the workflow observable and
steerable, while the specification-centered decomposition enables CLEAR
to localize failures, allocate the number of lower-cost coding model
selectively, and reduce unnecessary regeneration throughout long-horizon
ML research engineering.

### 3.1. Document-to-Specification Parsing

The first stage of CLEAR constructs the execution specification that
serves as the intermediate state for downstream implementation. Given a
task type $`T`$, research artifact $`P`$, optional task data $`D`$, and
user instruction $`S`$, CLEAR transforms these heterogeneous inputs into
two complementary views: a machine-oriented structured specification
$`P^{\prime}`$ and a human-readable method summary $`S^{\prime}`$.
Rather than providing another free-form description of the input,
$`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ explicitly captures what needs to be
implemented and how the resulting system should be executed and
evaluated.

We formalize the parsing process as

|     |     |                                     |     |
|-----|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|
| (6) |     |
             ``` math
             (P^{\prime},S^{\prime})=g(T,P,D,S),
             ```                                  |     |

where $`g`$ denotes the document-to-specification process. The
structured specification $`P^{\prime}`$ records implementation-relevant
requirements, including algorithms, inputs and outputs, dependencies,
parameters, interfaces, evaluation metrics, and submission constraints.
The summary $`S^{\prime}`$ provides a concise and human-readable view of
the inferred research method and serves as an inspection interface for
users. Both views are derived from the same underlying evidence and
jointly represent the execution specification used throughout the
subsequent workflow.

As illustrated in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3. Our Method ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering"),
CLEAR constructs $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ through three processing
steps, followed by an optional review step:

1.  (1)
    *Visual document parsing*, which extracts text, page structure,
    formulas, tables, figures, captions, and algorithmic descriptions
    from the input research artifact, preserving both semantic content
    and document structure.
2.  (2)
    *Traceable evidence packaging*, which identifies
    implementation-relevant evidence and organizes it into traceable
    evidence units. Each unit retains its source context, allowing
    specification entries to remain grounded in the original research
    artifact rather than being generated as unsupported summaries.
3.  (3)
    *Structured specification construction*, where the planning model
    $`M_{p}`$ synthesizes the evidence units into the structured
    specification $`P^{\prime}`$ and the summary of the corresponding
    method $`S^{\prime}`$. The resulting representation explicitly
    organizes the implementation requirements, component relationships,
    execution constraints, and evaluation procedures.
4.  (4)
    *Specification review*, where users can inspect $`S^{\prime}`$ and,
    when necessary, correct or refine the inferred understanding before
    code generation. This optional intervention provides an explicit
    control point for resolving ambiguous or incorrectly interpreted
    requirements before they propagate to downstream implementation.

The resulting $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ therefore transforms implicit
and heterogeneous research understanding into an explicit, traceable,
and reviewable execution state. This separation allows downstream
planning, generation, and repair to operate on specification-bounded
units rather than repeatedly interpreting the original research
artifacts, reducing ambiguity and limiting the propagation of
early-stage interpretation errors.

### 3.2. Specification-Conditioned Generation

Given the execution specification $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$, CLEAR
generates the initial repository $`C_{0}`$ through specification-bounded
decomposition and heterogeneous model collaboration. The key idea is to
separate *global reasoning* from *localized implementation*: the
stronger planning model $`M_{p}`$ handles algorithm interpretation,
project-level decomposition, and cross-component integration, while
lower-cost coding models $`M_{c}`$ implement individual subtasks whose
requirements and interfaces are explicitly specified by
$`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$.

The generation process consists of three steps:

1.  (1)
    *Specification-guided planning.* The planning model $`M_{p}`$
    jointly reads $`P^{\prime}`$ and $`S^{\prime}`$ and decomposes the
    implementation into $`n`$ specification-bounded subtasks
    $`I_{1:n}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}\}`$. Each subtask specifies its
    implementation requirements, relevant files, dependencies,
    interfaces, and expected outputs, so that localized workers can
    operate without repeatedly interpreting the original research
    artifact.
2.  (2)
    *Parallel local generation.* For each subtask $`I_{j}`$, a
    lower-cost coding worker $`M_{c}`$ generates the required code under
    the specified file paths and interfaces. Independent subtasks can be
    processed in parallel, allowing the number of low-cost coding
    workers to be dynamically allocated for local implementation without
    repeatedly invoking the stronger planning model.
3.  (3)
    *Specification-aware integration.* The planning model $`M_{p}`$
    collects the generated components and verifies their dependencies,
    interfaces, file structure, and cross-component consistency against
    $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$. It resolves conflicts when necessary
    and integrates the validated components into the initial repository
    $`C_{0}`$.

We formalize the generation stage as

|     |     |                                               |     |
|-----|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|
| (7) |     |
             ``` math
             C_{0}=G(P^{\prime},S^{\prime};M_{p},M_{c},n),
             ```                                            |     |

where $`G`$ denotes the specification-conditioned generation process,
$`M_{p}`$ performs global planning and integration, $`M_{c}`$ performs
localized implementation. Here, $`n`$ controls the degree of parallel
decomposition: more complex workflows with more independent
implementation units activate more coding workers, whereas simpler
workflows require fewer workers.

This design turns long-horizon implementation into a collection of
specification-bounded local decisions while preserving global
coordination. The stronger model is therefore reserved for tasks that
require project-level reasoning, whereas lower-cost models handle
routine and parallelizable coding work, improving the performance–cost
trade-off of generation.

### 3.3. Lifecycle-Controlled Execution and Repair

The generated repository $`C_{0}`$ is not assumed to be correct. CLEAR
therefore treats execution and repair as a controlled lifecycle rather
than a single post-generation verification step. The lifecycle harness
repeatedly executes the repository, observes execution feedback,
localizes failures, and invokes targeted repair until a runnable and
evaluable artifact $`C`$ is obtained. Specifically, the process consists
of three steps:

1.  (1)
    *Execution and diagnosis.* CLEAR executes $`C_{0}`$ in the task
    environment and collects runtime output, error messages, test
    results, and task-specific evaluation feedback. The harness uses
    these observations to identify the affected implementation
    components and determine whether further repair is required.
2.  (2)
    *Specification-conditioned repair.* When a failure is detected, the
    coding model $`M_{c}`$ receives the relevant execution feedback
    together with the corresponding specification-bounded subtask and
    modifies only the affected files or components. Unlike full
    repository regeneration, this localized repair preserves already
    validated components and avoids unnecessary model computation.
3.  (3)
    *Task-specific validation.* The repaired repository is re-executed
    and evaluated under the task requirements. For MLE tasks, this
    includes evaluation on the provided task data $`D`$ and
    submission-specific checks, such as competition submission
    validation. The lifecycle continues until the artifact satisfies the
    required execution and evaluation conditions or reaches the
    predefined intervention limit.

We formalize the execution and repair process as

|     |     |                       |     |
|-----|-----|-----------------------|-----|
| (8) |     |
             ``` math
             C=E(T,D,C_{0};M_{c}),
             ```                    |     |

where $`E`$ denotes the lifecycle-controlled execution and repair
process. The resulting artifact $`C`$ is therefore produced through an
iterative loop:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (9) |  |
``` math
C_{0}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{execute}}O_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{diagnose}}I_{j}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{repair}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{execute}}\cdots\xrightarrow{}C,
``` |  |

where $`O_{t}`$ denotes the execution and evaluation feedback at
iteration $`t`$. By localizing repair to specification-bounded
components and using execution feedback to guide subsequent actions,
CLEAR avoids unnecessary full-workflow regeneration and preserves
successful intermediate components. The lifecycle harness further
provides runtime observability and control over the generation,
execution, and repair states, enabling cost-aware intervention
throughout the long-horizon workflow.

### 3.4. Lifecycle-Oriented Orchestration

Long-horizon ML research engineering involves multiple dependent stages,
from document parsing and user review to generation, execution, and
repair. Failures or costly decisions can occur at any stage, making a
monolithic agent difficult to monitor, interrupt, and recover. CLEAR
therefore treats the workflow as a sequence of observable and
controllable lifecycle states, managed by a visual execution harness.

Interruptible and recoverable control. CLEAR maintains task states,
inputs, and intermediate artifacts in a persistent priority queue. This
enables concurrent task scheduling as well as recovery from service
interruptions without restarting completed stages. Users can pause,
stop, or resume tasks through the frontend, with control actions applied
at safe lifecycle boundaries. The harness also records failures,
timeouts, interruptions, and recovery states for subsequent diagnosis.

Human-in-the-loop checkpoints. CLEAR introduces an explicit checkpoint
after document-to-specification parsing, where the generated method
summary $`S^{\prime}`$ is presented to the user before code generation
begins. Users can inspect and revise the parsed understanding,
correcting missing assumptions, parameters, or evaluation requirements
before they propagate to downstream implementation. The workflow resumes
only after the user confirms or updates $`S^{\prime}`$.

Observable execution. CLEAR decomposes the workflow into 15 operational
stages that span parsing, planning, generation, execution, validation,
evaluation, and output archiving (as shown in the document of our
anonymous repository
[https://anonymous.4open.science/r/CLEAR-VSS/CLEAR_Running_Stages.pdf](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/CLEAR-VSS/CLEAR_Running_Stages.pdf)).
The harness reports the current state of the lifecycle and exposes
relevant intermediate artifacts, execution logs, and repair states
through the frontend. In addition, it tracks runtime, token consumption,
and estimated model cost, providing both users and the system with
visibility into execution progress and resource usage.

In general, the lifecycle harness transforms CLEAR from a black-box code
generator into an observable and steerable execution system. The
persistent state enables recovery, checkpoints allow targeted human
intervention, and stage-level observability enables cost-aware
monitoring and diagnosis throughout the long-horizon workflow.

## 4. Experiment Settings

Datasets. We consider two ML research engineering tasks, RAR and MLE
(Section
[2](#S2 "2. Task Formulation ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")),
and evaluate CLEAR using one dataset per task.

- •
  For RAR tasks, PaperBench ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib12))
  evaluates whether agents can understand 20 top-tier ML papers from
  scratch, implement their codebases, and successfully run experiments.
- •
  For MLE tasks, MLE-Bench ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)) evaluates
  agents’ ability to iteratively improve performance across 75 ML
  competitions. We noticed that MLE-Bench Lite is a carefully selected
  subset from MLE-Bench, providing an option that only requires about
  $`\frac{1}{4}`$ of the testing resources for teams with limited
  experimental resources while ensuring task diversity as much as
  possible. Meanwhile, the baseline methods referenced in this article
  were compared on MLE-Bench Lite. To ensure a fair comparison, we
  rigorously tested CLEAR on MLE-Bench Lite under the same conditions as
  the baseline method. Finally, We use MLE-Bench’s Lite subset (22
  tasks) as a lower-cost alternative to full evaluation.

The above two complementary datasets effectively evaluate the efficiency
and cost of agent execution and optimization in ML research engineering
during long-duration complex tasks.

Evaluation metrics. For PaperBench, its task score is automatically
evaluated based on a large model, which is the weighted mean of 8,316
leaf scoring nodes. For MLE-Bench Lite, we evaluated the dataset on 22
tasks, calculating the percentage of tasks that resulted in valid
submissions out of the total number of tasks, the percentage of tasks
that scored above the median, the percentage of tasks achieving bronze,
silver and gold medals, and the percentage of tasks obtaining any medal.

Baselines. We compare CLEAR against the following baselines on each
dataset:

- •
  On PaperBench, we compared our method with three different agent
  methods, namely AiScientist, BasicAgent, and IterAgent, under the same
  evaluation protocol, despite their distinct processes and structures.
- •
  On MLE-Bench Lite, we report comparative results against strong
  autonomous ML engineering systems, including methods such as AIDE
  ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)), ML Master 2.0 ([Zhu et al.,
  2026](#bib.bib10)), and LoongFlow ([Wan et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)).

Implementation details. Following the settings in AiScientist, we use
Gemini-3-Flash and GLM-5 ([Zeng et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)) as the two
backbone LLMs to instantiate the aforementioned baseline methods. For
our own method, CLEAR, to better meet the working needs of our designed
model collaboration, we have chosen Claude-Opus 4.6, which possesses
strong thinking capabilities, as the planning model $`M_{p}`$, while
Claude-Sonnet 4.6, which has similar habits but is more cost-effective,
has been selected as the coding model $`M_{c}`$.

The proposed visual lifecycle harness in the CLEAR features a
human-in-the-loop checkpoint that allows users to inspect and revise
specifications before code generation. We declare that: in the following
experiments, CLEAR did not use manual review of system generated
documents in all tests and did not make any modifications to the system
generated content.

In PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite, each run is allocated an Nvidia A800
80GB GPU and a 24-hour budget for each task. For the comprehensive

## 5. Experimental Results

We evaluate CLEAR from three perspectives:

- •
  RQ1: Effectiveness. Can CLEAR reliably perform long-horizon ML
  research engineering across both RAR and MLE settings?
- •
  RQ2: Efficiency. Does CLEAR achieve a favorable performance–cost
  trade-off compared with existing ML research agents?
- •
  RQ3: Mechanism. How do structured specifications,
  specification-conditioned generation and specification-conditioned
  repair contribute to CLEAR’s effectiveness and efficiency?

To answer the above three questions, we first evaluate overall
effectiveness and efficiency on PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite in
Sections
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1. Main Results on PaperBench ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")
and
[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2. Main Results on MLE-Bench Lite ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering").
We then analyze the contributions of the key design choices in Section
[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3. Mechanism Analysis ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering").

Finally, as a supplement to the above experimental results, a section of
token cost statistics will be shown in Section
[5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4. Token Consumption Analysis ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering").

### 5.1. Main Results on PaperBench

Table
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.4. Lifecycle-Oriented Orchestration ‣ 3. Our Method ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")
summarizes the overall performance on PaperBench. CLEAR achieves an
average score of 29.87 across 20 tasks, ranking second on 10 tasks,
while maintaining an average execution cost of only \$7.91 per task.
These results indicate that CLEAR achieves a competitive
performance–cost trade-off compared with existing agent frameworks. We
further analyze the comparison from three perspectives. (1) *Structured
collaboration improves over lightweight agents.*Compared with the
BasicAgent baseline, CLEAR improves the average score from 22.58 to
29.87 (+32.29%) with only a \$3.01 increase in average cost per task.
This comparison shows that the additional computation introduced by the
CLEAR workflow is accompanied by a substantial performance improvement
while retaining relatively low execution cost. (2) *More iterations do
not necessarily yield better efficiency.*Compared with IterAgent, CLEAR
achieves a higher average score while reducing the average cost by 85.6%
(\$7.91 vs. \$54.90). This comparison suggests that increasing iterative
interaction alone does not necessarily yield a favorable
performance–cost trade-off, whereas CLEAR maintains competitive
performance with substantially lower execution cost. (3) *Adaptive
resource allocation provides a favorable alternative to scaling a single
strong model.*Compared with AiScientist using the same model family,
CLEAR reduces the average cost by 35.2% while retaining 88.6% of the
performance. The result suggests that dynamically allocating a variable
number of coding workers under strong planning guidance is more
cost-effective than uniformly increasing model capacity.

At the task level, CLEAR exhibits substantial variation across different
PaperBench instances. It achieves competitive results on all-in-one,
bridging-data-gaps, mechanistic-understanding, and pinn, where it ranks
among the top-performing configurations. Meanwhile, larger performance
gaps remain on tasks such as adaptive-pruning and robust-clip. These
results indicate that the effectiveness of CLEAR is task-dependent and
leave room for more adaptive task-specific generation and repair
strategies.

### 5.3. Mechanism Analysis

To answer RQ3, we examine the three key design choices of CLEAR:
*Document-to-specification parsing*, *Specification-conditioned
generation* and *Specification-conditioned repair*. Table
[4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.1. Main Results on PaperBench ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")
and Figure
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.3. Mechanism Analysis ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")
report the corresponding results on PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite.

Document-to-specification parsing. We first remove the
document-to-specification parsing skills, denoted as *CLEAR w/o specs*,
and let the planning model $`M_{p}`$ directly interpret the input
documents. Removing structured specifications consistently degrades both
effectiveness and efficiency. On PaperBench, the average score drops by
19.18% while the average cost increases by 27.43%. On MLE-Bench Lite,
performance decreases by 9.09%, 10.53%, and 17.64% on Valid Submission,
Above Median, and Any Medal, respectively. These results support the
role of $`(P^{\prime},S^{\prime})`$ as an explicit intermediate
representation: turning the understanding of the task into more
structured representation reduces ambiguity before implementation and
avoids repeated interpretation during downstream generation and repair.

Specification-conditioned generation. We next examine heterogeneous
model assignment using *CLEAR w/ GLM*, where both the planning model
$`M_{p}`$ and coding model $`M_{c}`$ are replaced by GLM-5 while keeping
the rest of the CLEAR architecture unchanged. This variant removes the
heterogeneous model assignment while retaining the
specification-centered workflow. This homogeneous GLM-5 configuration
retains the specification-centered workflow but differs from the full
CLEAR configuration in both model assignment and backbone. Compared with
full CLEAR, its PaperBench score decreases by 11.59% and its average
cost increases by 51.07%. On MLE-Bench Lite, it also underperforms CLEAR
on Above Median and Any Medal, while achieving the same Valid Submission
rate. These results show that the full Opus–Sonnet configuration
achieves a better performance–cost trade-off than the homogeneous GLM-5
configuration.

Overall, the ablation results support both design principles of CLEAR:
structured specifications improve the reliability and efficiency of task
understanding, while heterogeneous model assignment improves the
performance–cost trade-off by matching model capacity to stage-specific
requirements.

### 5.4. Token Consumption Analysis

Dollar cost provides a direct measure of practical deployment
efficiency, but it depends on the pricing of the underlying models. To
complement dollar cost with a pricing-independent usage indicator, we
additionally report token consumption on the full PaperBench evaluation.

Table
[5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.3. Mechanism Analysis ‣ 5. Experimental Results ‣ CLEAR: Structured Specifications for Controllable Long-Horizon ML Research Engineering")
compares CLEAR with its two variants. CLEAR consumes an average of 564K
tokens per task, compared with 842K for CLEAR w/o specs and 2.99M for
CLEAR w/ GLM-5. CLEAR w/o specs therefore consumes 49.1% more tokens
than the full CLEAR configuration. This observation is consistent with
the hypothesis that maintaining an explicit intermediate specification
reduces repeated interpretation during downstream generation and repair.
The homogeneous GLM-5 configuration consumes more than five times as
many tokens as the full Opus–Sonnet configuration. This result is
consistent with the overall efficiency advantage observed for the full
CLEAR system. However, because the configurations use different model
families and tokenizers, the cross-model token counts should be
interpreted as a system-level usage comparison rather than a
model-independent measure of computational cost.

## 6. Related Work

We review two lines of related work that underpin our study: LLM-based
autonomous scientific research systems and multi-agent cooperation
workflows for complex task execution.

Automated scientific research. Recent work has explored the use of LLM
agents for automating scientific research, including idea generation([Lu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), experiment execution([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)), code development([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Li
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Seo et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), and
scientific writing([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Lu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). These developments position autonomous scientific
research as a realistic goal for general-purpose agent
systems([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7); [Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)).

Among them, automating ML research engineering is particularly
representative and challenging, as it requires transforming
heterogeneous research artifacts into executable systems under limited
cost and runtime constraints ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Zhu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib10); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). Recent systems
such as BasicAgent ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)), Leeroo
([Mohammadshahi et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)), AIDE ([Jiang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)), AIBuildAI ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)), and
AiScientist ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) demonstrate the
feasibility of this paradigm. However, these systems often operate
directly on heterogeneous research artifacts without an explicit
intermediate representation, which can lead to cascading errors and
expensive repair loops.

Multi-agent cooperation workflows. Multi-agent cooperation has become a
common paradigm for improving LLM agents on complex tasks. Early
frameworks explore role-playing agents, dynamic team formation, and
programmable multi-agent conversations ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib18);
[Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib19); [Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib20)). These
works show that task decomposition and agent specialization can improve
problem solving, but coordination is often mediated through flexible
natural-language interaction and relies on human-designed prompts
heavily. In software engineering, multi-agent workflows have been
adapted to code generation, repository editing, and issue resolution.
MetaGPT and ChatDev organize agents through standardized
software-development pipelines ([Hong et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Qian
et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)). SWE-agent emphasizes agent-computer
interfaces for repository-level editing and testing ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib23)), while AutoCodeRover and CodeR further incorporate
code search, program analysis, and task graphs for issue resolution
([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib24); [Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib28)).

Despite recent advances, multi-agent systems still suffer from
coordination failures, inter-agent misalignment, communication overhead,
and weak verification ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib29); [Yan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25)). These issues become more severe in long-horizon ML
research engineering, where task understanding, execution feedback, and
repair decisions must remain consistent across stages. This motivates
introducing a more explicit intermediate representation between research
understanding and executable implementation.

## 7. Conclusion

We presented CLEAR, a specification-centered framework for controllable
long-horizon ML research engineering. CLEAR introduces an intermediate
execution specification between heterogeneous research artifacts and
executable implementations, enabling specification-conditioned planning,
localized code generation, and targeted failure repair. Built upon this
representation, CLEAR further integrates heterogeneous model assignment
with lifecycle-oriented execution control, allowing different models to
be dynamically allocated according to their capabilities and roles.
Experiments on PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite demonstrate that CLEAR
achieves competitive performance while maintaining favorable
performance–cost trade-offs across both research automation and machine
learning engineering scenarios. Ablation studies further validate the
importance of structured specifications and adaptive model
collaboration. Overall, our results suggest that future ML research
engineering agents can benefit from explicitly separating *what should
be implemented* from *how it should be implemented, executed, and
refined*.

Future Work. Several directions remain open. First, the reliability of
execution specifications could be further improved through automatic
consistency checking, cross-modal verification, and execution-based
validation. Second, the number of low-cost coding workers could be
allocated more adaptively based on task difficulty, uncertainty, failure
type, and expected repair value. Finally, the specification layer could
be extended into a persistent research engineering memory that
accumulates reusable implementation patterns, failure modes, and repair
strategies across tasks.

## Ethical Considerations

This article is aimed at the problem that in automatic ML research
engineering, directly operating on heterogeneous research artifacts
without an explicit intermediate representation causes cascading errors
and expensive repair loops. To mitigate this issue, we propose CLEAR, a
controllable system for long-horizon ML research workflow in which a
structured specification layer transforms heterogeneous research
artifacts into human verifiable execution specifications. With respect
to data usage, all experiments are conducted exclusively on publicly
available academic benchmarks, including PaperBench and MLE-Bench Lite.

collection, training, or evaluation. Furthermore, the proposed method
does not introduce or expose any new personal information. As a result,

unauthorized access, and complies with common ethical standards for
academic research in information retrieval and natural language
processing.
