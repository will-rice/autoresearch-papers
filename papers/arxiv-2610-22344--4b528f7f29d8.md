###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are expanding from assisting scientific
research and engineering to autonomously developing and improving AI
systems, advancing AI for AI (AI4AI) as a pathway toward recursive
self-improvement (RSI). Workflow-driven machine learning engineering
(MLE) systems provide a practical path toward this pursuit through
complex orchestration that manages long-horizon experimentation, while
recent MLE systems adopt model-directed interaction, allowing models to
autonomously organize experiments and adapt optimization strategies
based on execution feedback. However, feedback from each experiment
reflects several preceding decisions, and a long MLE run contains many
such costly experiments. Training must therefore select useful
supervision from long interaction trajectories and learn from
intermediate outcomes as they become available. To address these
challenges, we propose a post-training method for autonomous
long-horizon MLE optimization and apply it to train MLExpert, a 35B
Mixture-of-Experts agentic model. We design the Kaggle-MLE Factory to
construct executable tasks and an interaction harness for model-directed
experimentation. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and
coding capabilities to MLE through outcome-guided supervision. Windowed
Interaction RL (WIRL) treats the multi-turn decisions between successive
experiment results as an execution window and assigns it a shared
outcome-based reward. Asynchronous collection and policy correction
support policy updates before the trajectory ends. On MLE-Bench Lite,
MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to
72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger frontier models such
as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. Further experiments show that MLExpert improves
performance on NatureBench Lite, agent scaffold optimization, and
algorithm optimization, suggesting that the learned optimization
capability generalizes to other tasks.

## 1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of reasoning, code generation, and tool
interaction in large language models (LLMs) is enabling artificial
intelligence (AI) to move beyond assisting scientific research and
engineering toward autonomously developing and improving AI systems.
These efforts include model training, optimization of solver programs,
and algorithm design, making AI for AI (AI4AI) a research direction
toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). In this context, machine
learning engineering (MLE) provides a concrete setting for this goal,
requiring long-horizon optimization through repeated execution, feedback
analysis, and revision rather than one-shot generation.

![Refer to caption](2610.22344v1/intro_mle_figure2.png)

Figure 1: Decision making in long-horizon MLE optimization. (a) External
orchestration organizes optimization through predefined workflows and

subsequent actions based on experimental states and execution feedback.

Most existing MLE systems \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2),
[3](#bib.bib3)\] extend code generation into iterative optimization
through search, memory, or multi-agent collaboration. As shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering")(a),
predefined workflows or search rules typically control experiment
sequencing and stage transitions, while LLMs primarily generate or debug
code at designated steps. These systems rely heavily on manually
designed control logic to organize long-horizon experimentation.

Recently, Arbor \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] and Argus \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] maintain
persistent research states and allow models to design, continue, and
revise experiments, placing greater demands on the model’s ability to
plan experiments and sustain optimization over long interaction
horizons. ML-Agent \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] combines multi-turn demonstrations
with single-step RL, while Frontis-MA1 \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] trains program
evolution operators through SFT and RL and integrates them into an
external evolutionary search framework. These approaches primarily train
models for single-step decisions or local operations within externally
organized processes. In contrast, we study how to train models to
organize experiments and improve solutions over sustained interaction,
using experience and execution feedback from real-world MLE tasks.
However, training this long-horizon optimization capability poses three
challenges. 1) Open-ended MLE requires an interaction environment that
preserves experimental states while leaving action selection to the
model. 2) High-quality long-horizon supervision is difficult to obtain
because MLE tasks are heterogeneous and interaction trajectories may mix
useful exploration and failure recovery with degraded decisions. 3) Each
costly experiment returns feedback on several preceding decisions before
the full run ends; online RL must use this intermediate signal while
handling policy mismatch during asynchronous collection.

To address these challenges, we propose a post-training method for
autonomous MLE that combines a model-directed long-horizon interaction
harness, hierarchical agentic SFT, and Windowed Interaction
Reinforcement Learning (WIRL). Based on this, we train MLExpert, a 35B
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) agentic model, to learn long-horizon
optimization strategies. As shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering")(b),
MLExpert uses sustained tool interaction within the interaction harness
to autonomously organize experiments, revise solutions based on
execution feedback, and maintain submission candidates. We build the
Kaggle-MLE Factory to convert historical Kaggle competitions into
executable MLE tasks. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general
tool-use and coding capabilities to MLE optimization, with execution
outcomes guiding the selection of trajectories and segments for
supervision. To use this intermediate feedback, WIRL treats each
completed experiment as the boundary of an execution window containing
decisions since the previous result. It trains those decisions with a
shared outcome-based reward, while asynchronous collection and policy
correction allow updates as trajectories continue. On MLE-Bench Lite,
MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to
72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger models such as Kimi-K3
and GLM-5.3. MLExpert also improves performance on NatureBench Lite and
transfers its optimization capabilities to agent scaffold and algorithm
optimization tasks. Our key contributions are:

- •
  We introduce MLExpert, a 35B MoE model for autonomous MLE, trained
  with hierarchical agentic SFT and WIRL. WIRL links execution feedback
  to multi-turn decisions within execution windows and combines local
  progress rewards with policy correction for asynchronous online
  learning.
- •
  We build the Kaggle-MLE Factory and an Interaction Harness to provide
  executable tasks and model-directed long-horizon interaction for
  training and evaluation.
- •
  Extensive experiments show that MLExpert improves the base model’s
  average medal rate from 37.9% to 72.7% on MLE-Bench Lite, improves
  performance on NatureBench Lite, and generalizes to agent scaffold and
  algorithm optimization tasks.

## 2 Preliminary

We formulate MLE as the iterative optimization of executable ML
solutions within a given time budget $`B_{e}`$. An MLE task is
represented as $`e=(d_{e},\mathcal{D}_{e},m_{e})`$, where $`d_{e}`$ is
the task description, $`\mathcal{D}_{e}`$ is the task data, and
$`m_{e}`$ is the evaluation metric. A candidate solution
$`y\in\mathcal{Y}_{e}`$ represents a complete ML pipeline including data
processing, feature engineering, model training, and prediction, where
$`\mathcal{Y}_{e}`$ denotes the solution space. The objective is to find
a best-performing solution for a given task,
$`y_{e}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{e}}S_{e}(y)`$, where
$`S_{e}(y)`$ denotes the task-specific score under metric $`m_{e}`$,
normalized so that higher values indicate better performance. Following
MLE-bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], the agent has access to the task
description and public data, and improves solutions through code
execution and self-constructed validation. The final submission is
independently evaluated using hidden test labels.

In Agentic MLE, an LLM policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ autonomously organizes
the optimization process. Given a task $`e`$, the model constructs,
executes, and improves candidate solutions through continuous tool
interaction within the time budget $`B_{e}`$. A trajectory of length
$`L`$ is represented as
$`\tau=(o_{0},a_{0},o_{1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{L-1},o_{L})`$, where $`o_{0}`$
is the initial task observation, $`a_{l}`$ is the model action at step
$`l`$, and $`o_{l+1}`$ is the observation returned after executing
$`a_{l}`$. Each action may include model reasoning and one or more tool
calls. At step $`l`$, the model samples
$`a_{l}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{l})`$, where $`c_{l}`$ consists of
the task information and retained interaction history.

Experimental states, including code, execution records, and candidate
solutions, are stored in an external workspace and exposed to the model
through tool observations. Rather than following a predefined workflow,
the model decides when to analyze, revise, execute, or change the
optimization direction based on the current state. The model maintains a
submission candidate throughout the interaction and selects its final
solution $`\hat{y}_{\tau}`$ at the end of trajectory $`\tau`$ based on
the experimental results and analysis available during interaction. The
objective of Agentic MLE is to maximize the expected quality of this
selected solution

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
J(\pi_{\theta};e)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\pi_{\theta}}(\cdot\mid e,B_{e})}\left[S_{e}(\hat{y}_{\tau})\right],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`p_{\pi_{\theta}}`$ denotes the trajectory distribution induced
by the policy and the interaction environment.

### 3.1 Kaggle-MLE Factory: Constructing Executable MLE Tasks

Long-horizon Agentic MLE training requires executable tasks covering
diverse data modalities, objectives, and domains. Historical Kaggle
competitions provide a rich source of real-world MLE problems, but their
heterogeneous data organization, submission formats, and evaluation
protocols make manual task adaptation difficult to scale. Therefore, we
build the Kaggle-MLE Factory, which converts historical competitions
into executable tasks with a public workspace, a hidden test split, and
a unified evaluation interface. The Factory uses a multi-agent pipeline
for task construction, validation, and repair. The discovery agent
filters competitions by status, scale, and submission format. After
competition data, leaderboard information, and official descriptions are
collected, the analyzer agent extracts data structures, fields, and
modality characteristics to form a task specification. The generator
agent then prepares task data, constructs a held-out test split with
hidden labels from the original labeled data, and implements the
task-specific evaluator. The task is executed on real data and
iteratively repaired based on validator checks of data integrity,
submission format, and evaluation behavior to produce valid executable
tasks. Standardized task packages support different MLE agent
frameworks. Final manual review excludes tasks with unresolved label or
scoring issues before trajectory collection. Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Details of the Kaggle-MLE Factory ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering")
details agent responsibilities and implementation.

### 3.2 Interaction Harness for Long-Horizon MLE Optimization

To support model-directed long-horizon MLE optimization, we design an
interaction harness that follows ReAct-style loop \[[9](#bib.bib9)\],
leaving branch selection and optimization-path decisions to the model
rather than an external search policy. The harness stores experimental
states in solution nodes, each containing candidate code, execution
logs, and metadata. Parent-linked revisions form an experiment tree that
the model can revisit and compare to decide whether to continue,
backtrack, or change optimization direction. The harness provides tools
for solution analysis, code revision, experiment execution, history
retrieval, and submission management. The model launches candidate
execution through execute_code and updates its submission candidate
through update_answer. The complete tool list and descriptions are
provided in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Interaction Harness Tools ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

As interaction history approaches the context limit, earlier messages
are summarized while recent interactions are retained. For complex
analysis, analyze delegates task analysis, data exploration, and result
diagnosis to an analysis sub-agent with independent context, returning
only a summary to the main model. Since overfitting or data leakage can
inflate validation scores, the model can call invalidate_node based on
experimental evidence and analysis to exclude unreliable candidates.
With new evidence, the model can update its submission candidate while
exploring alternatives.

## 4 Agentic MLE Post-Training

### 4.1 Hierarchical Agentic SFT

Long-horizon MLE optimization requires general tool-use, coding skills,
and domain-specific decision making. We therefore use two-stage
hierarchical agentic SFT. In the first stage, coding and tool-use
trajectories from diverse agentic tasks are used to learn basic tool-use
and code revision capabilities. The second stage adopts teacher
trajectories from MLE domain and literature-derived scientific tasks to
improve algorithm design, experimental analysis, and iterative
optimization.

Complementary MLE demonstrations. MLE adaptation data cover both task
initialization and sustained optimization. For trajectories whose first
complete solution executes successfully and reaches a quality threshold,
we extract the interaction prefix from task start through the
corresponding execution feedback as an initial-solution trajectory,
typically covering task understanding, algorithm design, and code
generation. We also use long-horizon optimization trajectories from
complete interactions, covering solution construction, feedback
analysis, iterative revision, and final submission selection. In
addition, analysis sub-agent trajectories are adopted to train the model
for independent data analysis and result diagnosis.

Outcome-guided supervision selection. Long-horizon MLE trajectories can
contain effective exploration and failure recovery, but may also include
misguided decisions or continued degradation after reaching a strong
solution. Therefore, we use execution results of submitted solutions to
select high-quality trajectories and the segments used for supervision.
For trajectories that pass a quality threshold, we identify the first
best-performing submission and measure the subsequent performance
degradation. When the post-peak degradation exceeds a threshold, we use
the first best-performing submission as the anchor for retaining a
trajectory prefix; otherwise, we use the last submission. The prefix
ends at a complete tool-interaction boundary near the anchor. This
removes degraded tails while preserving useful exploration and failure
recovery before the peak.

Supervision samples and training objective. To preserve multi-turn
context dependencies, each supervision sample combines existing
interaction context with a continuous sequence of subsequent model
actions and environment feedback from the selected MLE interactions.
When context compaction occurs, the retained history and summary form
the conditioning context for subsequent samples. Training loss is
applied only to newly generated reasoning, text responses, and tool-call
tokens, while tool feedback and inherited interaction history are used
only as conditioning context and masked out from the loss. Details of
outcome-guided filtering, score normalization, and the SFT objective
appear in Appendix
[C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Details of Hierarchical Agentic SFT ‣ Appendix C Agentic MLE Post-Training Details ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

### 4.2 Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning

Building on SFT, we further optimize the long-horizon decision policy
using execution feedback from the model’s own exploration. In MLE,
solution quality is available only after training and evaluation, and
each result depends on multiple rounds of analysis, code revision, and
tool interaction. We introduce Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL), which
groups consecutive model decisions made between successive experiment
results into an execution window $`u_{m}`$. Each window is conditioned
on the accumulated interaction history and associated with the outcome
of the corresponding experiment launched through the execute_code tool
(Sec.
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Interaction Harness for Long-Horizon MLE Optimization ‣ 3 Task Construction and Long-Horizon Interaction ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering")).
The first window starts from the initial task state. This allows policy
updates to use feedback from completed experiments without waiting for
the entire task trajectory to finish.

Window rewards and shared optimization weights. MLE tasks use different
evaluation metrics and score scales, so we use task-specific medal
thresholds as a common reference for solution quality and compare each
valid submission with the most recent valid submission in the trajectory
to capture local optimization progress. The reward of execution window
$`u_{m}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{m}=\begin{cases}-1.0,&\text{execution error caused by model actions},\\
-0.6,&\text{no such error but no scorable submission},\\
-0.4+T_{m},&\text{valid submission below the medal threshold},\\
1.0+T_{m},&\text{bronze or silver medal},\\
2.0+T_{m},&\text{gold medal}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`T_{m}`$ denotes the local performance trend and is set to $`0`$
for the first valid submission. For subsequent valid submissions,
$`T_{m}\in\{-0.2,0,0.2\}`$ according to whether performance decreases,
remains unchanged, or improves relative to the previous valid
submission, accounting for the direction of the task metric. Unlike
standard GRPO \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], WIRL directly uses the verifiable
execution reward as the shared optimization weight
$`\widehat{A}_{m}=R_{m}`$, without group-relative normalization, and
applies it to all main-model output tokens included in training within
window $`u_{m}`$.

Asynchronous collection and policy correction. Since experiment
durations vary, WIRL asynchronously collects windows with trusted
execution feedback to form training batches. A long trajectory may span
multiple policy updates, so its model calls may be generated by rollout
policies that differ from the current training policy. We therefore
apply token-level policy correction using log-probabilities recorded
during collection. For output token $`t`$ from model call $`j`$ in
window $`u_{m}`$, let $`\ell_{\theta,mjt}`$,
$`\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{mjt}`$, and $`\ell^{\mathrm{roll}}_{mjt}`$ denote
its log-probabilities under the current training policy, the policy
fixed before the update, and the rollout policy, respectively:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{mjt}=\exp\!\left(\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{mjt}-\ell^{\mathrm{roll}}_{mjt}\right),\qquad c_{mjt}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(q_{mjt}\mathbf{1}[q_{\min}\leq q_{mjt}\leq q_{\max}]\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient, $`\mathbf{1}[\cdot]`$
is the indicator function, and $`q_{\min}`$ and $`q_{\max}`$ are the
lower and upper correction bounds. Tokens within $`[q_{\min},q_{\max}]`$
are weighted by $`q_{mjt}`$, while those outside the interval are
excluded from the policy-gradient update.

Policy Optimization Objective. Let $`L^{\mathrm{PG}}_{mjt}`$ denote the
PPO-style token-level policy-gradient loss \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] with
shared window weight $`\widehat{A}_{m}=R_{m}`$. Because execution
windows vary in the number of model calls and output tokens, we
normalize the loss by the number of valid output tokens within each
window before averaging across windows. Let $`M_{mjt}`$ denote the
training mask for newly generated main-model output tokens and
$`N_{m}=\sum_{j,t}M_{mjt}`$ the number of valid output tokens in window
$`u_{m}`$. For a batch $`\mathcal{B}`$ containing $`B`$ windows, we
minimize

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{\max(N_{m},1)}\sum_{j,t}M_{mjt}\left[c_{mjt}L^{\mathrm{PG}}_{mjt}+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}K_{mjt}-\lambda_{H}H_{mjt}\right],
``` |  | (4) |

where $`K_{mjt}`$ is the KL regularization term relative to a fixed SFT
reference model, $`H_{mjt}`$ is the entropy of the current policy, and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ and $`\lambda_{H}`$ are their coefficients.
The full policy loss definition and implementation details are provided
in Appendix
[C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Details of Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning ‣ Appendix C Agentic MLE Post-Training Details ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

Benchmarks. We primarily evaluate our trained models on MLE-Bench Lite
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\], a 22-task subset of MLE-Bench consisting of
historical Kaggle competitions for end-to-end machine learning
engineering. We report the percentage of tasks awarded each medal tier
(Gold, Silver, and Bronze), the percentage receiving any medal (Any
Medal), and the percentage exceeding the median human score (Med+), with
Any Medal as the primary metric. All five metrics are computed over the
22 tasks and reported as percentages. We also evaluate on NatureBench
\[[12](#bib.bib12)\], following the ten-task Lite subset used in
Frontis-MA1 \[[7](#bib.bib7)\].

Baselines. We compare MLExpert against two groups of models: (1)
same-scale models, including Qwen3.6-35B-A3B \[[13](#bib.bib13)\],
Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], and Nex-N2.5-mini
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\]; and (2) larger frontier models, including
DeepSeek-V4-Pro Preview \[[16](#bib.bib16)\], MiniMax M3
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\], GLM-5.3 \[[18](#bib.bib18)\], Kimi-K3
\[[19](#bib.bib19)\], GPT-5.5 \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], and GPT-5.6 Sol
\[[21](#bib.bib21)\].

Implementation details. Each MLE task is assigned a maximum of 3,000
interaction steps and a 12-hour runtime, using 20 vCPUs, 220 GB of RAM,
and a single NVIDIA H200 GPU. MLExpert is initialized from
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. General SFT uses coding and tool-use trajectories from
StepFun \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. For MLE SFT, we collect teacher
trajectories from Kaggle-MLE Factory tasks, MLE-Dojo
\[[23](#bib.bib23)\] tasks, and scientific tasks derived from
Nature-family papers. Any training tasks overlapping with MLE-Bench or
NatureBench \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] are excluded. Data statistics, training
hyperparameters, and implementation details are provided in Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Agentic MLE Post-Training Details ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

## 6 Related work

### 6.1 Agents for Automated ML Research

LLM agents increasingly automate data analysis, model development, and
experimental iteration, with recent work extending to autonomous AI
system design and improvement \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. AIDE
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\] and MLE-STAR \[[2](#bib.bib2)\] improve candidate
solutions through iterative search and refinement, while MLEvolve
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\] and MARS \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] use structured tree-
or graph-based search, memory, and planning to organize long-horizon
experimentation. R&D-Agent \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] studies multi-role
collaboration, while InternAgent \[[30](#bib.bib30)\] and FM-Agent
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\] extend autonomous AI research across machine
learning, algorithm optimization, and engineering tasks. Recent systems
including Arbor \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] and Argus \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] maintain
persistent research states and increase model control.

Recent work develops MLE capabilities by training models on interaction
experience. ML-Agent \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] combines multi-turn
demonstrations with single-step RL, while RL for MLE Agents
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\], MLE-RL \[[33](#bib.bib33)\], and AceGRPO
\[[34](#bib.bib34)\] study online MLE training with execution feedback,
asynchronous sampling, or intermediate trajectory states. Learning to
Ideate \[[35](#bib.bib35)\] trains models to generate algorithm
improvement suggestions, whereas Frontis-MA1 \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] applies
SFT and RL to program evolution operators and combines them through an
external evolutionary framework. These approaches primarily train
individual interaction steps or local components of externally organized
optimization workflows. SandMLE \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] applies
trajectory-wise on-policy RL in micro-scale synthetic MLE environments.
We train model-directed long-horizon interaction policies on real-world
MLE tasks, with WIRL using execution windows to learn multi-turn
decisions from delayed execution feedback under asynchronous updates.

### 6.2 Post-Training Long-Horizon Agentic Models

Recent research has increasingly focused on post-training agentic models
for sustained interaction, complex tool use, and long-horizon decision
making. SWE-Gym \[[37](#bib.bib37)\] trains coding agents using
trajectories collected from executable environments, while Search-R1
\[[38](#bib.bib38)\] and WebRL \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] learn multi-turn
tool use from environment feedback and outcome rewards. AgentFlow
\[[40](#bib.bib40)\] directly optimizes planning policies in general
multi-turn agentic interaction and uses trajectory-level outcomes to
train intermediate decisions. Recent work has also explored generalist
agentic models for long-horizon interaction. Agents-A1
\[[41](#bib.bib41)\] uses long-horizon knowledge-action trajectories and
multi-stage training, together with multi-teacher on-policy distillation
(OPD), to combine capabilities from multiple domain teachers into a
unified model. Ornith-1.5 \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] extends self-scaffolding
by jointly optimizing task generation, scaffold construction, and
solution rollouts through RL.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce MLExpert, a 35B MoE agentic model trained to
autonomously conduct and improve machine learning experiments over long
interaction horizons. We build an automated MLE task factory and a
model-directed interaction harness, and train MLExpert with hierarchical
agentic SFT and WIRL to learn from teacher demonstrations and execution
feedback collected from its own exploration. Extensive experiments show
that MLExpert improves over its base model on MLE-Bench Lite and
achieves performance comparable to larger frontier models. Further
improvements on NatureBench Lite and in agent scaffold and algorithm
optimization demonstrate cross-task generalization. These results
support treating long-horizon optimization decision making as a
trainable model capability, providing a practical path toward autonomous
machine learning engineering and scientific experimentation.

## Appendix A Details of the Kaggle-MLE Factory

We begin with a Meta-Kaggle¹¹ 1
[https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/kaggle/meta-kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/kaggle/meta-kaggle)
competition metadata snapshot containing 11,649 entries. Guided by the
task-selection principles of MLE-bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], the discovery
agent considers task relevance, data availability, and the feasibility
of offline evaluation. Automated initial screening uses competition
completion, at least 50 participating teams, and a dataset size limit of
200 GB as default criteria, checks submission-interface compatibility,
and consults an exclusion list of existing tasks. The agent further
selects candidates across domains and data modalities, including vision,
text, audio, tabular data, and time series. Candidates are collected and
processed in multiple batches.

The downloader and scraper retrieve competition data, official
descriptions, and leaderboard information. The analyzer agent extracts
data structures, field information, and modality characteristics to form
a task specification, which the generator agent uses to produce
data-preparation and scoring code. When test labels are unavailable, the
pipeline constructs a held-out split from the original labeled data and
keeps its labels on the evaluation side. The executor runs generated
task packages on real data, while the validator agent checks structural
completeness, submission-format consistency, non-degenerate labels,
train–test identifier overlap, and data availability. The validator also
executes the scoring function to check that it produces a valid score.
Execution and validation errors are returned to the construction agents
for repair. We conduct a final human review of the 160 registered tasks,
excluding six with unresolved label or scoring-implementation issues and
retaining 154 tasks for subsequent trajectory collection.

0,&j^{\star}=K.\end{cases}
``` |  | (5) |

Given a degradation threshold $`\delta\geq 0`$, we use the first
best-performing submission as the truncation anchor when
$`d(\tau)>\delta`$; otherwise, we use the last submission. The retained
prefix includes the anchor’s update_answer call and its tool response,
followed by a randomly selected extension of up to ten complete tool
interactions. The extension stops before the next submission and ends
after a tool response. This filtering removes degraded trajectory tails
according to their overall post-peak behavior.

The default degradation threshold is $`\delta=0.05`$, as reported in
Table
[8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.3 Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix C Agentic MLE Post-Training Details ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

Supervision Samples and Training Objective. Let $`(w_{1},\ldots,w_{S})`$
denote the supervision samples constructed from a trajectory, and let
$`x_{s,t}`$ be the $`t`$-th token in sample $`w_{s}`$. The SFT objective
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}(w_{s})}\log\pi_{\theta}\left(x_{s,t}\mid x_{s,<t}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\mathcal{M}(w_{s})`$ contains only the positions of newly
generated reasoning, text responses, and tool-call tokens in the current
segment. Tool feedback and inherited interaction history are used only
as conditioning context and are masked out from the loss.

### C.2 Details of Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning

Local Optimization Objective. Let $`H_{m}`$ denote the experimental
history at the start of execution window $`u_{m}`$ and $`\zeta_{m}`$ the
closed-loop interaction segment within the window. The conditional
distribution $`P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{m})`$ is induced by the policy
and the interaction environment, and $`R_{m}(\zeta_{m})`$ is the reward
computed from the corresponding execution result. For a fixed
distribution $`D`$ over window-start histories, we define the local
objective as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{\mathrm{win}}(\theta;D)=\mathbb{E}_{H_{m}\sim D}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{m}\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{m})}\left[R_{m}(\zeta_{m})\right].
``` |  | (7) |

Local Performance Trend. For the first valid submission, we set
$`T_{m}=0`$. For subsequent valid submissions, let $`s_{m}`$ and
$`s_{\mathrm{prev}}`$ denote the current score and the most recent valid
score, respectively. We define the score change according to the metric
direction as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{m}=\begin{cases}s_{m}-s_{\mathrm{prev}},&\text{higher is better},\\
s_{\mathrm{prev}}-s_{m},&\text{lower is better},\end{cases}\qquad T_{m}=\begin{cases}0.2,&\Delta_{m}>0,\\
0,&\Delta_{m}=0,\\
-0.2,&\Delta_{m}<0.\end{cases}
``` |  | (8) |

Rollout Log-Probability and Policy Correction Details. For each
generated output token, we record the corresponding rollout
log-probability $`\ell^{\mathrm{roll}}_{mjt}`$ during data collection.
The correction ratio $`q_{mjt}`$ in Eq.
[3](#S4.E3 "In 4.2 Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning ‣ 4 Agentic MLE Post-Training ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering")
compares this recorded value with the pre-update training
log-probability $`\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{mjt}`$. The bounds $`q_{\min}`$
and $`q_{\max}`$ define the range in which token-level policy correction
is applied. Accordingly, the correction is used at the token level
rather than as a trajectory-level importance-sampling ratio.

Token-Level Policy-Gradient Loss. For output token $`t`$ from model call
$`j`$ in window $`u_{m}`$, we define the probability ratio
$`r_{mjt}=\exp(\ell_{\theta,mjt}-\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{mjt})`$ between
the current and pre-update policies. Using the shared window weight
$`\widehat{A}_{m}=R_{m}`$, the PPO-style token-level policy-gradient
loss is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L^{\mathrm{PG}}_{mjt}=\max\!\left(-r_{mjt}\widehat{A}_{m},\;-\operatorname{clip}(r_{mjt},1-\epsilon_{-},1+\epsilon_{+})\widehat{A}_{m}\right),
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\epsilon_{-}`$ and $`\epsilon_{+}`$ control the lower and upper
clipping bounds, respectively. This loss is weighted by $`c_{mjt}`$ in
the overall objective in Eq.
[4](#S4.E4 "In 4.2 Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning ‣ 4 Agentic MLE Post-Training ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

Window Normalization and Masking Details. The correction weight
$`c_{mjt}`$ is applied only to the policy-gradient term and does not
modify the KL regularization, entropy term, or window-normalization
denominator. The training mask $`M_{mjt}`$ selects newly generated
main-model output tokens. For a window with no valid output tokens, the
masked token sum is zero, while $`\max(N_{m},1)`$ prevents division by
zero. Padded window slots therefore contribute zero loss, and the outer
normalization remains over the $`B`$ window slots in the batch.

Execution Feedback Filtering. WIRL updates use only windows with trusted
execution feedback. Failures caused by infrastructure errors or
unavailable scoring signals are excluded from training rather than being
assigned the model-error reward in Eq.
[2](#S4.E2 "In 4.2 Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning ‣ 4 Agentic MLE Post-Training ‣ MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering").

### D.2 NatureBench Lite

We use the ten-task NatureBench Lite subset listed by Frontis-MA1
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\], with the S and M definitions from NatureBench
\[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. We follow the official NatureBench evaluation
protocol, with a four-hour runtime budget per task that excludes time
spent scoring submissions. Following NatureBench, we compute the
normalized relative gap for each evaluation instance by subtracting the
paper-reported SOTA value from the agent’s primary metric value,
adjusting the sign so that improvements are positive, and dividing by
the absolute SOTA value. Instances without a valid submission receive a
gap of $`-1`$. The task-level score $`g`$ is the mean gap across
instances. Match-SOTA and Surpass-SOTA report the percentages of tasks
with $`g\geq 0`$ and $`g>0.1`$, respectively. We report a single
evaluation of MLExpert over the ten tasks, with Surpass-SOTA on five
tasks and Match-SOTA on seven tasks, corresponding to 50.0% and 70.0%,
respectively. DeepSeek-V4-Pro Preview and MiniMax M3
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\] results are taken from Table 2 of Frontis-MA1. Our
NatureBench evaluations use NVIDIA H200 GPUs; external reference results
retain their original evaluation conditions.

Matching and surpassing scientific reference results. Under Claude Code,
MLExpert improves Surpass-SOTA from 0.0% to 50.0% and Match-SOTA from
40.0% to 70.0% relative to the base model. Compared with Nex-N2.5-mini,
MLExpert has a higher Surpass-SOTA rate, 50.0% versus 20.0%, but a lower
Match-SOTA rate, 70.0% versus 80.0%. MLExpert more frequently exceeds
the reference by the Surpass-SOTA margin, while Nex matches the
reference on a larger fraction of evaluations. MLExpert matches the
50.0% Surpass-SOTA rate of GLM-5.3, whose Match-SOTA rate is higher at
90.0%.

### D.3 AIME Agent Scaffold Optimization

The outer model optimizes a Python agent scaffold that calls a fixed
gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14 solver. All optimizers start from the same
single-query scaffold and share the task environment, tools, and
evaluator. Each search has a four-hour time limit and a maximum of 1,000
interaction steps, with a context limit of 200,000 tokens and an output
limit of 65,536 tokens per step. The scaffold can modify prompting,
calling, verification, voting, and answer-extraction procedures, with at
most ten inner-model calls per problem. Initial uses one solver call,
while Majority@5 takes a majority vote over five independently sampled
answers.

Development uses all 30 problems from AIME 2025, with three evaluations
per problem for each candidate scaffold. The outer model receives
aggregate accuracy, call counts, and sanitized error summaries.
Exact-request response caching is used during development to reuse
inner-solver responses to identical requests. After search, the selected
scaffold is frozen and evaluated using fresh solver calls on AIME 2023,
2024, and 2026. Each held-out year contains 30 problems and is evaluated
three times; these years provide no feedback for scaffold development or
selection. Thus, each optimizer contributes one frozen scaffold, with
three evaluation repetitions per year.

Accuracy is the fraction of answers that exactly match the reference
integer answer; missing or incorrectly formatted answers count as
incorrect. Table

reports development accuracy, accuracy for each held-out year, the mean
across the three held-out years, and mean inner-model calls per problem
on the held-out evaluations. Each annual accuracy averages the three
evaluations of that year’s 30 problems.
````
