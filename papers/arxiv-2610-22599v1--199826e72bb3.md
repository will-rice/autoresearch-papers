# Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?

Ayush Bansal Affiliation: Autolab AI    Aldo Pareja Affiliation:
Institute of Foundation Models    Sean Klein Affiliation: Autolab AI   
Serge Didenko Affiliation: Autolab AI    Artem Lukoianov Affiliation:

###### Abstract

Recent advances in AI make Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) increasingly
plausible. Large Language Models (LLMs) can already propose, select, and
run Machine Learning (ML) experiments. However, the space of possible
design choices in ML is vast. An LLM that proposes an experiment needs a
reliable intuition of whether it is worth running given the timing
constraints of long training runs. In this work, we measure such
intuition in current frontier LLMs by evaluating how accurately they
predict the impact of self-generated ML experiment ideas before these
are put into training runs. To this end, we construct a dataset of
$`288`$ LLM-proposed ideas on eight MLE-bench tasks. We then prompt five
frontier LLMs (GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, Kimi K3,
and GLM-5.3) to predict the impact of each idea and compare their
predictions with the actual outcomes after training. We report four main
findings. First, LLM predictions are substantially better than random
chance but far from accurate: even the best model, Claude Opus 5.5,
explains only half of the variance in outcomes ($`R^{2}=0.49`$). Second,
we notice that all tested models systematically underestimate the
magnitude of the changes. Third, prediction quality varies widely across
tasks. Finally, the choice of the coding harness that actually
implements the ideas has little effect on outcomes, and we find no
evidence of self-preference. Higher reasoning settings and additional
context lead to modest gains. Overall, our results show that current
LLMs have useful but limited intuition about which ML ideas will work.
We release our dataset to support further study.

## 1 Introduction

AI agents are increasingly used to run machine-learning experiments end
to end \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3)\]. They are able
to propose an idea, implement it, set up the training runs, evaluate the
results, and propose a further improvement strategy. This loop is often
called autoresearch \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], and is closely related to
LLM-guided evolutionary search over programs \[[5](#bib.bib5),
[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\]. While proposing new ideas is now cheap
and fast, the training process itself is slow, which creates pressure to
select only the most valuable ideas to balance out the high opportunity
costs at training time. In practice, many proposed ideas do not improve
the score, so a large share of time and compute is spent on experiments
that are later discarded. To propose good ideas in the first place, an
LLM needs an intuition of which ideas will work and which will not. Even
when the model is never asked to judge its ideas explicitly, its early
choices shape downstream experiments that it will later run, which
compounds the cost of experimental runs. This raises the question of
whether LLMs can predict the outcome of their ideas before running them.

Prior works have evaluated the choices that an LLM makes when selecting
the better of two ideas or solutions before running them
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\],
and found that LLMs pick the better option more often than chance
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [11](#bib.bib11)\]. Others asked for a predicted
score, but used it only as a training reward \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] or
reported only an aggregate error \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. Benchmarks such
as MLE-bench \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] evaluate how well agents solve ML
tasks, not how well they anticipate the outcome of an idea. A choice
between two ideas only tells us which option a given LLM prefers, but
not the magnitude of this preference. In this work, we measure this
magnitude by prompting a model for a numerical estimate of the score
after the idea is implemented. We build a dataset of $`288`$ ML ideas
with measured outcomes, prompt five frontier LLMs to predict the score
after each idea, and measure how closely the predictions approximate the
actual results after training.

In Section
[2](#S2 "2 Related work ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
we review related work. In Section
[3](#S3 "3 Methodology ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
we describe how we build ML experiments with known outcomes and collect
each model’s prediction. In Section
[4](#S4 "4 Results ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
we compare these predictions to the real results. In Section
[5](#S5 "5 Ablations ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
we test whether a higher reasoning level or more context in the prompt
helps, and then whether the model implementing an idea matters and
whether models have self-preference.

Our contributions are as follows:

1.  1.  We propose a way to measure the ML intuition of LLMs, and release a
        dataset of $`288`$ LLM-proposed ideas on eight MLE-bench tasks
        together with the real change each one produced.¹¹ 1
        [https://github.com/autolab-ai/EV-estimation](https://github.com/autolab-ai/EV-estimation)
2.  2.  We evaluate how well five current frontier LLMs (GPT-6 Astra, Claude
        Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3) estimate the
        effect of ML engineering ideas.
3.  3.  We vary the reasoning level and the context provided to the
        predictor, ablate the implementing model, and test for
        self-preference.

## 2 Related work

#### ML research agents.

Prior work on ML research agents has mostly focused on building better
agents, such as The AI Scientist \[[1](#bib.bib1), [15](#bib.bib15)\],
AIDE \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], AIRA \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], and autoresearch
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. These agents are usually evaluated on benchmarks of
ML tasks, such as MLE-bench \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], RE-Bench
\[[16](#bib.bib16)\], MLAgentBench \[[17](#bib.bib17)\], MLGym
\[[18](#bib.bib18)\], and PaperBench \[[19](#bib.bib19)\], which score
an agent by the results its code achieves. Most agents decide which
ideas to keep by running every candidate. However, some use an LLM’s
judgment to decide which ideas to run in full \[[20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22)\], while others avoid it altogether,
since LLM self-assessments are often unreliable \[[23](#bib.bib23)\].
The quality of this judgment therefore directly affects how efficiently
such agents work.

#### Predicting the outcome of an experiment.

Closest to our work are studies that ask LLMs to pick the better of two
ideas or solutions before running them, either on MLE-bench solutions
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\], on research ideas \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\], or inside agent loops \[[11](#bib.bib11),
[24](#bib.bib24)\]. For ML solutions, LLMs pick the better option more
often than chance, but far from perfectly \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[24](#bib.bib24)\]. For research ideas, the best results come from
models fine-tuned on past outcomes \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\].
Other works ask for a predicted score instead. In particular, DSWorld
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\] and WMRL \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] use predicted scores
in place of real runs, and LLMs have also been used to predict the
performance of hyperparameters, architectures, and GPU kernels
\[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26), [27](#bib.bib27)\]. Concurrent
with our work, Wang et al. \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] ask LLMs to predict the
gain of code and configuration changes in LLM research environments and
show that records of past experiments improve these predictions. We
focus on the intuition a model brings to a new task before it has any
such records. We also measure not only how well the predictions rank the
ideas, but also whether their magnitude and direction match the actual
changes.

#### Forecasting with LLMs.

More broadly, LLMs are increasingly used to forecast real-world events
\[[29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30), [31](#bib.bib31)\] and the results
of experiments in other sciences \[[32](#bib.bib32)\]. These forecasts
often have systematic biases. For example, LLMs hedge their
probabilities toward $`50\%`$ on binary questions \[[31](#bib.bib31)\],
while they overestimate the size of effects in social science
experiments \[[32](#bib.bib32)\]. In this work, we study the same
question for ML experiments.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset

We build a dataset of ML ideas, each paired with the measured change in
score that the implemented idea produced. We start from a baseline
solution and its score, and prompt an LLM to propose an idea intended to
either improve or worsen the score. The same model then implements the
idea and trains the resulting model, which we evaluate to obtain the new
score. For example, one of our baselines uses TF-IDF features with
logistic regression and has a log loss of $`0.432`$. Fable 5.1 proposed
adding a second model and averaging its predictions with those of the
first, which reduced the log loss to $`0.361`$.

Repeating this process from the baseline and from each resulting
solution produces a tree, as shown in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Normalization. ‣ 3.1 Dataset ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?").
Each idea is an edge, and the solution it leads to is a node. The model
proposes four ideas for the baseline, and then two follow-up ideas for
each of the four new solutions, for a total of $`12`$ ideas per tree.

We collect ideas on eight ML tasks from MLE-bench Lite
\[[14](#bib.bib14)\], which cover three kinds of metrics: log loss
(spooky author identification, dog breed identification, and leaf
classification), ROC AUC (tabular playground series May 2022, toxic
comment classification, and random acts of pizza), and RMSE (New York
City taxi fares and denoising dirty documents). All eight are past
Kaggle competitions with public leaderboards. For each, we choose a
baseline that scores near the middle of the leaderboard, which leaves
room for ideas to both improve and worsen the score.

For each task, we repeat this process three times, once each with GPT-6
Astra, Fable 5.1, and Kimi K3 proposing and implementing the ideas. This
yields $`8`$ tasks $`\times`$ $`3`$ models $`\times`$ $`12`$ ideas
$`=288`$ ideas. We exclude $`19`$ ideas whose runs failed or for which a
model returned no usable prediction, leaving $`269`$ ideas for analysis.

#### Uniform sampling.

Our goal is to measure how well LLMs estimate the outcome of an idea,
whether that outcome is an improvement or a degradation of the score. To
sample outcomes more evenly in both directions, we prompt the model to
improve the score for half of the ideas in each tree and to worsen it
for the other half. These are plausible, good-faith proposals rather
than deliberately broken changes (Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Prompts ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
In practice, many ideas still move the score in the unintended
direction. Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Direction of the proposed ideas ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")
shows how often each model’s ideas moved the score in the prompted
direction, and Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Example ideas ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")
shows example ideas.

#### Normalization.

The eight tasks use different metrics on very different scales. For
example, a change of $`0.01`$ means something very different for ROC
AUC, which lies between $`0`$ and $`1`$, than for the RMSE of taxi
fares, which is measured in dollars. To compare and pool results across
tasks, we therefore measure every outcome in standard deviations of the
outcomes on the same task. For each idea, we take the difference between
the new score and the score of the solution the idea was applied to, and
flip its sign for metrics where lower is better, so that a positive
change always means an improvement. We then divide this change by
$`\sigma`$, the standard deviation of the actual changes across all
ideas that the three models ran on that task. We normalize each
predicted change in the same way, using the predicted score and the same
$`\sigma`$, so that predictions and outcomes are on one scale. Thus, a
change of $`+1\sigma`$ is an improvement as large as the typical spread
of outcomes on that task.

and then two follow-up ideas for each. Each node is a working solution
and each edge is an idea. Right: a real example from our dataset,
showing the idea, its code diff, and the predicted versus actual score
after training. Here, Opus 5.5 predicts the right direction but
underestimates the size of the improvement.

### 3.2 Measuring ML intuition

We define a model’s intuition as how closely its predicted changes in
score follow the measured outcomes. To measure this, we prompt five LLMs
to predict the result of every change. We use Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1, GPT-6
Astra, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3, all at their maximum reasoning level. For
each change, the model is given the task, the current code with its
score, the idea, and the code diff, and then predicts the new score
(Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Prompts ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
For each model, we then fit a least-squares line to its predicted
changes as a function of the actual changes (Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
We summarize the model by two properties of this line: its $`R^{2}`$,
the share of the variation in the predictions that the actual outcomes
explain, which tells us how well the model separates better ideas from
worse ones (our primary measure); and its slope, which tells us whether
the predicted changes have the right size (secondary measure).

We do not expect LLMs to be calibrated to output the exact score change
for every metric of every ML task. Moreover, in autoresearch the exact
value matters less than how the ideas compare to each other, since we
want to pick the best one out of many candidates. We therefore focus on
how closely the points follow the fitted line, which we measure with
$`R^{2}`$. The slope is a matter of calibration, and the intercept is
close to zero for every model. Rank correlation measures, such as
Spearman’s $`\rho`$, provide a reasonable alternative. However, a rank
measure only uses the order of the ideas, like a choice between two
ideas. We focus on $`R^{2}`$, since it also uses how much better one
idea is expected to be than another, and in the regime of many candidate
ideas it directly determines the expected quality of the selected idea.
Under a simple Gaussian model, the idea a model ranks highest gives on
average $`\sqrt{R^{2}}`$ of the gain of the best idea, both measured
from the average idea. Thus, a model with $`R^{2}=1`$ always finds the
best idea, while a model with $`R^{2}=0`$ does no better than picking at
random.

The slope remains informative, as it indicates whether a model
anticipates the magnitude of a change. A slope of $`1`$ means the
predictions match the actual size of each change, whereas a slope below
$`1`$ means the model underestimates the largest changes. Magnitude
matters for separating ideas that yield larger improvements, such as
changes to the model architecture, from ideas that likely yield only
small gains.

## 4 Results

Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")
shows each model’s predicted changes against the actual ones, with the
fitted lines. Opus 5.5 performs best ($`R^{2}=0.49`$), followed by Fable
5.1 and GPT-6 Astra at $`R^{2}=0.41`$ and $`R^{2}=0.38`$, respectively.
These three models substantially outperform the open-weight models
GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3, which reach only $`R^{2}=0.18`$ and $`R^{2}=0.13`$,
respectively. Under the Gaussian model from Section
[3](#S3 "3 Methodology ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
the idea ranked highest by Opus 5.5 achieves on average about $`70\%`$
of the gain of the best idea, compared with $`64\%`$ for Fable 5.1,
$`62\%`$ for GPT-6 Astra, $`42\%`$ for GLM-5.3, and $`36\%`$ for Kimi
K3.

However, every model has a slope below $`1`$, that is, every model
underestimates how much an idea will change the score. Opus 5.5 has the
highest slope at $`0.61`$, followed closely by Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra
at $`0.57`$ and $`0.56`$, respectively, while GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3 have
much lower slopes at $`0.25`$ and $`0.21`$, respectively. For instance,
an actual change of $`+2\sigma`$ corresponds to a predicted change of
about $`+1.2\sigma`$ for the best model and $`+0.5\sigma`$ for the worst
(Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).

marker one model. The same model can be highly accurate on one task and
poor on another, and Opus 5.5 beats Kimi K3 on every task. The two image
tasks, dog breeds and document denoising, are the hardest for the best

pools all eight tasks. Computing $`R^{2}`$ separately for each task

reveals large differences: the same model can be highly accurate on one
task and poor on another. For example, Opus 5.5 reaches $`R^{2}=0.94`$
on toxic comment classification but only $`0.39`$ on dog breed

tabular playground task to $`0.02`$ on leaf classification. The two
image tasks, dog breed identification and denoising dirty documents, are
the hardest to predict: even the best model reaches only $`R^{2}=0.39`$
and $`0.43`$.

The models are considerably better at predicting the direction of a
change than its magnitude (Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
Opus 5.5 predicts the correct direction for $`82\%`$ of the ideas, Fable
5.1 $`78\%`$, GPT-6 Astra $`76\%`$, GLM-5.3 $`72\%`$, and Kimi K3
$`69\%`$. However, many ideas lie between $`-0.25\sigma`$ and
$`+0.25\sigma`$, where the sign of the change may depend on the
particular training and test split rather than on the idea itself.
Excluding these ideas increases every model’s accuracy (table in Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).

## 5 Ablations

We now ablate the main components of our system. First, we change what
the predictor gets, namely its reasoning level and the context in its
prompt. Then, we check two possible confounds in how the dataset was
built: the model that implements each idea, and whether models favor
their own ideas.

### 5.1 Reasoning level

Figure 5: $`R^{2}`$ of each model as a function of reasoning level, from
low to max. GLM-5.3 only has three reasoning levels, while all others
have five. Error bars are $`95\%`$ intervals from bootstrapping over the
ideas. $`R^{2}`$ rises with reasoning level for the three strongest
models, but not for Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3.

Figure 6: From left to right, the model sees only the idea, the idea and
its code diff, or the idea, its code diff, a data card describing the
data, and the first $`20`$ rows of the data. Error bars are $`95\%`$
intervals from bootstrapping over the ideas. Removing the code diff
lowers $`R^{2}`$ for the three strongest models, while the data card
helps only Opus 5.5.

All main results use each model at its maximum reasoning level. Here, we
test whether additional reasoning improves the predictions. Prior work
finds that longer reasoning helps primarily on math and symbolic
problems \[[33](#bib.bib33)\], and demonstrates no consistent gain on
numeric estimates \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. We find that $`R^{2}`$ increases
with the reasoning level for the three strongest models, with Opus 5.5
going from $`0.35`$ to $`0.49`$, Fable 5.1 from $`0.31`$ to $`0.41`$,
and GPT-6 Astra from $`0.28`$ to $`0.38`$ (Figure
[5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.1 Reasoning level ‣ 5 Ablations ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
For Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3, $`R^{2}`$ varies little across reasoning
levels. Although the bootstrapped error bars overlap, the gain from the
lowest to the highest reasoning level is clearly above zero for GPT-6
Astra and nearly so for Opus 5.5. Additional reasoning therefore helps
only the strongest models.

### 5.2 Context provided

Next, we test whether a model predicts better when it has more
information. In Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?"),
many ideas cluster between $`-0.25\sigma`$ and $`+0.25\sigma`$, and for
these, whether an idea helps can depend on the training data itself. To
test this, we add to the prompt a “data card” that describes the data,
together with its first $`20`$ rows, since the full dataset does not fit
in the context window. In a second test, we provide only the idea,
without the code diff. Removing the code diff degrades the three
strongest models, whereas adding the data card helps only Opus 5.5
(Figure
[6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.1 Reasoning level ‣ 5 Ablations ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3 are largely unaffected. However, most changes are
within the error bars, and only Opus 5.5’s overall gain from the idea
alone to the full prompt is clearly larger than the noise. Overall,
additional context benefits the strongest model but adds little for the
others.

### 5.3 Implementer of the ideas

Figure 7: Each point is one idea, plotted at its change in score when
implemented by Fable 5.1 (x-axis) and by Kimi K3 (y-axis). Points on the
dashed line scored identically under both implementations. The four
circled ideas are examples where the implementer does and does not
matter. Most ideas score nearly the same regardless of the implementer,
with $`58`$ of $`66`$ within $`0.25\sigma`$.

In our main dataset, each idea is implemented by the same model that
proposed it. Since an outcome depends on both the text description of
the idea and its implementation, i.e. the exact code diff, predicting it
is only well defined if different implementations of the same idea give
similar results. To test this, we collect a separate set of ideas on
three tasks, in which Kimi K3 and Fable 5.1 propose ideas and each model
implements both its own ideas and those of the other. Most ideas yield
nearly the same result under either implementation (Figure
[7](#S5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 5.3 Implementer of the ideas ‣ 5 Ablations ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")),
with $`58`$ of $`66`$ within $`0.25\sigma`$ of each other and $`25`$
giving the exact same score. Precise ideas that specify the exact
settings produce the same result regardless of the implementer. The
implementer changes the result substantially in only a few cases. Some
ideas are open-ended and leave choices to the implementer, such as which
additional data to use, so the two models effectively run different
experiments. Others fail in one implementation, for example when a model
imposes its own time limit, stops before training finishes, and falls
back to a trivial prediction. Overall, the implementer has little
effect.

### 5.4 Self-preference

Since the same models both write ideas and predict them, a model could
be more optimistic about its own ideas, a phenomenon known as
self-preference. LLM judges have been found to rate their own outputs
higher \[[35](#bib.bib35), [36](#bib.bib36)\], although part of this
goes away once the quality of the answers and of the judge is controlled
for \[[37](#bib.bib37), [38](#bib.bib38)\]. To test this, we compute,
for each idea, how far a model’s prediction lies above that model’s line
of best fit (Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")).
We then compare this residual with those of the other four models on the
same idea, which controls for idea quality and each model’s overall
optimism. All three diagonal cells in Figure
[8](#S5.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 5.4 Self-preference ‣ 5 Ablations ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")
are close to zero (at most $`+0.03\sigma`$). On average, a model’s
residual on its own ideas exceeds that of the other models by only
$`0.01\sigma`$ ($`95\%`$ interval $`-0.06\sigma`$ to $`+0.08\sigma`$).
We therefore find no evidence of self-preference.

Figure 8: Each cell shows how far one model’s predictions (rows) lie
above its own line of best fit, relative to the other four models on the
same ideas, averaged over the ideas written by each model (columns). The

close to zero, so we find no self-preference.

## 6 Conclusions

In this work, we asked whether LLMs can predict the outcome of an ML
idea before running it. To this end, we built a dataset of ML
experiments with known outcomes and prompted five frontier models to
predict how each idea would change the score. Even the best model, Opus
5.5, explains only about half of the variance in outcomes
($`R^{2}=0.49`$), while GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3 explain very little. All
tested models underestimate the magnitude of large changes, and
prediction quality depends strongly on the task. The models are more
reliable at predicting the direction of a change, particularly for
larger changes, than at identifying which ideas will have a large
effect. Our ablations show that additional reasoning helps only the
strongest models, that additional context helps only Opus 5.5, that the
implementing model has little effect, and that there is no evidence of
self-preference.

For autoresearch, these results suggest that model predictions can
already help prioritize which ideas to run, but cannot yet replace
running the experiments. This matters most for recursive
self-improvement (RSI), where one experiment may mean training a new
model and choosing the wrong ideas is costly.

Our study has several limitations. Each idea was run only once, so part
of each measured change may reflect training noise, and we do not know
the ceiling that a perfect predictor could reach. Moreover, we evaluate
only eight tasks, all of them small ML problems rather than LLM training
runs. Future work could rerun ideas with multiple seeds to quantify this
noise, extend the evaluation to larger tasks and codebases, and test
whether models can predict the outcome of a sequence of ideas rather
than a single one.

black line marks the median, and the numbers on the right show how often
the score went up or down. Points beyond $`\pm 3\sigma`$ and failed runs

Figure
[9](#A1.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix A Direction of the proposed ideas ‣ Can LLMs predict the outcomes of machine learning experiments?")
shows that ideas proposed to improve the score sometimes worsen it.
Fable 5.1’s ideas improved the score $`88\%`$ of the time, GPT-6 Astra’s
$`62\%`$, and Kimi K3’s only $`52\%`$. Similarly, when prompted to
worsen the score, $`15\%`$ of the ideas from Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra
and $`27\%`$ from Kimi K3 actually improved it. Models therefore cannot
reliably anticipate the direction of their own proposals.
