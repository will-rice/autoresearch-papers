###### Abstract

Large language models have significantly lowered the barrier to writing
code. Naturally, this has also led to the development of data science
agents that promise automating the design and execution of data science
from task formulation to model development. Existing evaluations of
agentic data science (agentic DS) assess downstream predictive
performance and code correctness, providing preliminary insight in
agentic DS capabilities. We argue that these evaluations overlook the
fundamental principles of data science: methodological data science- and
domain knowledge. As such, only assessing downstream performance masks
methodological failures such as data leakage and inappropriate
validation, limiting the development of robust agentic DS systems. In
this paper, we evaluate agentic DS beyond accuracy from three different
angles: 1) a large-scale analysis of failure modes in common agentic DS
frameworks and models across 220 data science tasks, 2) a reusable and
extensible test suite with curated data science tasks that test for six
specific failures in a controlled manner, and 3) a qualitative
assessment of agentic DS pipelines against expert solutions on Kaggle
competitions. We find that the majority of agentic solutions contain at
least one failure mode despite the complexity of agentic harness or
model capacity. Moreover, we identify a positive correlation between the
occurrence of data leakage failures and predictive performance,
confirming the necessity to evaluate agents beyond accuracy. Our
qualitative analysis shows that DS agents generally lack problem solving
creativity and domain knowledge, compared to competition-winning data
scientists. We believe that principled failure mode analysis, as
presented here, should be integral part of agentic DS evaluations, and
that the insights we surface will be key in developing trustworthy
agentic DS pipelines. Project resources are available at
[https://trl-lab.github.io/ds-agent-failures/](https://trl-lab.github.io/ds-agent-failures/).

## 1 Introduction

The strong code generation capabilities in large language models (LLMs)
have led to their wide adoption in software engineering ([Jimenez et
al., 2024](#bib.bib10)). When connected to execution environments, LLMs
can be deployed as agents for the autonomous completion of tasks, which
has become an emerging practice across applications and industries ([Pan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). While coding agents generally improve with
more capable backbone LLMs ([Jimenez et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)),
understanding performance beyond end-to-end capabilities is key ([Gu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). Prior analyses of agentic code-based systems
have surfaced common failure modes beyond code correctness, such as
disobeying task specifications, misaligned agent interactions, and
incorrect task verification ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)).

In parallel with coding agents, we observe an influx in the automation
of data science pipelines through specialized data science agents as
well ([Guo et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Grosnit et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22); [Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). Data science use
cases, particularly of a predictive nature, are traditionally
characterized as multi-disciplinary processes that involve long-trained
data scientists, domain experts, software and data engineers ([De Bie et
al., 2022](#bib.bib8)). What constitutes a “good” data science pipeline
is also inherently task- and data-dependent, and cannot be coupled with
a hard verifiable signal. For example, model training and evaluation may
require a temporal splitting procedure if the use-case demands temporal
anticipation, while predictive performance metrics need to suit the
target data distribution. These characteristics also translate into
different evaluation protocols: whereas software is tested in an
integrated verifiable manner, the assessment of (statistical) validity
and utility in data science is typically spread over the entire process
and requires input from domain experts, engineers, and data
scientists ([De Bie et al., 2022](#bib.bib8); [Cong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)). These characteristics, therefore, necessitate
different evaluations beyond code generation capabilities.

An early large-scale evaluation of generic agents across diverse tasks
common in industry, among which data science tasks, highlighted a
significant difference where most LLMs had a success rate of zero on
data science tasks compared to approximately 15% higher success rates on
other tasks ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)). Specialized DS agent
benchmarks provide a more holistic analysis of metrics such as cost,
efficiency, and success rates, surfacing the differences across models
and DS agent frameworks ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Jing et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2); [Nayak et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). However, we argue
that the potential presence of silent failures can give a distorted view
of generalizability, hence making it risky to assess the actual
capability of DS agents with downstream metrics only. The development of
reliable DS agents, therefore, demands more fine-grained evaluations
beyond downstream performance metrics to understand their true failure
modes and capabilities.

In this paper, we present ① the first large-scale analysis of common
failure modes of various DS agents and models on 220 tasks, ② insights
from a reusable controlled test suite with curated tasks, and ③ a
qualitative analysis of capabilities in comparison with human expert
solutions, all grounded in data science best practices ([Lones,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)). Across analyses, we
find that the majority of agent-generated pipelines contain at least 1
data science failure mode regardless of the complexity data science
agent framework
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")).
We also find that data leakage failure rates correlate with inflated
predictive performance, underlining the necessity to evaluate agents
beyond accuracy. Finally, our qualitative analysis shows that agents’
solutions are weaker on task formulation and feature engineering
compared with human expert solutions, illustrating a lack of
problem-solving creativity and domain knowledge. We believe that
principled failure mode analysis, as presented in this paper, should be
an integral part of agentic DS benchmarks and evaluations. We also
envision that the DS practices framework and surfaced insights are key
for developing robust data science agents.

## 2 Related Work

Automated data science. Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) systems
address the need for automating the selection of model architectures and
optimization of hyperparameters ([Hutter et al., 2019](#bib.bib16)),
through frameworks like TPOT ([Olson et al., 2016](#bib.bib21)),
AutoGluon ([Erickson et al., 2020](#bib.bib19)), and
auto-sklearn ([Feurer et al., 2015](#bib.bib20)). By optimizing over a
large search space, AutoML frameworks often match the performance of
models engineered by machine learning experts ([Hutter et al.,
2019](#bib.bib16); [Erickson et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)), yet remain
constrained to a predefined search space and narrow scope of the
end-to-end data science workflow. The recent line of work on tabular
foundation models (TFMs) intends to remedy the hyperparameter search and
instead train and predict in one forward pass using a pre-trained
in-context learning model ([Hollmann et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Qu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). Given the time-consuming nature of data science
and the limited human capacity, it has naturally become a new objective
to automate many steps in the end-to-end data science workflow ([De Bie
et al., 2022](#bib.bib8)). The increasing code generation capacity of
LLMs has led to their deployment in autonomous data science agents,
performing tasks across the entire workflow. Approaches for building and
refining machine learning pipelines vary significantly. Some agents
construct them in a single loop ([Grosnit et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)),
while others iteratively refine them ([Guo et al., 2024](#bib.bib23);
[Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)) or
systematically explore the design space ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)).
Our analysis includes DS-tailored agents, simpler ReAct agents, and
AutoGluon as a reference AutoML implementation.

Agentic data science benchmarks and evaluations. Benchmarks have been
proposed alongside the proliferation of research into data science
agents. Their aim has mostly been to assess the predictive performance
of the models these agents produce. These benchmarks predominantly build
on machine learning competitions sourced from Kaggle ([Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Jing et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Shu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Nie et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Sun et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Nayak et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)), which provide reference solutions that can be used
to define relative performance metrics. They focus on predictive
performance and mostly do not analyze failures. Those that do report
only high-level failure categories, mostly focused on execution errors
such as incorrect tool use and coding mistakes ([Nie et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25); [Shu et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)). We instead analyze
how data science agents implement predictive models by characterizing
which practices they follow and where they fail.

Failure mode analysis in LLM agents. Analyzing code and execution traces
of agentic systems has become essential for understanding these systems.
Such analyses have yielded taxonomies of failure modes specific to
multi-agent systems ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)), safety
aspects ([Bryan et al.,](#bib.bib28) ), and alignment under adverse
incentives ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). A consistent finding across
analyses is that the ability to write working code and correctly use
tools is getting less relevant to the performance of agentic systems,
whereas errors concentrate in planning and reasoning ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)) and understanding data ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)) and the broader context ([Patel et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31)). Existing taxonomies and analyses are mostly proposed
for code generation and thus focus on failures in agent coordination,
grounding, and execution, providing an incomplete view of violations in
data science methodology.

## 3 Evaluation Methodology

In this section, we introduce the methodology used to understand why
data science agents fail. We first introduce the approach used to carry
out large-scale analysis of data science failures using LLM-as-judge
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Large-scale Failure Mode Analysis ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")),
then we present the systematic test suites with curated tests
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Curated Test Suite with Data Science Fallacies ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")),
and finally we outline the qualitative comparisons with high-performing
solutions
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Skill Gap Analysis with Expert Data Science Solutions ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")).

### 3.1 Large-scale Failure Mode Analysis ![[Uncaptioned image]]()

Evaluating agentic data science across a large number of tasks through
manual inspection is costly. Following previous evaluations of agentic
systems ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)), we devise an LLM-as-Judge
evaluation ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)) to assess whether
generated pipelines exhibit specific failure modes. However, not all
failure modes are applicable in every data science use case, and often
depend on properties of the task, data, or pipeline itself. For example,
ignoring temporal order when splitting the data constitutes a failure
only when the data involves a temporal structure for which chronological
separation is required, and is irrelevant in other cases.

To account for the task-conditional relevance of failure modes, we apply
a two-stage LLM-based evaluation. In the first stage, the judge analyzes
each generated solution to identify its pipeline structure and determine
which failure modes are applicable. Specifically, it extracts whether
the solution contains key pipeline components, including data cleaning,
feature engineering, validation, hyperparameter tuning, model
comparison, and ensembling, as well as whether it uses deep learning or
stochastic models (uses deep learning and uses stochastic model). This
structural information serves as a filter for determining the
applicability of individual failure modes.

In the second stage, the judge evaluates the generated pipeline for the
applicable failure modes. We evaluate all failure modes within a single
LLM call, providing the pipeline structure extracted in the first stage
as additional context. The judge produces a binary decision for each
failure mode, indicating whether the corresponding failure is present in
the solution, and, if so, extracting the lines of code where the failure
mode occurs. Besides filtering out inapplicable failure modes per
pipeline, this two-stage evaluation process separates pipeline
understanding from failure detection and improves consistent performance
across agents.

We inform the failure modes with commonly identified bad
practices ([Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Lones, 2024](#bib.bib5)),
as shown in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 Large-scale Failure Mode Analysis ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").
These failure modes span the stages of data science pipelines  ( ) . We
broadly categorize them into “data leakage” modes, indicated using
\leakbadgeFM, and “non-leakage” modes, using \otherbadgeFM. Detailed
definitions for all failure modes are available
in [Section A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Failure Modes Definitions ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").
Our analysis focuses on a predefined set of common data science failure
modes and therefore does not exhaust all possible issues in a pipeline.
The detected failures should thus be interpreted as a subset of
potential methodological problems. To partially capture failures outside
the taxonomy, we additionally include an other category that allows the
judge to flag and describe additional issues. We also validate the judge
against independent human annotations to assess reliability.

### 3.2 Curated Test Suite with Data Science Fallacies ![[Uncaptioned image]]()

Although the large-scale analysis provides quantitative insights into
agent behaviour over naturalistic tasks, it cannot control what the
correct practice should look like because the tasks were not designed
with specific defects. To assess agents under more controlled
conditions, we design a test suite in which each test targets a specific
failure mode through a predefined perturbation. For example, to evaluate
whether agents appropriately handle class imbalance, we construct a task
with a highly imbalanced target distribution and examine whether the
generated pipeline adopts suitable practices. Each test follows a
“unit-test-style” design: the relevant pitfall is deliberately embedded
in the task, and failure indicates that the agent did not identify or
appropriately address the targeted issue. For each task, we additionally
define multiple levels of hints that vary in instructional explicitness.
The base condition provides no hint about the failure mode. We then
increase the amount of guidance.

To determine whether an agent exhibits the appropriate behaviour for a
given failure mode, we use an LLM-based judge to inspect the generated
pipeline. For each test, we predefine the set of acceptable treatments
corresponding to the targeted failure mode, and the judge checks whether
any of these treatments is present. To obtain a stable estimation, we
run each agent-task-hint combination five times and aggregate the
results across runs. The six designed tests are shown in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Curated Test Suite with Data Science Fallacies ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science"),
with the formalization and evaluation defined in each row. These failure
modes cover different stages of data science pipelines. We select these
failure modes that satisfy three criteria: (1) can be instantiated
through a controlled dataset or task design, (2) the expected
methodological response can be specified clearly enough to support a
controlled evaluation, and (3) the targeted decision can be isolated
from other parts of the pipeline with limited confounding.

### 3.3 Skill Gap Analysis with Expert Data Science Solutions ![[Uncaptioned image]]()

The previous evaluations primarily focus on identifying practices that
violate established data science principles, hence compromising the
validity of a solution. However, the absence of such failures does not
necessarily imply that a pipeline is highly effective. For example, an
agent may produce a methodologically valid pipeline while ignoring a
better task formulation, or a non-obvious feature transformation that
can better capture the underlying structure of the task. To understand
how data science agents compare to human experts in data science, we
curate a set of Kaggle challenges that accept late submissions. For each
task, we submit the predictions or solutions produced by agents to the
leaderboard and compare their performance with high-performing
solutions. The leaderboard provides an external reference for assessing
generated pipelines. Moreover, we compare both the predictive
performance and the modelling practice used by high-ranking solutions.
This helps identify practices that contribute to performance gains even
when their absence does not constitute a methodological failure.
Additionally, this helps in understanding the true performance of
agents-generated solutions on realistic challenges. As an additional
reference, we compare the DS agent solutions with the solution of
AutoGluon on selected tasks.

### 4.1 Experimental setup

#### Datasets.

The evaluation set contains 220 tasks paired with corresponding datasets
and is used for the large-scale failure mode analysis ![[Uncaptioned
image]](). The tasks are drawn from five sources: 108 from
STRABLE ([Blayer et al., 2026](#bib.bib38)), 44 from Kaggle, 36 from
OpenML, 24 from UCI¹¹ 1 Kaggle: [kaggle.com](https://www.kaggle.com/)
 OpenML: [openml.org](https://www.openml.org/)  UCI:
[archive.ics.uci.edu](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/), and 8 from
MLE-bench ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)). The benchmark comprises 123
regression tasks, of which 122 have a single output and one has
multi-outputs, and 97 classification tasks, of which 54 are binary and
43 are multi-class. To prepare the datasets, we perform an 80:20 split
for the train and test sets. At runtime, agents are provided with both
sets and are expected not to utilize the test set at training time,
following the correct practice. Besides, a separate set of 17 tasks is
used for the curated test suite ![[Uncaptioned image]](), with 3
underlying datasets. Additional dataset details are available in
[Section A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Dataset Details ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").

#### Data science agents.

We evaluate three data science agents, namely a ReAct baseline ([Yao et
al., 2022](#bib.bib36)), AIDE ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)), and
MLEvolve ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). MLEvolve is the
highest-ranking open-source DS agent in MLEBench ([Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12)), while AIDE consistently achieved the strongest
classification performance in the recent benchmark by [Nayak et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib26). MLEvolve is a self-evolving multi-agent system that
iteratively optimizes ML pipelines using methods such as progressive
Monte Carlo Graph Search, retrospective memory, and hierarchical
adaptive code generation. AIDE generates pipelines using an iterative
code refinement process, leveraging evaluation feedback and execution
logs to progressively improve generated solutions over multiple
iterations. It consistently achieved the strongest classification
performance in the recent benchmark by [Nayak et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib26). For comparison, we set both AIDE and MLEvolve
agents’ steps to 10. Additionally, we include AutoGluon ([Erickson et
al., 2020](#bib.bib19)), a widely used AutoML system, which serves as a
strong non-agentic baseline based on heuristic-driven optimization and
ensemble learning. Comparing these solutions across different failure
modes allows us to obtain insights into what agent frameworks are really
improving.

#### LLMs.

The capacity of LLMs is important to agentic systems, since smaller
models usually perform worse than larger LLMs in agentic tasks. To
understand the impact of different models within agentic data science
frameworks, we use three models of different capacities. We select
qwen-3.5-4b and qwen-3.8-27b, which can be hosted locally on commodity
hardware, and gemini-3.7-flash, which we select because it has a good
performance-efficiency trade-off in agentic tasks  [Google
(2026)](#bib.bib1). All LLM judges use the more capable gemini-3.1-pro.
Additionally, for the curated test suite, we also evaluate gpt-5.6-sol.
To assess the reliability of the LLM judge output, we compare with
independent human annotations. Across 285 failure mode ratings among 19
pipelines, the judge achieves 89.5% agreement with the human annotator
(Cohen’s $`\kappa=0.62`$, 95% CI \[0.50, 0.73\]), with precision (0.71)
and recall (0.65) for detecting present failures. The detailed agreement
breakdown is in
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Human Annotator Agreement ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").

Validation of dataset memorization robustness. Many tabular prediction
tasks are publicly available online, and LLMs are likely to have seen
them in their training data. To understand if a good predictive
performance is partly because the underlying model has seen the tabular
data, we run memorization tests using column header completion ([Bordt
et al., 2024](#bib.bib37); [Lai et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). We then
select a subset of tasks with high and low memorization rates,
respectively. Following a similar procedure as in KramaBench ([Lai et
al., 2026](#bib.bib32)), we then randomize the column names and rerun
the agents on tasks with masked metadata and description. We also rerun
the agents on the unmasked version as a placebo to obtain unbiased
effects. A large difference in $`\Delta_{plc}`$ and $`\Delta_{alt}`$ for
a high-memorization set indicates that the performance is derived from
memorization and vice versa. Finally, we apply the non-parametric
Wilcoxon test to compare whether there are significant predictive
performance differences when tables are memorized. We test this on three
DS agent frameworks paired with gemini-3.7-flash since it has the lowest
data science failure rate. We present the results
in [Section A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Details on memorization check setup ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").
All $`p`$ values are non-significant in both sets with high and low
memorization rates, indicating that memorizing tables does not
significantly affect predictive performance.

shows the prevalence of failure modes in agent frameworks and models. It
shows that the specific failures the pipelines exhibit depend on how
completely they cover the data science workflow. Failures happen either
because agents perform operations incorrectly, or because they outright
skip parts of the workflow (e.g., “No val split”, “No feature
engineering”). More capable models and elaborate frameworks shift
failures from skipping steps to failures within these steps.

Failures occur across models and frameworks. All framework–model
configurations have at least one failure mode that occurs in 43% or more
of the pipelines. Agent frameworks reduce some of the failures of the
ReAct baseline. With gemini-3.7-flash, both AIDE and MLEvolve reduce “No

engineering” from 0.38 to 0.00 and “naive ensembling” from 0.90 to 0.04.
At the same time, frameworks also increase non-leakage failure modes.
AIDE reports only a single evaluation metric in 85%–88% of its pipelines
regardless of the model, compared with 15% and 14% for ReAct with
qwen-3.5-4b and gemini-3.7-flash, which is consistent with its design of
scoring and selecting candidate solutions by a single validation
metric ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). Whether a framework improves
adherence to DS practices depends on the model used. For example, AIDE
lowers “No feature engineering” with gemini-3.7-flash (0.38 to 0.12),
but raises it with qwen-3.8-27b (0.50 to 0.71).

Higher capacity models cover the data-science stages better but do not
follow good practices. The low rates of leakage failures for small
models do not indicate sound practices. ReAct with qwen-3.5-4b never
designates a validation set and rarely performs feature engineering;
consequently, the leakage modes that depend on the agent performing
either do not occur (“Impute before split”, “Ignores temporal order”,
“Scale before split”, and “Feature-selection before split” are all
0.00). Under AIDE, qwen-3.5-4b does use a validation split, resulting in
increased leakage across the related failure mode; for instance,
imputation before splitting increases from 0.00 to 0.08. More capable
models more frequently perform feature engineering and designate a
validation set themselves, but they similarly show elevated leakage
failure modes. For example, gemini-3.7-flash ignores the temporal order
in more than a third of the pipelines over applicable tasks for all
three agents. Overall, the results indicate that model capacity improves
the coverage of the data-science workflow but that higher capacity
models still commit errors within the expanded workflow. Thus, the raw
number of failures over a pipeline is not indicative of the pipelines
quality, since skipping part of the workflow removes the opportunity to
fail within it.

Leakage extends beyond the taxonomy. We additionally analyse the
failures flagged by the judge that fall outside of the fixed taxonomy,
with full details reported
in [Section A.7](#A1.SS7 "A.7 Failure Modes beyond the Taxonomy ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science").
We find additional failures in 37% of the pipelines. Most commonly in
the form of further leakage modes, namely leakage of validation into
train data, e.g., where preprocessing or target encoding is fitted
before cross-validation, and additional leakage of test into train data.
The leakage modes show increasing prevalence with increasing model
capacity, following the same trend as the leakage modes inside the
taxonomy. Notably, 50% of the pipelines of MLEvolve with
gemini-3.7-flash, the configuration with the fewest failures within the
taxonomy, exhibit leakage between validation and train data, even though
MLEvolve includes a dedicated agent for checking leakages ([Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)).

Failure types correlate with reported performance. Lastly, we analyse
how the failures relate to predictive performance. For each task, we
rank the pipelines of each framework–model configuration by the
predictive performance the pipelines achieve on the test set provided to
the agent. Since the configuration (i.e., a combination of model and the
agent framework) influences the failures that occur in a pipeline as
well as its performance, a direct comparison of raw ranks would
primarily reflect differences between these configurations. We therefore
compare each pipeline’s rank to the rank expected for its configuration,
and measure how this difference changes when a failure mode is present
([Section A.6](#A1.SS6 "A.6 Effects of failures on predictive performance ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")).
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Large-scale Analysis Surfaces Recurrent Silent Failures ‣ 4 Surfacing Silent Failures in Data Science Agents ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")
shows that leakage and non-leakage modes affect performance in opposite
directions. All data leakage modes lead to improved rankings, which can
be expected as leakage introduces information from the evaluation data
into the training process. This illustrates why downstream performance
alone is not a good measure of agentic DS capability. The non-leakage
failure modes have the opposite relation: more failure modes correlate
with lower ranks. Not using a validation split (\otherbadgeFM06) to
assess model generalizability has the largest effect, deteriorating the
performance by more than a full rank position on average. An exception
to the pattern is that using only one evaluation metric
(\otherbadgeFM15) is associated with a better rank. We hypothesize that
pipelines that evaluate a single metric optimize narrowly for the metric
specified by the task, which is also the metric we rank by, possibly at
the cost of robustness that a single metric cannot capture. Overall, the
results show that a better score on test data does not imply a better
pipeline, and evaluating agents solely by their scores can reward
leakage modes.

Some methodological failures persist across configurations. The clearest
examples are correlation and causation reasoning (\tfbadgeTF03) and
leakage in feature selection (\tfbadgeTF05), where every configuration
reaches a failure rate of $`1.0`$. Messy data normalization
(\tfbadgeTF02) shows similar behaviour: five configurations fail every
run, while ReAct with gemini-3.7-flash fails at a rate of $`0.8`$. In
contrast, class imbalance (\tfbadgeTF01), domain feature engineering
(\tfbadgeTF04), and metric selection (\tfbadgeTF06) vary substantially
across agents and models, indicating that different methodological
decisions depend on both the underlying LLM and the agent framework.

Stronger models do not consistently yield better data science practice.
Compared to gemini-3.7-flash, gpt-5.6-sol reduces failures for ReAct on
class imbalance (\tfbadgeTF01) and domain feature engineering
(\tfbadgeTF04), but the effect does not generalize across tests. This
suggests that improvements in model capability do not transfer uniformly
to methodological decision-making.

Explicit hints reduce failures, but not all.
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.3 Controlled Evaluation Confirms Consistent Failure Patterns ‣ 4 Surfacing Silent Failures in Data Science Agents ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")
shows that ReAct and AIDE improve sharply as hints become more explicit,
with failure rates approaching zero under the strongest guidance. This
suggests that many failures arise from failing to recognize the relevant
methodological constraint rather than from an inability to carry out the
correct practice once prompted. However, MLEvolve behaves differently:
although moderate hints reduce its failure rate, stronger hints do not
lead to further improvement, and its failure rate remains substantially
higher than ReAct and AIDE under more explicit guidance. This points to
a limitation of more advanced frameworks, where elaborate search and
optimization may fail to maintain explicit constraints during the
pipeline generation.

Case Study. To examine where this gap comes from, we compare the
pipelines generated by ReAct, AIDE, and MLEvolve using gemini-3.7-flash
with the 1st-place solution for the Kaggle Problematic Internet Use
challenge
(Figure [8](#A1.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ A.8 Procedural comparisons between agentic solutions and expert solutions ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")
in

Our test split contains PCIAT-\* variables, including PCIAT-PCIAT_Total,
from which the target sii can be derived, although these variables are
not included in the actual Kaggle test set. ReAct and AIDE selected
these leaked features and consequently achieved local QWK scores near
1.0, despite producing pipelines that would fail under the competition
setting. This again confirms that predictive scores during agents’
optimization are not trustworthy. Structural evaluation further reveals
recurring weaknesses across frameworks: ReAct exhibited preprocessing
leakage, limited feature engineering, arbitrary ensemble weights, and
invalid test IDs; AIDE relied largely on raw features and mishandled
categorical variables; and MLEvolve produced richer domain-informed
features but still introduced leakage during encoding and relied on
equal-weight ensembling. But this case study also indicates that the gap
to expert solutions goes beyond failures. The 1st-place solution has
fundamentally different, creative modelling choices. For example, it
predicts the continuous PCIAT_Total rather than sii directly, applied
optimized thresholds only at the final stage, used a Tweedie objective,
adopted multi-seed 10-fold cross-validation, and combined diverse model
families. These findings can inform future DS agent design to also focus
on higher-level choices in problem formulation, task-specific
objectives, validation design, feature engineering, and model diversity.

## 5 Conclusion

With the strong coding capabilities of large language models, they are
increasingly integrated in data science agents. With higher-capacity
models and advanced data science scaffolding, recent benchmarks show
improving downstream performance. In this work, we systematically
evaluate failure modes in the pipelines that DS agents implement to get
a more nuanced view of their true data science practices beyond
accuracy. We perform a large-scale analysis across 220 tasks and nine
configurations of model–agent configurations, an extensible controlled
test suite, and comparisons with expert real-world solutions. We surface
many different failures that purely downstream performance evaluation
masks. In fact, our results indicate that data leakage failure modes –
which we identify to exist in many forms – can actually lead to stronger
predictive performance on a test set. We also find that more
sophisticated agent frameworks do not necessarily eliminate these
failures. Despite the presence of dedicated data leakage checks in some
frameworks, these can still exhibit leakage failures. This suggests that
preventing such failures requires more than adding specialized
components. Agent designs must reason more explicitly about
methodological validity throughout the pipeline. Altogether, our
findings reinforce the need to evaluate agentic data science beyond
accuracy. We envision that our findings will improve agentic DS
evaluations broadly, and inform the design of future agentic data
science systems and models.

### A.2 Failure Modes Definitions

In
[Table 6](#A1.T6 "In A.2 Failure Modes Definitions ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")
we include the definition of each of the failure modes evaluated in the
large-scale analysis.

| ID   | Failure Mode                       | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ---- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| FM01 | Wrong task formulation             | The agent frames the modelling problem in a way that discards the signal the task actually provides.                                                                                                                                           |
| FM02 | Peeking at test data during EDA    | Exploring test data during initial analysis creates untestable assumptions baked into model design.                                                                                                                                            |
| FM03 | Not cleaning data                  | Ignoring data duplication, missing values, and outliers/inconsistencies before model building.                                                                                                                                                 |
| FM04 | Imputation before splitting        | Performing mean/median imputation before splitting – leaks test statistics into training.                                                                                                                                                      |
| FM05 | Ignoring temporal order            | Randomly splitting time-series data without respecting temporal order – introduces look-ahead bias.                                                                                                                                            |
| FM06 | No train/val split                 | Model trained and evaluated on the same data – performance is a misleading overestimate.                                                                                                                                                       |
| FM07 | No feature engineering             | The agent uses raw columns as-is (at most basic imputation/encoding) with no derived features or domain-informed transforms, even where the task or column names suggest obvious ones.                                                         |
| FM08 | Scaling before splitting           | Fitting scaler on the full dataset before splitting – test statistics leak into training.                                                                                                                                                      |
| FM09 | Feature selection before splitting | Selecting features on the full dataset before splitting – causes test leakage.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| FM10 | Wrong model type for task          | Using a regression model for a classification task (or vice versa) – conceptual mismatch.                                                                                                                                                      |
| FM11 | No regularisation in DL            | Training deep learning models without dropout or weight decay – prone to overfitting.                                                                                                                                                          |
| FM12 | Naive ensembling                   | When the agent combines multiple models, it uses an unweighted/equal-weight average or simple majority vote with no tuning of the combination itself, where a more sophisticated combination is straightforward given what’s already computed. |
| FM13 | Reusing test set across variants   | Same test set used to select among multiple models – test set gradually leaks into model selection (sequential overfitting).                                                                                                                   |
| FM14 | Single eval of stochastic model    | Single evaluation of a stochastic/unstable model; results may not reflect true performance.                                                                                                                                                    |
| FM15 | Using only one eval metric         | Single metric gives an incomplete picture; different metrics reveal different failure modes.                                                                                                                                                   |

Table 6: Failure modes definition.

### A.4 Human Annotator Agreement

To assess the reliability of the LLM judge, a human annotator
independently re-labeled all 15 failure modes on 19 randomly sampled
pipelines (11 of the 30 originally sampled pipelines were excluded
because their generated code was no longer recoverable on disk).
Agreement was scored on a binary present/not-present basis per failure
mode; a diagnostic check showed the annotator’s absent/N/A distinction
did not track the judge’s own applicability gating consistently in
either direction, so that distinction was not scored as disagreement.
Across all 285 (pipeline × failure-mode) ratings, the judge agreed with
the human annotator on 89.5% of ratings (Cohen’s $`\kappa=0.62`$, 95% CI
\[0.50, 0.73\], via a pipeline-cluster bootstrap), indicating
substantial agreement. Precision and recall for the present class were
comparable (precision = 0.71, recall = 0.65), with no significant
directional bias toward over- or under-flagging failures ($`p=0.29`$).
Disagreement concentrated in a small subset of failure modes, i.e.,
naive ensembling and reliance on a single evaluation metric (each at
0.68 agreement), while the remaining 13 failure modes achieved
$`\geq`$0.79 agreement, 9 of them $`\geq`$0.89.

### A.6 Effects of failures on predictive performance

To calculate the failure modes’ effect on the predictive performance, we
provide the correlation between the two variables. However, scores alone
are not comparable because tasks use different metrics. Therefore, we
use the rank among all solutions as the measurement of predictive
performance. We run generated solutions on the tasks and calculate the
score on the pre-processed test set. Finally, we rank the solutions
across all 9 configurations. A naive correlation between failure modes
and predictive rank is confounded by model and agent capability. For
example, qwen-3.5-4b may exhibit fewer detected failures because it
produces simpler pipelines, while also ranking poorly. We therefore
estimate the effect of each failure mode after controlling for model and
agent identity.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \text{rank}\sim\text{present}+C(\text{model})+C(\text{agent})
       ```                                                            |     |

For each failure mode, we fit a model that predicts a pipeline’s rank
from three things: whether the failure mode is present, which model
produced it, and which agent produced it. Giving the model and agent
their own separate adjustments, a model that’s simply bad overall
doesn’t get mistaken for the failure mode itself being harmful. The one
number we care about is how much presence of the failure mode shifts the
rank, once that adjustment is made.

For visualization in
Figure [7](#A1.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ A.6 Effects of failures on predictive performance ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science"),
we use the corresponding partial residual:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       r_{i}=\beta_{1}\text{present}_{i}+\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}.
       ```                                                       |     |

Its group means are $`0`$ when the failure is absent and $`\beta_{1}`$
when present, so the plotted difference directly represents the adjusted
effect.

### A.7 Failure Modes beyond the Taxonomy

In addition to the failure modes captured in the taxonomy, we evaluate
the additional failure modes the LLM-judge reports as a list of
free-text failures
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Large-scale Failure Mode Analysis ‣ 3 Evaluation Methodology ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")).
Across pipelines, the judge flags 2,294 additional failures. We filter
these failures to only include actual violations of data science
principles, filtering out other issues flagged by the judge, such as
failures to produce code or implementation bugs, yielding 1,343
additional data science failures. We assign these failure modes into
categories that we extract from manually screening them and then assign
a category to each failure by independent keyword matching and through
an LLM-judge (qwen-3.5-9b), where disagreements are manually resolved.
On a sample of 59 reports that were labelled blindly, the final labels
agree in 95% of the cases.

Additional failure modes.
[Table 11](#A1.T11 "In A.7 Failure Modes beyond the Taxonomy ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ The Silent Failures in Agentic Data Science")
shows the three most prominent categories.
Validation$`\rightarrow`$train leakage concerns instances where
information from the validation data leaks into the trained model,
without involving the test set. For instance, preprocessing or target
encoding fitted on the full training data before cross-validation, early
stopping on the fold the model is scored on, and ensemble weights fitted
and scored on the same predictions. Test$`\rightarrow`$train leakage
encompasses leakage of test data into the training that falls outside of
the taxonomy, such as transforms (e.g., label encoding, TF-IDF, or
aggregated features) that are fitted on the training and test data.
Flawed training protocol includes training without early stopping,
predicting with the model of a single fold, and not retraining the final
model on the full training data. Less frequent additional categories are
inconsistent preprocessing of training and test data, nominal features
treated as continuous, identifier columns used as features, target
leakage through a feature, and incorrectly implemented metrics, each
occurring in at most 6% of the pipelines.

Observations. The additional failures follow the same pattern described

Validation$`\rightarrow`$train leakage increases with model capacity,
from 8% of the pipelines for qwen-3.5-4b to 16% for qwen-3.8-27b and 26%

judge reports these failures without being prompted for them
specifically, the shares should be interpreted as lower bounds on their
actual prevalence.
