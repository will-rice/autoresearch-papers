# Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation

Liyang Zhao    Yong Cao    Haibin Ling

###### Abstract

Automated research ideation has advanced rapidly, with multi-agent
workflows that generate whole research ideas end to end. These
frameworks leave largely unexamined the step that seeds a good idea:
identifying an actionable limitation from literature. In this paper, we
focus on this first step in the research ideation pipeline. Starting
from a pilot study, we find that as large language models (LLMs) scale
from 0.6B to 32B parameters, they become far better at recognizing which
limitation later work addressed, but no better at generating one,
suggesting that the bottleneck lies in the supervision signal rather
than model capacity. We therefore construct ActLim-Bench from forward
citations, yielding 18,279 evidence-linked supervision triples with
human verification. On this signal, we propose a three-stage
post-training pipeline: supervised fine-tuning, verifier-filtered
rejection-sampling fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning with the
verifier as an online dense reward. Experimental results show that our
method achieves the highest recall score compared to existing methods
among all frontier models. We further show that our generated
limitations are preferred in 64.9% of non-tied human comparisons and
improve downstream ideation systems. We will release our data and code
upon publication.

## 1 Introduction

The rapid progress of LLMs has sparked growing interest in automated
research ideation. Existing multi-agent frameworks can autonomously
generate end-to-end research ideas, from literature analysis to proposal
generation ([Wang et al. 2024](#bib.bib1); [Baek et al.
2025](#bib.bib3); [Lu et al. 2024](#bib.bib5)). Despite these advances,
one fundamental step remains largely overlooked: identifying limitations
from existing literature that can serve as the foundation for novel
research ideas.

Therefore, as illustrated at the top of
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"),
we focus on the first stage of the research ideation pipeline:
identifying a source-supported limitation of given scientific
publication that can motivate concrete follow-up work. We call this
_actionable limitation generation_. The task is narrower than peer
review and more inspectable than end-to-end ideation, reducing error
propagation in later stages.

The central challenge is supervision. Existing limitation benchmarks
draw from author-written sections, reviewer critiques, or controlled
synthetic perturbations ([Faizullah et al. 2024](#bib.bib8); [Al Azher
et al. 2025b](#bib.bib9); [Xu et al. 2025](#bib.bib10)). LimAgents adds
citation context but still derives reference limitations from author
statements and OpenReview comments ([Al Azher et al.
2025a](#bib.bib11)). Concurrent work uses future publications to
evaluate complete proposals or models idea evolution over citation
graphs ([Jiang 2026](#bib.bib19); [Wang et al. 2026](#bib.bib20); [Gao
et al. 2026](#bib.bib21)).

These data sources and attempts capture what authors disclose or
reviewers criticize, but provide limited evidence about which
shortcomings later become productive starting points for research. Nor
does model scale resolve this gap. In a controlled study, larger models
become better at selecting a future-pursued limitation from candidates,
while source-only generation does not improve consistently. This
judgment–generation asymmetry suggests that models can increasingly
recognize useful shortcomings when shown them, yet still lack a direct
target for producing them.

![Refer to caption](2610.23038v1/teaser_figure.png)

Figure 1: Overview of our framework. Using forward-citation supervision
as the training signal, we align general LLMs into domain experts for
actionable limitation generation.

Future citations provide that target. Later citing papers reveal which
limitations they adopted as motivation and attempted to address. This
turns a subjective judgment into an auditable supervision signal. As
shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Forward-citation verification. ‣ 3 ActLim-Bench ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"),
we construct ActLim-Bench by linking each source-supported limitation to
the later citing work that takes it up. The benchmark contains 18,279
evidence-linked instances, with supporting spans retained from both the
source and citing papers.

We then use the same verified supervision signal in three training
stages. Verified limitations first provide direct demonstrations; the
verifier then selects useful model-generated variants; finally, a
set-level reward optimizes match quality, coverage, redundancy, and
format across the complete three-item response. The resulting 8B
generator, ActLim-8B, requires only the source paper at inference time.

Experimental results show that ActLim-8B reaches a graded recall of
0.452 on the temporally held-out NLP test set, compared with 0.262 for
the strongest zero-shot baseline, and is preferred in blind human
evaluation. With NLP-only training, it transfers to Computer Vision at
0.314 versus 0.252. Its limitations also improve the retrospective
alignment of ideas from a fixed proposer from 0.302 to 0.480.

In summary, our contributions are threefold: (1) We introduce
forward-citation supervision for actionable limitation generation and
present ActLim-Bench, an auditable benchmark of 18,279 evidence-linked
instances with high annotation quality; (2) We develop a three-stage
training framework that exploits the same verified signal for
supervision, synthetic data filtering, and reward optimization, yielding
ActLim-8B, which requires only the source paper at inference time; (3)
Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our method
through both automatic and human evaluations across two domains.

## 2 Related Work

#### Scientific feedback and limitation generation.

Automated scientific feedback includes full-review generation and
reviewer-assistance systems ([Yuan and Liu 2022](#bib.bib12); [Zhou et
al. 2024](#bib.bib13); [Zhu et al. 2025](#bib.bib14); [Li et al.
2026](#bib.bib16); [Biswas et al. 2026](#bib.bib15); [Idahl and Ahmadi
2025](#bib.bib44); [Zeng et al. 2025](#bib.bib45)). These systems assess
broad dimensions such as correctness, novelty, clarity, and
significance, whereas limitation generation isolates paper shortcomings.
Literature-review agents instead synthesize evidence across collections
of papers ([Zhang et al. 2025](#bib.bib17); [Go et al.
2026](#bib.bib18)). LimGen learns from author-written limitation
sections ([Faizullah et al. 2024](#bib.bib8)); BAGELS combines venue
limitations and external reviews with retrieval ([Al Azher et al.
2025b](#bib.bib9)); LimitGen adds a limitation taxonomy, controlled
perturbations, and human-written targets ([Xu et al. 2025](#bib.bib10));
and LimAgents uses author statements, OpenReview comments, and citation
context ([Al Azher et al. 2025a](#bib.bib11)). Our target is different:
each instance links a source paper to a source-supported limitation and
to later citing work that adopts the limitation as motivation and
attempts to address it.

#### Research ideation and future-grounded evaluation.

Literature-grounded systems generate hypotheses, research problems,
methods, experiments, or complete proposals using retrieval, scholarly
graphs, critique, and multi-agent refinement ([Wang et al.
2024](#bib.bib1); [Yang et al. 2024](#bib.bib2); [Baek et al.
2025](#bib.bib3); [Li et al. 2025](#bib.bib4); [Lu et al.
2024](#bib.bib5)). Dedicated benchmarks assess research-ideation and
scientific-discovery capability ([Qiu et al. 2025](#bib.bib47); [Liu et
al. 2026](#bib.bib46); [Lou et al. 2025](#bib.bib36)). Human studies
report strong proposal-stage judgments for LLM-generated ideas, while
execution-based evaluation shows that such judgments do not
straightforwardly predict completed research outcomes ([Si et al.
2025a](#bib.bib6); [Si et al. 2026](#bib.bib7)). Recent work uses
held-out future literature to evaluate proposals or supervise
research-idea generation ([Jiang 2026](#bib.bib19); [Wang et al.
2026](#bib.bib20); [Gao et al. 2026](#bib.bib21)). We operate at a
finer, independently inspectable granularity: the specific shortcoming
in a given paper that later work takes up as motivation.

#### Citation analysis and verifier-guided training.

Citation contexts support citation-intent classification and
citation-oriented related-work annotation ([Cohan et al.
2019](#bib.bib22); [Li et al. 2022](#bib.bib23)). Temporal citation
analysis and early-impact prediction examine how scientific impact
develops over time ([Arnaout et al. 2026](#bib.bib25); [Zhao et al.
2025](#bib.bib24)). We use forward-citation contexts not only as
scholarly metadata, but as evidence for a structured generation target.
Our later stages build on self-training, reward-ranked fine-tuning, and
online reinforcement learning ([Dong et al. 2023](#bib.bib26); [Shao et
al. 2024](#bib.bib27); [Guo et al. 2025](#bib.bib28)).

## 3 ActLim-Bench

#### Source and candidate retrieval.

To ensure fair training and evaluation, Train and Dev sets contain NLP
source papers from 2017–2023. The temporally held-out Test split
contains papers from 2024–2025, and for every pair the recorded
publication date of $`Q`$ is strictly later than that of $`P`$. A frozen
extractor reads the abstract and introduction of $`Q`$ and returns
candidate limitations with citing-paper evidence spans; source-paper
evidence is then retrieved from the abstract, introduction, and
conclusion of $`P`$. The extractor only proposes candidates. Acceptance
is decided by the verifier below.

#### Forward-citation verification.

A candidate $`\hat{\ell}`$ is retained only when
$`A(P,Q,\hat{\ell})\wedge C(P,\hat{\ell})\wedge S(P,\hat{\ell})\wedge R(Q,\hat{\ell})`$.
Attribution $`A`$ requires that $`Q`$ attribute the shortcoming to $`P`$
or to a clearly defined method family containing $`P`$. Consistency
$`C`$ requires agreement with the method, assumptions, evidence, and
stated scope of $`P`$. Specificity $`S`$ requires a concrete component,
mechanism, assumption, data condition, or evaluation constraint rather
than a generic call for more experiments. Response $`R`$ requires that
the main contribution of $`Q`$ attempt to address it. Each accepted
record keeps $`(P,\hat{\ell},e_{P},e_{Q},Q)`$, where $`e_{P}`$ supports
consistency and $`e_{Q}`$ supports attribution and response. Each
accepted record retains both source- and citing-paper evidence, making
individual targets checkable rather than merely scored.

#### Aggregation and splits.

Different citing papers may pursue semantically equivalent limitations
of the same source paper. We merge these into a canonical paper-level
target $`\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{P}`$ while retaining every supporting
citing paper and span. We therefore distinguish three units throughout:
a citation pair $`(P,Q)`$, an evidence-linked triple $`(P,\ell,Q)`$, and
an aggregated target $`\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{P}`$. Splits are built at the
source-paper level, with source and citing papers disjoint across
splits. To limit version and near-duplicate leakage we collapse arXiv
and conference versions, normalize metadata, remove highly similar
abstracts, audit citation-graph overlap, and inspect similar limitation
paraphrases across splits. All prompts, extraction rules, and
verification thresholds were fixed before Test was finalized. A separate
Computer Vision set of 2024–2025 papers is built by the identical
pipeline and used only for transfer evaluation.
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Forward-citation verification. ‣ 3 ActLim-Bench ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")
summarizes the dataset statistics.

#### Quality control.

Two annotators audited 400 accepted triples, equally split between Train
and Test. Requiring every criterion (see
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Triple-level audit ‣ Appendix A Benchmark Construction ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"))
gives a triple-level precision of 0.87 (95% Wilson CI $`[0.833,0.899]`$)
and inter-annotator agreement $`\kappa=0.74`$ ([Cohen
1960](#bib.bib40)). Precision is comparable on Train (0.88) and Test
(0.86). A separate pipeline audit obtains $`\kappa=0.76`$ on pair
acceptance, and 91% of accepted pairs contain at least one fully valid
limitation.

Direct attribution accounts for 82% of the audited triples and has
precision 0.95. The remaining 18% use attribution to a named method
family containing the source paper, with precision 0.89. Full sampling
strata, rubrics, and adjudicated examples are in the supplement.

## 4 Method

#### Task definition.

Let $`x_{P}`$ denote the visible text of a source paper $`P`$, and let
$`\mathcal{G}_{P}`$ denote its observed set of future-pursued
limitations. Given $`x_{P}`$ and an output number $`K`$, the model
generates
$`\mathbf{Y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{K})\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{P},K)`$.
Each output should identify a technically specific limitation that is
consistent with the source paper and can motivate concrete follow-up
work. Because $`\mathcal{G}_{P}`$ is recovered from later citations, it
records observed future response rather than every valid limitation of
$`P`$. At inference time, the model receives only $`x_{P}`$ and $`K`$;
citing papers, observed targets, and verifier scores are unavailable to
models.

#### From judgment to supervision.

Before presenting our method, we first conduct a pilot study to
investigate how limitation discrimination and generation scale with
model size (see Experiments for more details). The resulting
judgment–generation asymmetry
(Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.5 Judgment–Generation Scaling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"))
motivates our training design. When candidate limitations are made
explicit, models increasingly recognize which one later work will
pursue, yet this judgment does not reliably emerge in open-ended
generation. Standard post-training recipes specify how to learn from
demonstrations, preferences, or rewards, but are largely agnostic to
which scientific shortcomings should define those signals. For
actionable limitation generation, this target is the missing ingredient.
We therefore use forward-citation verification at three cumulative
interfaces (see
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Forward-citation verification. ‣ 3 ActLim-Bench ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")):
first to define what the model should imitate, then to select which of
its own generations are worth learning from, and finally to assign
graded credit to a complete response.

#### Graded verifier.

The frozen training verifier $`V_{\mathrm{train}}`$ scores a generated
limitation $`y`$ against an observed target $`\ell`$ as
$`m_{V_{\mathrm{train}}}(y,\ell)\in\{0,0.2,0.6,1.0\}`$, corresponding to
_none_, _category_, _facet_, and _specific_ matches. A category match
recovers only the broad type of shortcoming; a facet match also
identifies the affected component and aspect; and a specific match
recovers the concrete shortcoming pursued by later work. The verifier
reads the source paper and the candidate–target pair, but not the citing
paper.
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Graded verifier. ‣ 4 Method ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")
illustrates the four levels and the one-to-one matching used throughout
training and evaluation process.

![Refer to caption](2610.23038v1/img/Figure4.png)

Figure 3: Graded one-to-one matching. Predictions are aligned with

#### Stage 1: Verified demonstrations.

The first requirement is to make the desired target explicit. The
scaling result shows that models can recognize a future-pursued
limitation when alternatives are provided; Stage 1 turns such
limitations into supervised generation targets. We perform supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) on verified future-pursued limitations. For each
paper, we serialize $`N_{P}=\min(K,|\mathcal{G}_{P}|)`$ targets into a
structured response and explicitly request $`N_{P}`$ outputs. When
$`|\mathcal{G}_{P}|>K`$, we use a fixed subset selected to maximize
diversity in the supporting citing evidence; the subset is not resampled
across epochs. When $`|\mathcal{G}_{P}|<K`$, we request the observed
number rather than padding the response with unsupported items. This
count-conditioned objective teaches both the target construct and the
required list structure.

#### Stage 2: Verifier-guided expansion.

Verified demonstrations provide precise anchors, but each paper exposes
only a finite set of canonical formulations. The Stage-1 policy can
produce additional paraphrases and refinements, while model likelihood
or reference-free self-selection cannot determine whether these variants
remain aligned with future-pursued targets. We therefore turn the
verifier into a data-selection rule and perform verifier-filtered
rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RSFT). From the Stage-1 policy, we
sample a candidate pool $`\mathcal{C}_{P}`$ and retain facet-or-better
matches:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{P}=\left\{y\in\mathcal{C}_{P}:\max_{\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{P}}m_{V_{\mathrm{train}}}(y,\ell)\geq 0.6\right\}.
``` |  |

After removing semantic near-duplicates with a multilingual text
embedding model ([Chen et al. 2024](#bib.bib35)), we greedily group
retained candidates into diverse $`K`$-item responses and continue
fine-tuning. This stage broadens how a verified target can be expressed
without replacing future-grounded selection with the generator’s own
confidence.

#### Stage 3: Set-level optimization.

Candidate filtering uses the verifier as a threshold and evaluates
limitations largely one at a time. The task, however, requires a set of
complementary shortcomings: each item should match deeply, the response
should cover distinct targets, and repeated paraphrases should not
receive additional credit. We therefore retain the verifier’s graded
judgment and apply it online to complete responses using GRPO ([Shao et
al. 2024](#bib.bib27); [Guo et al. 2025](#bib.bib28)). For
$`\mathbf{Y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{K})`$, the reward is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(P,\mathbf{Y})=\alpha\,\operatorname{Match}+\beta\,\operatorname{Cover}-\gamma\,\operatorname{Red}+\delta\,\operatorname{Fmt},
``` |  |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Match}(P,\mathbf{Y})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{P}|}\max_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{(y,\ell)\in\mathcal{M}}m_{V_{\mathrm{train}}}(y,\ell),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Cover}(P,\mathbf{Y})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\max_{\mathcal{M}_{0.6}}|\mathcal{M}_{0.6}|}{\min(K,|\mathcal{G}_{P}|)}.`$ |  |

Here $`\mathcal{M}`$ is a maximum-weight one-to-one matching, and
$`\mathcal{M}_{0.6}`$ is a maximum-cardinality one-to-one matching over
facet-or-better edges. Match measures graded target recovery, whereas
Cover explicitly rewards distinct facet-or-better matches.
$`\operatorname{Red}`$ is the mean pairwise semantic similarity among
generated limitations, and $`\operatorname{Fmt}`$ is one only when the
response contains exactly $`K`$ parseable items. We select
$`(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)=(1.0,\,0.3,\,0.2,\,0.1)`$ on the Dev set.

Starting from the Stage-2 checkpoint, the current policy samples a group
of complete responses for each source paper. The frozen verifier scores
each response, and GRPO updates the policy using relative advantages
within the group, with a KL penalty to the Stage-2 policy.

#### One signal, three forms of supervision.

The verifier retains the same semantics across all three stages while
its training role changes. Stage 1 uses future-pursued limitations as
targets; Stage 2 uses the same judgment to curate model-generated
training data; and Stage 3 uses it to score the quality and coverage of
a complete response. The stages therefore ground *what* to generate,
broaden *how* the target can be expressed, and coordinate multiple
limitations as a set. The verifier is a training-time scaffold:
ActLim-8B requires neither citing papers nor verifier calls at
deployment.

## 5 Experiments

We evaluate our method via two domains, then isolate the two choices
that define ActLim: the source of supervision and the role assigned to
the verification signal. We next test the judgment–generation asymmetry
across model scale, and close with human and downstream validation.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Dataset and baselines.

We evaluate our method on papers from both NLP and CV domains. Each
system receives the abstract, introduction, and conclusion and returns
exactly $`K=3`$ limitations. Citing papers, evidence spans, and verified
targets are withheld. Retrieval baselines access only documents
published no later than the source paper.

For comparison, we select representative open source models and agentic
methods. Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B ([Yang et al. 2025](#bib.bib29)) provide
same-family capability references for the trainable Qwen3-8B generator.
Qwen-72B-Chat ([Bai et al. 2023](#bib.bib48)), Claude-3-Opus,
Gemini-1.0-Ultra ([Gemini Team et al. 2023](#bib.bib49)), and
GPT-4-Turbo provide additional zero-shot references. Exact API
snapshots, release dates, and documented knowledge cutoffs are reported
in the supplement. Prompting baselines use Qwen3-8B ([Wei et al.
2022](#bib.bib30); [Madaan et al. 2023](#bib.bib31)). Retrieval and
multi-agent comparisons include pre-publication RAG ([Lewis et al.
2020](#bib.bib33)), a MARG-style system adapted to three outputs
([D’Arcy et al. 2024](#bib.bib32)), and BAGELS-RAG with retrieval
restricted to pre-publication documents ([Al Azher et al.
2025b](#bib.bib9)). Trainable conditions use three seeds. Main-table
entries are seed means; paired intervals use 10,000 paper-level
bootstrap resamples after averaging aligned seed scores. Human pairwise
tests use exact binomial tests with Holm correction ([Holm
1979](#bib.bib42)). See supplement for detailed prompts and parameter
settings.

#### Evaluators and metrics.

The construction evaluator, training verifier $`V_{\mathrm{train}}`$,
and benchmark evaluator $`V_{\mathrm{B}}`$ use one frozen DeepSeek model
identifier with role-specific prompts, following LLM-as-a-judge
evaluation ([Liu et al. 2023](#bib.bib37); [Zheng et al.
2023](#bib.bib38)); only the construction evaluator sees citing papers.
The independent evaluator $`V_{\mathrm{I}}`$ uses GLM-5.2 ([GLM-5 Team
2026](#bib.bib50)) and is not used for construction, filtering, reward,
or model selection. We therefore treat $`V_{\mathrm{I}}`$ as primary and
report $`V_{\mathrm{B}}`$ as a same-benchmark robustness check.

For source paper $`P`$ and generated set
$`\mathbf{Y}_{P}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})`$, let

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{(y,\ell)\in\mathcal{M}}m_{V}(y,\ell),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{GRec@3}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})}{|\mathcal{G}_{P}|},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{GMatch@3}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})}{\min(3,|\mathcal{G}_{P}|)}.`$ |  |

The maximum ranges over one-to-one matchings
$`\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbf{Y}_{P}\times\mathcal{G}_{P}`$ and is
solved by the Hungarian algorithm ([Kuhn 1955](#bib.bib43)). GRec@3 is
macro-averaged over papers. SpecRec@3 uses the same matching with binary
weights $`\mathbb{I}[m_{V}(y,\ell)=1]`$. GMatch@3 removes the
output-count ceiling when $`|\mathcal{G}_{P}|>3`$ and is used only in
the target-count analysis.

#### Held-out NLP results.

As shown in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"),
ActLim-8B reaches 0.452 GRec@3_(I) and 0.241 SpecRec@3_(I), compared
with the strongest zero-shot values of 0.262 and 0.098. The 8B model
therefore gains 0.190 graded recall, more than doubles specific-level
recovery, and exceeds the same-family 32B reference. The benchmark
evaluator gives the same ordering, 0.485 versus 0.271. The cumulative
stages are also visible in the table: verifier-guided expansion adds
0.058 GRec@3_(I) and 0.055 SpecRec@3_(I) over verified SFT; set-level
GRPO adds a further 0.042 and 0.015. Expansion contributes most of the
specific recovery, while the set-level objective adds graded alignment
and coverage across the three-item response.

#### Transfer to CV.

The CV test set contains 1,737 source papers from CVPR, ICCV, and ECCV
2024–2025, with 3,961 evidence-linked triples and 3,562 aggregated
targets. No CV data are used for training or model selection. ActLim-8B
reaches 0.314 GRec@3_(I), 0.062 above the strongest zero-shot system
(95% CI $`[0.048,0.076]`$), and 0.116 above Qwen3-8B
($`[0.102,0.130]`$). SpecRec@3_(I) rises from the strongest zero-shot
score of 0.086 to 0.141. The Stage-1–Stage-2–Stage-3 sequence continues
at 0.232, 0.279, and 0.314, showing that both verifier-guided expansion
and set-level optimization transfer beyond the training domain. A
separate 100-triple audit yields 0.83 joint precision (95% Wilson CI
$`[0.745,0.891]`$; $`\kappa=0.71`$). CV papers contain fewer observed
targets on average than NLP papers (2.05 versus 2.50), so absolute
recall values are interpreted within domain.

#### Target-count and length controls.

The gains persist across target-count strata and after matching output
length. ActLim-8B produces shorter outputs than Qwen3-32B (19.7 versus
29.9 tokens); stratified analyses are in the supplement.

### 5.3 Why Forward-Citation Supervision Helps

To isolate the supervision source, we train four models on the same
1,240 papers, 2,795 examples, and approximately 2.1M target tokens,
using the same recipe and three seeds. Target-length distributions are
matched. Forward- citation supervision reaches 0.353 GRec@3_(I), 0.117
above the strongest alternative, and 0.172 SpecRec@3_(I), compared with
0.087 for controlled perturbations
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.3 Why Forward-Citation Supervision Helps ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")).
The benchmark evaluator gives a 0.120 gain (95% CI $`[0.101,0.140]`$).
The effect is therefore not limited to broad category agreement; it is
largest at the specific level that can support a concrete follow-up.

The author-supervised model provides a useful diagnostic. It reaches
0.47 GRec@3 on held-out author targets but 0.193 on future-pursued
targets. The model can fit the author-written signal; what changes is
which shortcomings the supervision teaches it to recover.

| Supervision              | GRec@3_(I) | SpecRec@3_(I) | GRec@3_(B) |
|--------------------------|------------|---------------|------------|
| Author-stated            | 0.193      | 0.072         | 0.198      |
| Reviewer-written         | 0.219      | 0.081         | 0.224      |
| Controlled perturbations | 0.236      | 0.087         | 0.242      |
| Forward-citation         | 0.353      | 0.172         | 0.362      |

### 5.4 How Verification Enters Training

Starting from the same Stage-1 checkpoint, DPO ([Rafailov et al.
2023](#bib.bib34)), verifier-guided expansion, and matched set-level
GRPO each consume 9,000 generated items and 9,000 verifier calls. They
differ only in whether the judgment becomes a binary preference, an
item-level selection rule, or a response-level reward. As
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.4 How Verification Enters Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")
shows, verifier-guided expansion reaches 0.410 GRec@3_(I), while the
matched set-level objective reaches 0.425. Combining expansion with
set-level GRPO gives the full ActLim-8B result of 0.452. Under the
matched budget, response-level optimization improves over candidate
filtering by 0.015 GRec@3_(I) and 0.021 GRec@3_(B) (95% CI
$`[0.005,0.037]`$).

The reward components make complementary contributions. Match alone
reaches 0.458 GRec@3_(B); adding coverage, redundancy, and format terms
raises the score successively to 0.472, 0.481, and 0.485. These results
mirror the three roles developed in the method: verified targets
establish what to generate, expansion broadens acceptable realizations,
and set-level reward coordinates a complete response.

| Strategy                  | Use of judgment        | GRec@3_(I) | GRec@3_(B) |
|---------------------------|------------------------|------------|------------|
| Self-rerank@10            | None                   | 0.371      | 0.390      |
| DPO                       | Binary preference      | 0.365      | 0.382      |
| Verifier-guided expansion | Item filter            | 0.410      | 0.428      |
| Set-level GRPO (matched)  | Response reward        | 0.425      | 0.449      |
| Full: expansion + GRPO    | Filter+response reward | 0.452      | 0.485      |

Table 4: Ablation study on Verifier. DPO, expansion, and matched
set-level GRPO share the Stage-1 initialization, source papers,
generated-item and verifier-scoring volume. Self-rerank@10 is an
inference-only reference; the final row combines the two complementary
verifier-guided stages.

### 5.5 Judgment–Generation Scaling

We evaluate Qwen3 at 0.6, 1.7, 4, 8, 14, and 32B parameters. Each
selection item contains one future-pursued limitation and seven
counterfactual negatives matched for category, length, specificity,
lexical style, and source relevance; chance is 12.5%. An ambiguity
screen removes 91 of 1,512 sets, and 97% of 200 audited retained sets
have a unique correct answer. A candidate-only classifier that does not
see the source paper scores $`0.138\,[0.121,0.156]`$, with an interval
containing chance.

Selection accuracy rises from 0.148 to 0.368 across the Qwen3 family
(Spearman $`\rho=0.94`$; OLS slope $`+0.130`$ per decade, 95% CI
$`[0.076,0.185]`$). Generation on the same papers shows no consistent
positive trend: GRec@3_(B) ranges from 0.176 to 0.213, with slope
$`-0.005`$ per decade and 95% CI $`[-0.038,0.028]`$. Earlier-release
systems show the same qualitative separation, with selection accuracies
of 0.412–0.512 and generation scores of 0.242–0.271
(Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.5 Judgment–Generation Scaling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")).
Scale improves recognition of the future-pursued limitation far more
reliably than it improves open-ended generation.

### 5.6 Human Evaluation and Downstream Validation

#### Human Study.

Three NLP PhD annotators provide position-randomized pairwise judgments
with a tie option, together with binary actionability and
source-faithfulness ratings. Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ ([Fleiss

#### Does a better limitation produce a better idea?

We test whether improving the supplied limitation improves downstream
research ideation. For each paper, the same fixed Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
model ([Grattafiori et al. 2024](#bib.bib39)) generates one research
idea under four conditions: from the paper alone, or from the paper
paired with a zero-shot, ActLim-8B, or gold forward-citation limitation.
The proposer, prompt, decoding settings, and paper context are identical
across conditions, so the supplied limitation is the only varying input.
We then evaluate whether each generated idea aligns with research
directions later pursued by citing papers. This alignment score,
RetAlign, is averaged over the same 200-paper subset for every
condition.

A zero-shot limitation raises RetAlign from 0.302 to 0.348, while an
ActLim-8B limitation raises it to 0.480
(Table [6](#S5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Does a better limitation produce a better idea? ‣ 5.6 Human Evaluation and Downstream Validation ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")).
Supplying a gold forward-citation limitation gives an oracle reference
of 0.556. ActLim-8B therefore recovers 70.1% of the observed gap between
direct ideation and the gold-limitation oracle:
$`(0.480-0.302)/(0.556-0.302)`$. The oracle is an empirical reference
for this fixed proposer rather than a deployable condition. Human
ratings show significant gains in relevance and specificity.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we aim to optimize current ideation system by exploring
supervision signals derived from future-citation publications. We first
observe a judgment–generation asymmetry: scaling up language models
consistently improves limitation identification, while open-ended
limitation generation benefits much less. Forward citations naturally
bridge this gap by linking a source paper, a specific limitation, and
subsequent work that attempts to address it, providing reliable
supervision for training. Based on this insight, we construct
ActLim-Bench, an auditable benchmark that captures these
citation-grounded limitation links, and propose a three-stage training
framework that effectively leverages this supervision. Extensive
experiments show that our approach outperforms frontier models and
competitive baselines, generalizes across domains, and improves
downstream research ideation. While ActLim-Bench captures only
limitations that are later pursued by follow-up work, it provides a
practical and scalable bridge between understanding prior research and
proposing future directions.

## Appendix A Benchmark Construction

### A.1 Corpus and temporal split

Source papers are drawn from ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, EACL, COLING, and TACL
between 2017 and 2025. Train and Dev contain source papers published
2017–2023; the temporally held-out Test split contains source papers
published 2024–2025. For every source–citing pair $`(P,Q)`$, the
recorded publication date of $`Q`$ is strictly later than that of $`P`$.
A paper’s date is the earliest public appearance of its content: the
arXiv v1 timestamp where one exists, otherwise the venue publication
date. When arXiv and camera-ready versions of the same work both appear,
the camera-ready metadata is retained and the two are treated as one
canonical paper carrying the earlier date.

The Computer Vision transfer set is drawn from CVPR, ICCV, and ECCV
2024–2025 by the identical pipeline with no domain-specific
modification, and is used only for transfer evaluation.

### A.2 Extraction and verification

The extractor reads the abstract and introduction of $`Q`$ and returns
candidate limitations with citing-paper evidence spans $`e_{Q}`$;
source-paper evidence $`e_{P}`$ is then retrieved from the abstract,
introduction, and conclusion of $`P`$. The extractor only proposes
candidates, and acceptance is decided by the verifier. Extractor and
verifier both use DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp, accessed through the deepseek-chat
endpoint on 2025-11-04, at temperature $`0.0`$ and top-$`p`$ $`1.0`$.
Construction calls use a 1,024-token cap; parsing failures are retried
up to three times at temperature $`0.3`$, and pairs that remain
unparseable are discarded (1,204 pairs, 0.45% of those entering
extraction).

A candidate $`\hat{\ell}`$ is retained only when
$`A(P,Q,\hat{\ell})\wedge C(P,\hat{\ell})\wedge S(P,\hat{\ell})\wedge R(Q,\hat{\ell})`$:

- •
  Attribution $`A`$: $`Q`$ attributes the shortcoming to $`P`$, or to a
  named method family that explicitly includes $`P`$. Family attribution
  counts only when the class is named, $`P`$ is cited within $`Q`$ as an
  instance of it, and the shortcoming holds of $`P`$ under $`C`$.
- •
  Consistency $`C`$: the shortcoming agrees with the method,
  assumptions, evidence, and stated scope of $`P`$. A candidate that
  misstates $`P`$, or criticizes $`P`$ for something $`P`$ excludes from
  scope, fails $`C`$ even when $`Q`$ asserts it.
- •
  Specificity $`S`$: a concrete component, mechanism, assumption, data
  condition, or evaluation constraint, rather than a generic call for
  more experiments, datasets, or baselines.
- •
  Response $`R`$: the main contribution of $`Q`$ attempts to address the
  shortcoming. A contribution counts as main when it appears in the
  abstract or in the enumerated contribution list of $`Q`$; a
  shortcoming mentioned in passing, or handled only in a secondary
  ablation, fails $`R`$.

Each accepted record keeps $`(P,\hat{\ell},e_{P},e_{Q},Q)`$. No soft
threshold is applied at construction time.

### B.2 Graded match rubric

$`V`$ assigns $`m(y,\ell)\in\{0,0.2,0.6,1.0\}`$: none, different
shortcomings; category, the broad type matches but not the affected
component; facet, type, component, and aspect all match; specific, the
concrete shortcoming later pursued, at the granularity a follow-up would
act on. For a target concerning quadratic attention cost at long
context, “no ablation of the tokenizer” scores *none*; “the model is
computationally expensive” scores *category*; “the attention module
scales poorly with sequence length” scores *facet*; and “full
self-attention makes cost quadratic in context length, preventing use
beyond 4k tokens” scores *specific*.

### B.3 Metric computation

For predictions $`\mathbf{Y}_{P}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})`$ and observed
targets $`\mathcal{G}_{P}`$, let $`\mathcal{M}`$ range over one-to-one
matchings and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})=\max_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{(y,\ell)\in\mathcal{M}}m_{V}(y,\ell),
``` |  |

solved exactly by the Hungarian algorithm on the
$`3\times|\mathcal{G}_{P}|`$ score matrix. Then
$`\mathrm{GRec@3}=W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})/|\mathcal{G}_{P}|`$ and
$`\mathrm{GMatch@3}=W_{V}(P,\mathbf{Y}_{P})/\min(3,|\mathcal{G}_{P}|)`$.
SpecRec@3 repeats the maximization with binary weights
$`\mathbb{I}[m_{V}(y,\ell)=1]`$ rather than reusing the graded matching.
All are macro-averaged over papers.

#### Worked example.

With $`K=3`$ predictions and $`M=4`$ targets matched at the specific,
facet, and category levels, $`W_{V}=1.0+0.6+0.2=1.8`$, giving
$`\mathrm{GRec@3}=1.8/4=0.45`$, $`\mathrm{GMatch@3}=1.8/3=0.60`$, and
$`\mathrm{SpecRec@3}=1/4=0.25`$, since only one pair is a specific
match. This is the configuration illustrated in the main paper.

#### Edge cases.

Fewer than three parseable items: missing slots score 0 and the
denominator is unchanged. More than three: only the first three are
scored. Score-matrix ties are broken by target index, making the
matching deterministic. Normalizing GRec@3 by the full target set means
the metric is bounded above when $`|\mathcal{G}_{P}|>3`$; GMatch@3
removes that bound and is used in the target-count analysis.

#### Selection items.

Each source paper contributes one selection item. When a paper has more
than one observed target, the positive is the target supported by the
largest number of citing papers, ties broken by earliest citing-paper
date. Each item pairs that positive with seven counterfactual negatives,
generated by the same DeepSeek model used for construction, under an
edit instruction that changes the affected component, the mechanism, or
the evaluation condition while preserving surface form, then matched to
the positive on category, length within $`\pm 3`$ tokens, specificity
level, lexical style, and source relevance. 4,118 generated negatives
(27.9%) were discarded for failing these constraints. An ambiguity
screen removes 91 of 1,512 candidate sets in which two or more
candidates were accepted as facet-or-better matches to the same target,
leaving 1,421; in a human audit of 200 retained sets, 194 (97%) have a
unique correct answer. A candidate-only logistic classifier over
BAAI/bge-m3 embeddings, which never sees the source paper and is trained
and evaluated by five-fold cross-validation over the 1,421 retained sets
with accuracy pooled across held-out folds, scores $`0.138`$
$`[0.121,0.156]`$, an interval containing chance, so the negatives carry
no exploitable surface signal.

### E.3 What post-training changes

Three differences recur across the audited outputs and account for the
gap in
Table [24](#A5.T24 "Table 24 ‣ E.2 Paired comparisons across limitation categories ‣ Appendix E Case Studies ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation").

#### Grounding in a named component.

Post-trained outputs attach the criticism to an operation, an
assumption, or an evaluation condition that appears in the source text:
column-wise normalization, per-layer input–output similarity,
token-independent scoring. Zero-shot outputs more often describe a
property of the system as a whole. This distinction separates
CATEGORY 0.2 from FACET 0.6 under the rubric of
Section [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Graded match rubric ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"),
and it is the level at which a follow-up can begin.

#### Carrying the consequence.

The step from FACET 0.6 to SPECIFIC 1.0 is a statement of what the
mechanism prevents: code that no longer parses, robust layers pruned as
redundant, long-range context left unused. Verified demonstrations
supply the component and verifier-guided expansion sharpens the
mechanism, but the consequence appears most consistently after set-level
optimization, which is consistent with the stage-wise SpecRec@3 pattern
reported in the main paper.

#### Spending the three slots differently.

Zero-shot systems frequently return three variations on the same
concern, typically evaluation breadth; the NONE 0.0 output in
Table [24](#A5.T24 "Table 24 ‣ E.2 Paired comparisons across limitation categories ‣ Appendix E Case Studies ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")
is one such case, and its two companions in the same response repeat it.
The redundancy term in the set-level reward penalizes this directly, and
the mean within-response similarity of ActLim-8B is correspondingly
lower. The three outputs therefore cover distinct shortcomings rather
than restating one, which is what the coverage term rewards and what the
Cover component of the reward ablation in
Section [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Reward-component ablation ‣ D.2 Supervision-source controls ‣ Appendix D Additional Results ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation")
isolates.

## Appendix F Human and Downstream Evaluation

### F.1 Limitation human study

binary actionability and source-faithfulness ratings. Median time per

ratings are averaged within each paper before the paired Wilcoxon test.

#### Rubrics.

*Actionable*: “Could a researcher who has read only this paper turn this
statement into a concrete next experiment or system change, without
first having to decide what the statement refers to?” *Faithful*: “Is
this statement true of the paper as written, given its stated method,
assumptions, and scope?” *Pairwise*: “Which would be more useful as the
starting point for follow-up work? Prefer the one that is both faithful
and actionable; if one is faithful but generic and the other specific
but inaccurate, select a tie.”

### G.2 Construction verification

> Judge the candidate limitation on four criteria, each strictly yes or

> supporting span from P.
>
> P sections: {p_text} \n Q sections: {q_text} \n Candidate:

### G.3 Graded matching

> Given the source paper and a pair (prediction, target), output one of:
> NONE if they concern different shortcomings; CATEGORY if the broad
> type matches but the affected component does not; FACET if the type,
> the affected component, and the aspect all match; SPECIFIC if the
> prediction identifies the same concrete shortcoming at the granularity
> a follow-up would act on. Output the label only.
>
> Source: {p_text} \n Prediction: {y} \n Target: {target}

### G.4 Generation instruction

> Read the paper below and identify exactly {N} limitations that could
> motivate concrete follow-up work. Each must be supported by the paper
> itself and must name a specific component, mechanism, assumption, data
> condition, or evaluation constraint. Do not ask for more experiments
> or better results in general. Output a numbered list of {N} single
> sentences and nothing else.
>
> {p_abstract}\n{p_intro}\n{p_conclusion}

### G.5 Selection-item negative generation

> Rewrite the limitation below so that it remains fluent, equally
> specific, and of similar length, but refers to a DIFFERENT {component
> \| mechanism \| evaluation condition} so that it is no longer true of
> the source paper. Preserve the sentence structure and register. Output
> one sentence.

## Appendix H Ethics, Licensing, and Release

#### Provenance and licensing.

Source and citing papers are drawn from arXiv, the ACL Anthology, and
the open proceedings of CVPR, ICCV, and ECCV. For sources whose licenses
permit redistribution, the release contains evidence spans, canonical
limitations, and paper identifiers. For non-redistributable sources, it
provides the DOI, section identifier, and character offsets rather than
the underlying text. No OpenReview content is included in ActLim-Bench;
the reviewer-written supervision arm of the supervision-source
comparison uses public OpenReview text under its terms of use and is
released as identifiers and offsets only.

#### Personal data.

The corpus consists of published scientific papers. Author names and
affiliations are retained as published, since they are part of the
public bibliographic record. No annotator identifiers are released.

#### Annotator welfare.

Compensation, recruitment, and review status are in
Section [F.1](#A6.SS1 "F.1 Limitation human study ‣ Appendix F Human and Downstream Evaluation ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation").
Annotators could decline any item and were informed of the purpose of
the study.

#### Intended use and scope.

ActLim-Bench records limitations that later work in fact pursued. It is
a proxy for actionability rather than a measure of scientific value: a
limitation may go unpursued because of timing, resources, or shifting
research priorities, and a pursued direction is not necessarily the most
important shortcoming of a paper. The dataset supports research on
scientific ideation and should not be used to evaluate individual
papers, authors, or venues.

#### Evaluator design.

Scoring is anchored at three points. The primary metric comes from an
evaluator in a different model family that takes no part in
construction, filtering, reward, or model selection. The targets are
validated by the 400-triple human audit of
Section [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Triple-level audit ‣ Appendix A Benchmark Construction ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"),
and the graded scale by the validation in
Section [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Verifier validation ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation").
Blind human preference judgments
(Section [F.1](#A6.SS1 "F.1 Limitation human study ‣ Appendix F Human and Downstream Evaluation ‣ Where Research Ideation Begins: Forward-Citation Supervision for Actionable Limitation Generation"))
evaluate the resulting outputs with no model evaluator in the loop.
````
