###### Abstract

Research ideas are always proposed under resource constraints: a plan
must fit the available compute, API credit, and time. Yet evaluations of
research-ideation agents mostly score unconstrained novelty or overlap
with a published paper, and their LLM judges are rarely checked against
peer review. We introduce AlphaIdeaBench, a benchmark of 100 ideation
tasks (50 workshop calls for papers and 50 open problems stated in
surveys), each paired with an explicit resource budget and a shared
proposal schema. An LLM judge scores each proposal on 12 dimensions
covering validity, novelty, and feasibility. To validate the judge, we
compare its scores on four novelty axes (problem, method, scenario, and
insight) with scores derived from reviewer comments on 600 papers from
ICLR 2026, NeurIPS 2025, and ICML 2025. For problem and method novelty,
Gemini 3.7 Flash is within one point of the reviewer-derived score on
94–97% of papers; agreement on scenario novelty is relatively lower. We
evaluate eight published harnesses on a shared backbone and, within one
harness, eight backbone LLMs. CoI-Agent obtains the highest overall
score, narrowly ahead of SciMON, which leads on method and insight
novelty. All harnesses score low on scenario novelty and rarely change
the problem setting. The backbone largely determines which open problem
a proposal targets, whereas the harness determines whether a proposal
addresses one problem or combines several. Weaker backbones such as
Llama 4 Scout fail to carry out that harness’s strategy and score low on
budget feasibility. Finally, when only the budget is varied on three
topics, a larger budget changes the experimental setting on
compute-intensive topics but only adds tests on an inexpensive one. We
release the tasks, rubrics, and scores to foster standardized,
reproducible benchmarking of future research-ideation agents under
constraints.

## 1 Introduction

Automating research is no longer only a writing aid. Agents now read a
literature, name an open question, and draft the experiment that later
code and papers will carry out ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Yamada et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Baek et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Su et al.,
2025](#bib.bib5)). That first write is the expensive step. A plan that
is fluent but empty still consumes GPU time, API credit, calendar, and
reviewer attention, and systems that continue into execution or
manuscript drafting cannot repair a weak question ([Schmidgall et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)).If automated research
is going to be used, the idea itself has to be something a group could
realistically defend and actually run.

Producing that idea is harder than producing text that looks like a
paper. A usable proposal has to stay inside the given literature, be
novel in the question, the method, or the setting in which the method is
run, and fit a budget the authors can actually spend. These properties
do not move together. Overlap with one published paper punishes a
legitimate departure from that paper, and a single overall score hides
which of them failed. Current comparisons do not separate them. Each
harness, the scaffold of retrieval, critique, and rewrite steps wrapped
around a language model, is usually shown with its own prompt and its
own judge, and with no shared statement of how much compute or calendar
the author may spend ([Si et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)). Studies that ask humans whether a model idea matches
an expert idea do not leave a reusable checklist. Language-model judges
scale, but without a check against peer review they are easy to
overtrust ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13)). Workshop suites such as MLR-Bench package a real
call for papers, then move on to later experimental and writing stages
([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)). What is still missing is a benchmark
of the written idea: one context, one resource envelope, one schema, and
a novelty judge checked against reviewer text from premier machine
learning conferences.

AlphaIdeaBench is that testbed. Every task file gives the agent the same
scholarly context, the same resource envelope, and the same JSON schema
(title, question, hypothesis, method, experiments, baselines, success
criteria, related work, limitations, and a resource plan). We score the
completed proposal on 12 dimensions covering validity, novelty, and
feasibility, and we release the checklists. Novelty is split into
problem, method, scenario, and insight. Before those axes are used on
agent writes, we map them from reviewer comments on 600 papers at ICLR
2026, NeurIPS 2025, and ICML 2025 and compare the judge with that
mapping. The tasks follow the two routes a human researcher actually
uses: 50 workshop calls and 50 open problems taken from surveys. In the
main study the envelope is an academic lab budget: four A100 GPUs, 200
GPU hours, \$200 of API credit, eight weeks, and 40 annotation hours. On
the 600 papers, problem and method novelty from the Gemini 3.7 Flash
judge sit within one point of the reviewer-mapped scores on 94% to 97%
of items. On the 100 tasks, eight harnesses that share this strong
backbone fill validity and feasibility, so their rank is mostly a
novelty rank. That ceiling depends on the model: under the same harness,
a weak backbone loses validity and feasibility as well. CoI-Agent is
best overall (4.31) because it pairs novelty with near-ceiling
testability. Scenario novelty stays low for every harness, at most 1.87.
Holding the topics fixed, the pretraining prior of the model decides
which listed challenge looks worth writing, while the harness decides
whether to dig one bottleneck or to fuse several. Stretching the same
envelope from a student budget to a lab budget, extra compute changes
the experiment on costly topics, moving from simulation toward the real
system, and is spent as more tests of the same plan when the topic is
already cheap to run. Our contributions are:

- •
  A public 12-dimension scoring rubric, and released fine-grained
  novelty scores (problem, method, scenario, and insight) mapped from
  reviewer comments on 600 ICLR, NeurIPS, and ICML papers.
- •
  A 100-task suite that abstracts two human routes to an idea, reading a
  survey and answering a workshop CFP, with 50 tasks in each route and
  an explicit resource cap.
- •
  A factorial comparison that separates the harness from the ideation
  model: eight published harnesses on one backbone, and eight backbones
  under one harness.
- •
  An envelope study that varies only the budget, on topics whose
  experiments differ in cost, and asks how the proposed idea changes in
  ambition and experimental scope.

## 2 Related work

Autonomous Scientific Agents. End-to-end systems try to run more than
ideation. AI-Scientist-V1 generates hypotheses, runs experiments from
code templates, and drafts a paper ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)).
AI-Scientist-V2 drops those templates and uses agentic tree search over
experiments ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)). Agent Laboratory takes a
human-provided idea and moves it through literature review,
experimentation, and report writing with PhD, postdoc, and engineer
roles ([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). AI-Researcher orchestrates
literature review, hypothesis generation, implementation, and manuscript
writing; Scientist-Bench is the evaluation suite introduced in that
paper, not the agent itself ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). Other
lines target the ideation step. ResearchAgent retrieves related papers
and knowledge entities, then iteratively refines the problem, method,
and experiment design with LLM reviewers ([Baek et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)). CoI-Agent organizes retrieved papers into a
progressive idea chain rather than a bag of citations, then writes an
idea and an experiment plan ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)). SciMON
retrieves inspirations from prior work and rewrites the suggestion until
a novelty comparison with retrieved papers is cleared ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)). VirSci (Virtual Scientists) forms a team from author
profiles and runs inter- and intra-team discussions to produce an idea
([Su et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). These systems are usually demonstrated
with their own prompts and judges, and several originally continue into
code execution or manuscript writing. AlphaIdeaBench holds the topic,
resource envelope, output schema, and judge fixed, and scores only the
final written research proposal.

Benchmarks for Scientific Ideation. Scoring a research idea is not one
number: novelty, soundness, and whether the plan can be run do not move
together. Early automatic scores use overlap with a gold paper and
punish legitimate departures. Human studies ask whether models can match
expert ideas ([Si et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)), but leave no reusable
checklist. VBench makes the same point for video, splitting one quality
score into separate dimensions ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)); we
take that split as the right grain for ideation. NovBench checks LLM
novelty assessments against reviewer text on published papers ([Wu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). We likewise check four novelty axes against
reviewer comments, rather than treating one overall score as a novelty
label. MLR-Bench packages workshop CFPs and then scores later
experiments and writing ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)). We stop at the
proposal and score both its novelty and feasibility under one shared,
explicit academic resource envelope.

### 3.1 Benchmark construction

#### Task Collection.

The suite has 100 tasks in two tracks of 50, matching the two ways a
human researcher starts a project. The workshop track uses official
calls hosted at NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR from 2023 to 2025. Each item
keeps the workshop narrative, the listed research questions, and the
original topic scope, so the question is still partly open. The survey
track uses 50 surveys. We take the open problems the authors themselves
state, so the agent is asked for an intervention on a named bottleneck
rather than for an open frontier. Every file carries the same academic
envelope and the same required output, both defined next, so no harness
gets a friendlier prompt.

The 100 tasks cover nine fields
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix A Meta evaluation and domain tables ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")):
LLMs and reasoning (22), AI for science (13), efficiency and systems
(13), multimodal and vision (12), trustworthy ML and safety (12),
robotics and reinforcement learning (8), interpretability and theory
(8), graphs and geometry (6), and continual learning (6).

and
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Experimental Evaluation ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")(c)

Required Output. A harness may retrieve, debate, or rewrite internally.
It must return one JSON object. The schema requires a title, question,
hypothesis, method, experiments, baselines, success criteria, related
work, limitations, and a resource plan. These fields are what the judge
reads. A missing baseline, a method that does not attack the stated
question, or a resource line that ignores the envelope is then a
property of the proposal. We do not score the internal trace. Role play
and rewrite loops affect the result only through the JSON they emit,
which is what makes harnesses with different control flow comparable
across diverse agent architectures and search strategies.

Resource Envelope. Feasibility is scored against a stated budget, not
against an implicit guess that a lab could probably run the plan. Agents
receive
$`\mathcal{B}=\langle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{gpu}},\;\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{api}},\;\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{cal}},\;\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{human}}\rangle.`$
In the 100-task study every topic uses the same academic setting: four
A100 40GB GPUs, 200 GPU hours, \$200 of API credit, eight calendar
weeks, and 40 annotation hours. The judge compares the proposed compute
and labor plan with this $`\mathcal{B}`$, and the agent sees the same
numbers.
Section [4](#S4 "4 Experimental Evaluation ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")
later keeps the topic fixed and replaces the whole envelope. The student
setting is one A100, 20 GPU hours, \$20 of API credit, two weeks, and 8
annotation hours. The lab setting is eight A100 GPUs, 2000 GPU hours,
\$2000 of API credit, 16 weeks, and 80 annotation hours. A plan that
becomes more ambitious only by ignoring the cap fails budget
feasibility.

### 3.2 Evaluation System

Scoring Dimensions. The rubric is public, and the scoring rules were
written by human experts. Each of the twelve checks is scored from 1 to
5, and
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Scoring rubrics and 12 way scores ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")
releases the descriptor and the questions the judge must answer. A
single overall score hides which part of the idea failed, and overlap
with one published paper punishes a legitimate departure without saying
whether the plan can be run. The twelve checks fall into three groups
that do not substitute for one another: a proposal can be well grounded
and still be a dataset swap, or novel and still exceed the envelope.

- •
  Validity. Context grounding penalizes hallucinated literature and
  phantom baselines. Problem formulation asks whether the topic is
  turned into a bounded, answerable question rather than a restatement
  of the prompt. Motivation and method consistency checks that the
  proposed method attacks the stated bottleneck. Testability requires
  observable outcomes, controls, and baselines that could falsify the
  stated claim rather than a success criterion that cannot fail once the
  plan is run. Together, these four checks establish empirical
  soundness: an idea must be anchored in real prior art and open to
  experimental falsification before it is judged on novelty.
- •
  Novelty. Problem novelty concerns the question or failure mode. Method
  novelty concerns a non-trivial change to the algorithm or the
  objective, not a rename of a known gadget. Scenario novelty concerns a
  new domain, distribution, or operating constraint that forces a
  redesign rather than a dataset swap. Insight novelty concerns a
  mechanism, causal factor, or boundary condition that changes how the
  problem is understood. A high method score and a high scenario score
  are different claims. These four axes are the ones Alignment
  Verification checks against reviewer text.
- •
  Feasibility. Technical feasibility rejects oracle information and
  circular dependencies, such as a method that needs the label it claims
  to predict. Data feasibility checks access, licenses, and labels.
  Budget feasibility compares the full experimental program, including
  baselines and ablations, with the envelope. The judge can use internet
  tools to look up API and GPU prices rather than trusting the agent’s
  own arithmetic. Verification feasibility asks whether the metrics and
  the sample size can isolate the claimed effect rather than a metric
  that moves with every confound in the data.

Together, these twelve checks decouple scientific merit into orthogonal
axes: a proposal cannot compensate for hallucinated grounding with
algorithmic novelty, nor can an ambitious formulation bypass physical
resource bounds. Evaluating them independently prevents aggregate metric
compression and isolates precisely where automated ideation succeeds or
fails across distinct dimensions of scientific proposal quality.

Judge. The production judge is Gemini 3.7 Flash. It reads the JSON and
the task file, and applies the 1 to 5 descriptors in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Scoring rubrics and 12 way scores ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment").
It does not see reviewer text, author identities, or accept decisions.
Validity and feasibility have no human reference in this study: they are
checks against the declared experiment and against $`\mathcal{B}`$. A
second judge, GPT-5 mini, does not produce the main scores. It
independently re-scores the same finished writes, which allows us to
verify whether the relative agent ranking remains stable under an
alternative judge rather than depending on a single scoring model
(Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Ranking stability under a second judge ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")).

Alignment Verification. Alignment Verification, the block in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 The AlphaIdeaBench benchmark ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment"),
checks the four novelty axes against real reviewer comments downloaded
for these papers. Reviewers did not fill in a 1 to 5 score for problem,
method, scenario, or insight. Before those axes are used on agent
writes, Gemini 3.7 Flash reads those comments and maps each discussed
facet to a ground-truth score from 1 to 5, following NovBench ([Wu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). The set is 600 papers, 200 each from ICLR 2026,
NeurIPS 2025, and ICML 2025, stratified to each venue’s official
mean-score mix
(Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix A Meta evaluation and domain tables ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")).
Facets that reviewers did not discuss are left missing, so the number of
labeled items differs by axis. The comparison score is a separate pass:
that judge sees the title and abstract only, not the reviews, so the
experimental section is invisible both to the ground truth and to the
judge.

Metrics. Let $`s_{t,d}\in\{1,\ldots,5\}`$ be the judge score of proposal
$`t`$ on dimension $`d\in\{1,\ldots,12\}`$. With $`T`$ proposals for a
harness ($`T{=}100`$ in the main tables),

|     |                                       |                                                                 |                              |                                                               |                                   |                                                                     |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Overall}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{12}\sum\nolimits_{d=1}^{12}s_{t,d},`$ | $`\displaystyle\bar{s}_{d}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}s_{t,d},`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Overall}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{12}\sum\nolimits_{d=1}^{12}\bar{s}_{d}.`$ |     | (1) |

Overall is an unweighted mean. A gain on one novelty axis is not
rescaled by the eight validity and feasibility scores, but neither can a
single novelty spike hide a plan that fails testability. We report the
twelve means as well as Overall, because the mean alone compresses when
validity and feasibility sit near the ceiling. For one novelty axis, let
$`\hat{y}_{i}`$ and $`y_{i}`$ be the judge score and the reviewer-mapped
score on item $`i=1,\ldots,N`$, and let $`p,q`$ be the corresponding
histograms on $`\{1,\ldots,5\}`$.

DistAcc is taken from NovBench ([Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). It is
one minus half the L1 distance between the two score histograms, so it
checks whether the judge and the reviewers use the scale in the same
way. Within $`\pm 1`$ is the fraction of items on which the two scores
differ by at most one point, which is the agreement we treat as usable
on a five-point rubric. Exact Acc is the fraction of ties. MAD is
$`\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}|`$. Judge mean J$`\mu`$
and human mean H$`\mu`$ are the two averages, and Gap is J$`\mu`$ minus
H$`\mu`$. A negative Gap means the judge is stricter than the mapped
reviews. DistAcc, Within $`\pm 1`$, and Exact Acc are reported as
percentages. On the 50 surveys we also record which listed open problem
the proposal actually writes. A hit is an explicit “challenge $`k`$” or
“C$`k`$” tag, otherwise keyword overlap with a listed problem.
Multi-challenge means more than one hit:
$`\mathrm{Multi}=\frac{1}{50}\sum_{t=1}^{50}\mathbf{1}[n_{t}>1]`$, with
$`n_{t}`$ the number of hits on survey $`t`$. This is a property of the
text, not a thirteenth judge score. It is how
Section [4](#S4 "4 Experimental Evaluation ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")
separates a model prior, which picks the challenge, from a harness
policy, which decides whether to dig one bottleneck or to fuse several.

Budget. The 100-task study holds $`\mathcal{B}`$ at the academic
setting. To test whether a harness writes a different plan when the
numbers change, we keep CoI-Agent and the Gemini 3.7 Flash judge and
replace the whole budget on three surveys that differ in cost: tool use,
vision-language-action, and mechanistic interpretability. The agent and
the judge see the same $`\mathcal{B}`$, making the resource constraint
explicit to both sides.

The harness does write different plans. Every cell stays inside its own
cap
(Figure [7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4 Experimental Evaluation ‣ AlphaIdeaBench: Benchmarking Automated Research Idea Generation under Feasibility Constraints with Peer Review Alignment")(c)),
and the nine writes are not one template with a rescaled GPU line. Extra
budget buys a more novel idea only on compute-heavy topics. On
vision-language-action the operating world moves from a LIBERO tabletop
in simulation, to contact and impedance, to simulation plus 240
real-robot trials once the lab budget is large enough. On
interpretability the trained object scales: frozen sparse autoencoders
with no training, then 2B dictionaries, then 8B and 9B chain-of-thought
circuits. On a cheap and already instrumented topic the extra money does
not change the research world. Tool use stays in prompt injection plus
public benchmarks. The three writes differ in how many tests they run.
At the lab cap, tool use claims only 740 of 2000 GPU hours, while
vision-language-action and interpretability spend 1400 and 1850. A
larger budget alters the experimental world only when compute is the
binding constraint, and otherwise accumulates as additional tests of the
same baseline setting rather than forcing novelty where resources are
already sufficient.

## 5 Limitations and Conclusion

Limitations. The 100 tasks are drawn from machine learning, so findings
on challenge crowding and scenario ceilings may behave differently in
wet lab fields where experiments cannot be simulated. Moreover, while
our comparison separates backbone priors from search policies across
eight harnesses, it is not an exhaustive model zoo, and newer models may
alter the ranking as model capabilities continue to evolve.

Conclusion. AlphaIdeaBench scores structured research proposals under a
shared academic budget and a 12-dimension rubric, and it checks the
novelty axes against reviewer text from 600 ML conference papers. We
evaluated eight types of harnesses and several ideation models within
this framework; CoI-Agent performed relatively well across the various
metrics. Subsequently, during the process of decoupling the LLM from the
harness, we found the following: the backbone largely determines which
listed challenge looks worth writing, and the harness decides whether to
dig one bottleneck or to fuse several. However, less capable LLMs cannot
follow the harness instructions or the stated budget well. Extra budget
buys a more ambitious write on compute-heavy topics and is absorbed as
extra tests on cheap ones rather than opening a new research world on
those topics. With a strong backbone, validity and feasibility sit near
the ceiling, so harness rank is mostly about novelty. Scenario novelty
stays low for every system we ran. We release the tasks, the rubric, and
the reviewer-mapped scores so later agents can be compared under the
same envelope and the same judge. Later work can keep this evaluation
protocol fixed and change only the agent under test as automated
research systems continue to improve.

## Appendix C Scoring rubrics and 12 way scores

The Gemini 3.7 Flash judge scores one dimension at a time on an integer
scale from 1 to 5 and must answer every checklist item as yes, no,
partial, or n/a. A yes on a positive item supports a higher score; a yes
on a negative item supports a lower score. Resource shortfalls are
scored only under budget feasibility; they are not re-penalized in
verification feasibility or research readiness. On novelty axes, a solid
conference paper baseline is typically 3; 4 requires a clearly
non-obvious new mechanism or formulation; 2 is routine module
combination or minor adaptation. The descriptors below are the
production rubrics.

### C.1 Validity

#### Context grounding.

Does the idea correctly understand and use the given research
background, prior papers, and research direction?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                             |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Largely detached from the given context, or contains serious factual errors; the problem is unrelated to the given direction           |
| 2   | Superficially related to the context, but clearly misreads prior work, research goals, or limitations                                  |
| 3   | Broadly understands the context, but only uses the general theme and does not use the key papers or research gaps accurately           |
| 4   | Correctly understands prior work and identifies a research gap consistent with the context                                             |
| 5   | Deeply and accurately synthesises the papers, limitations, and background in the context; the idea follows naturally from that context |

Positive checks. Is the idea relevant to the given research direction.
Are existing methods described accurately. Are the limitations of prior
work identified correctly. Is key information from the context actually
used. Negative checks. Is a problem already solved by prior work
mistaken for an open problem. Are there factual errors about the papers.
Does it introduce a key assumption absent from the context without
explaining where it comes from. Are there fabricated papers, fabricated
results, or incorrect citations.

#### Problem formulation.

Does the idea turn a broad direction into a clear, specific,
well-bounded research problem?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | No clear research problem; only a topic, a slogan, or a wish                                                                                                                |
| 2   | The general direction is visible, but the subject, conditions, goal, and boundaries are all vague                                                                           |
| 3   | The problem is broadly understandable, but important qualifying conditions or key variables are still missing                                                               |
| 4   | Problem, subject, applicable conditions, and goal are reasonably clear, and the scope is roughly manageable                                                                 |
| 5   | The research problem is defined precisely, with clear key variables, boundaries, assumptions, and goals; it could almost be turned directly into a formal research question |

Positive checks. Is the object of study stated. Is the phenomenon or
failure under study stated. Are the study conditions stated. Are the key
variables stated. Is the research goal stated. Is there an explicit
research question or hypothesis. Negative checks. Is it merely vague
phrasing such as “improve performance”, “enhance capability”, or “solve
robustness”. Are several unrelated problems mixed into one idea. Is the
scope of the idea too large.

#### Motivation and method consistency.

Does the proposed method actually address the research gap identified in
the motivation?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                  |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Method and motivation are essentially unrelated; there is no visible reason the method would solve the problem                              |
| 2   | Method relates to the motivation only superficially and cannot address the core research gap                                                |
| 3   | Method broadly responds to the motivation, but a key mechanism or logical link in between is missing                                        |
| 4   | Method explicitly targets the core problem in the motivation, with a plausible mechanism of action                                          |
| 5   | Motivation, hypothesis, method, and experiments form a closed loop; the method addresses the core bottleneck rather than a peripheral issue |

Positive checks. Is the bottleneck identified by the motivation stated
explicitly. Does the method target that bottleneck directly. Can the
method test the key hypothesis. If the method succeeds, would the
original problem be alleviated. Would the planned experiments show that
the method really solves the problem in the motivation. Negative checks.
Is there a “motivation says A, method does B” mismatch. Is the method
merely a module related to the problem without actually solving it.

#### Testability.

Can the core scientific claim of this idea be clearly tested or
falsified?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                            |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | No clear way to verify it; the core claim is essentially untestable                                                                   |
| 2   | There is a rough experimental direction, but no clear variables, metrics, or decision criteria                                        |
| 3   | A preliminary test is possible, but strong baselines, controls, or clear falsification criteria are missing                           |
| 4   | Clear hypothesis, experimental setup, evaluation metrics, and comparison targets                                                      |
| 5   | Rigorous falsifiable hypothesis, controlled variables, control conditions, success criteria, and analysis of alternative explanations |

Positive checks. Is there an explicit hypothesis. Are there observable
variables. Is there a measurable outcome. Is there a baseline. Is there
a control condition. Is there an explicit success criterion. Could the
results support or overturn the core claim. Can competing explanations
be distinguished. Is “the model gets better” turned into a measurable
metric. Negative checks. Are there untestable strong claims such as
“truly understands” or “achieves general intelligence”.

### C.2 Novelty

#### Problem novelty.

Is the research problem, phenomenon, failure mode, hypothesis, or
research perspective new relative to prior work?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                               |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The problem has already been explicitly posed and largely solved in prior literature                                                     |
| 2   | Minor repackaging, cosmetic re-framing, or routine extension of an existing problem without posing a new fundamental question            |
| 3   | Meaningful new angle or specific failure mode within an existing problem domain, but closely following established research trajectories |
| 4   | Distinctly new, well-defined research problem, non-obvious failure mode, or mechanistic question that challenges existing assumptions    |
| 5   | Foundational new problem formulation or conceptual paradigm that defines an entirely new research area                                   |

Positive checks. Does it identify a genuinely new failure mode or
unexplored bottleneck. Does it identify a new contradiction or challenge
to existing assumptions. Does it pose a distinct, non-obvious research
question. Does it propose a new problem boundary or object of study. Is
there a substantive difference from closest prior work. Negative checks.
Has this problem already been studied or largely solved in existing
literature. Is it only a cosmetic re-framing or routine change of
dataset, model, or benchmark.

#### Method novelty.

Is the algorithm, model, training procedure, experimental method, or
theoretical tool used to solve the problem new?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                                         |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | The method essentially restates, directly adopts, or replicates existing methods with no substantive modification                                                  |
| 2   | Routine combination or minor adaptation: standard stitching of off-the-shelf modules, trivial backbone swap, or straightforward application of existing toolkits   |
| 3   | Solid incremental method: meaningful procedural or architectural modification tailored to the problem, but the core mechanism remains within established paradigms |
| 4   | Non-obvious, substantive new method design: introduces a distinct, well-formulated new algorithmic formulation, loss objective, mechanism, or theoretical tool     |
| 5   | Foundational or paradigm-shifting methodological breakthrough that establishes a fundamentally new computing, modeling, or learning principle                      |

Positive checks. Does the method introduce a non-obvious algorithmic
delta beyond standard module combinations. Does it change the core
solving mechanism or mathematical or algorithmic formulation. Is the new
method specifically and non-trivially designed for the bottleneck of
this idea. Negative checks. Does the method already appear in existing
papers or standard toolkits. Is it primarily a routine assembly or
stitching of standard existing components (for example standard models,
prompting, or off-the-shelf RL or loss). Is it only a backbone swap,
hyperparameter tuning, or dataset swap. Is it only the addition of a
common module without changing the core solving mechanism. If it is a
combination of methods, does that combination already have direct
precedent or is it a routine pairing (answer n/a if the method is not a
combination).

#### Scenario novelty.

Does the idea introduce a new domain, task, modality, data distribution,
deployment environment, or real-world constraint, and does that give
rise to a meaningful new research problem?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                  |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The scenario completely follows existing work, or is only renamed                                                                           |
| 2   | Only the dataset, application domain, or input format changes; the core research problem and technical challenges are essentially unchanged |
| 3   | Introduces a new application domain, task, or constraint, but standard methods can be transferred directly without fundamental adaptations  |
| 4   | The new scenario creates distinct, meaningful challenges that fundamentally require targeted method redesign rather than direct transfer    |
| 5   | The new scenario opens an entirely new research frontier with unprecedented evaluation, modeling, or real-world requirements                |

Positive checks. Is it a new domain, task, modality, or deployment
environment. Does the new scenario introduce unique challenges that
prevent standard direct transfer. Does the scenario necessitate targeted
algorithmic or evaluation redesign. Does the new scenario change the
underlying research problem. Negative checks. Is it just applying
existing methods directly to a new dataset or domain without unique
challenges.

#### Insight novelty.

Does the idea offer a new understanding of a phenomenon, failure mode,
method behaviour, or causal mechanism?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                  |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | No new explanation; only describes a phenomenon, repeats known conclusions, or provides cosmetic explanations                               |
| 2   | Offers a generic, high-level, or templated explanation that does not reveal concrete mechanisms                                             |
| 3   | Provides a specific empirical observation or preliminary mechanistic hypothesis, but remains close to existing understandings               |
| 4   | Proposes a clear, non-obvious new mechanism, boundary condition, or causal explanation supported by concrete evidence or falsifiable claims |
| 5   | Reveals a foundational, non-obvious new principle or causal mechanism that alters how the field understands the phenomenon                  |

Positive checks. Does it propose a new mechanistic or causal
explanation. Does it point to specific, verifiable causal factors. Does
it identify a new failure mechanism or boundary condition. Does it
disentangle factors previously conflated. Is it substantively different
from existing explanations in the literature. Can the mechanistic claim
be tested experimentally or theoretically. Negative checks. Is it merely
renaming an existing phenomenon or offering a generic explanation.

### C.3 Feasibility

#### Technical feasibility.

Can the technical plan of this idea be implemented?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                            |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Contains a fundamental technical contradiction, or depends on unobtainable information; essentially not implementable |
| 2   | Key technical components are missing; only a conceptual description, hard to implement                                |
| 3   | A simplified version or prototype is implementable, but the core plan still carries clear technical risk              |
| 4   | The technical path is clear, the required components mostly exist, and it can reasonably be implemented               |
| 5   | The implementation path is mature and complete, technical risk is low, and prototyping could start immediately        |

Positive checks. Do the required algorithms, models, and components
exist. Is the method pipeline complete. Are inputs and outputs clearly
defined. Are the training and inference loops closed. Can a prototype be
built. Negative checks. Does it depend on unobtainable information. Does
it require an oracle. Are there self-contradictory assumptions. Does it
require solving an unmentioned core technical challenge.

#### Data feasibility.

Can the data, labels, ground truth, and data access this idea needs be
obtained?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The required data does not exist, cannot be obtained, or there is no workable ground truth                     |
| 2   | Major obstacles to obtaining data; requires large-scale collection, expert annotation, or access approval      |
| 3   | Substitute data or a simplified version is possible, but data quality, scale, or representativeness is limited |
| 4   | The required data is largely obtainable, needing only routine cleaning, processing, or limited annotation      |
| 5   | Data is public, sufficient, of suitable quality, with reliable labels, and can directly support verification   |

Positive checks. Does the data exist. Is it publicly available. Is the
amount of data sufficient. Is the data quality sufficient. Is there
reliable ground truth. Can train, validation, and test splits be
constructed. Can the data really support the claim being verified.
Negative checks. Does new data need to be collected. Is expert
annotation required. Are there privacy, copyright, or ethical issues.
Could train-test leakage occur.

#### Budget feasibility.

Can this idea be completed under the compute, time, API, human-effort,
and funding constraints specified by the benchmark?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                                                       |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | Clearly exceeds the stated budget; no meaningful verification is possible as things stand                                                        |
| 2   | Key resources are severely insufficient; it can only remain conceptual                                                                           |
| 3   | A scaled-down proof-of-concept is possible, but full verification is not                                                                         |
| 4   | Reasonable experiments can be completed within the given compute, time, and funding                                                              |
| 5   | Resource needs are clearly below budget, the experimental path is clear, and there is ample headroom for baselines, ablations, and repeated runs |

Positive checks. Is GPU or CPU capacity sufficient. Is training time
sufficient. Is inference cost affordable. Is the human annotation cost
affordable. Is there enough time for data collection and processing. Is
there enough time for baselines, ablations, and repeated runs. Can a
meaningful verification be completed within budget, not just a working
demo. Negative checks. Does the number of API calls exceed the budget.
Are special devices or a deployment environment required.

#### Verification feasibility.

Under real conditions, can a sufficiently credible experimental or
theoretical verification be carried out?

| \#  | Descriptor                                                                                                         |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | Credible verification is essentially impossible; data, metrics, baselines, or observable outcomes are missing      |
| 2   | Only indirect, insufficient, or highly unreliable evidence can be obtained                                         |
| 3   | Preliminary verification is possible, but experiment scale, controls, or measurement capability are limited        |
| 4   | Clear, executable experimental procedure, baselines, metrics, and basic controls                                   |
| 5   | A rigorous, reproducible verification plan that can distinguish alternative explanations and support falsification |

Positive checks. Is there an executable experimental procedure. Is the
required data obtainable. Are baselines implemented. Are the evaluation
metrics appropriate. Can ablations be carried out. Can key variables be
controlled. Can experiments be repeated. Can alternative explanations be
ruled out. Is the resulting evidence strong enough to be credible rather
than merely suggestive. Could the results support or refute the core
claim. Whether resources fit the budget belongs to budget feasibility;
do not re-penalize the budget here.

#### Why the scores move.

Six families write a variant of “make the science agent falsify its own
hypothesis”. The labels differ (severe testing, minimax skeptics,
conformal checks, information gain), but the operating setting is still
an LLM science agent with a debate or verifier loop, so scenario novelty
stays at 1 to 3. SciMON is the only system at 4 on all four novelty
axes: CausalPopper forces an identifiable structural causal model and a
contrastive intervention, which the judge treats as a change of
scientific environment rather than another debate template. CoI-Agent
also reaches chemistry tools and optimal design (scenario 3) but keeps
method novelty at 3 because the core loop is still competing DAGs plus
Bayesian design. Virtual Scientists leave the generic science agent and
audit medical imaging workflows (scenario 3), at the cost of method and
insight (both 2): the judge reads ARCA as a calibration and
confounder-stress toolkit stitched onto existing radiology agents.
AgentLaboratory stays inside the Popper cluster on LAB-Bench and
ChemBench (scenario 1). AI-Scientist-V2 is the brittle case: the judge
says the core workflow can be prototyped, but the write is
underspecified, so technical feasibility drops to 3 and testability to 4. Validity remains at ceiling for every family except those two
execution dents.
