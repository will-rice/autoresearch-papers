Across human history, knowledge has undergone several transitions, from
orality to writing and then to digital archives. At each transition,
humans established new conventions to preserve knowledge for the new
era. Nowadays, as research agents are increasingly deployed for
laboratory experiments, knowledge is crossing another boundary: from
records written for humans to those for machines. In this work, we
introduce VerifiedLab, a multi-agent harness framework for this
human-to-machine transition of knowledge. VerifiedLab transforms
research output from _passive artifacts_ into _active systems_ that
accelerate downstream use, adoption and prediction of future trends. In
VerifiedLab, a team of AI scientists with distinct roles reasons over
records verified field by field: a principal investigator plans, a
librarian computes the evidence directly from the records, specialists
draft, and a gate and a critic check each claim against the measured
reliability of its data before the harness decides whether to release or
withhold it. We demonstrate the efficacy of VerifiedLab through
extensive experiments on perovskite solar cells: VerifiedLab recovers
known physical trends, anticipates devices reported a year later, and
lets AI scientists inherit the literature together with a measure of how
far it can be trusted. By scaling the inference-time verification
compute, VerifiedLab introduces a new pathway for knowledge
dissemination and machine harness for AI co-scientists.

For most of human history, knowledge was carried in human minds. How to
knap a flint, read the weather or set a broken bone was held in memory,
shown by hand and learned by watching. Across generations, social
learning allowed knowledge to accumulate beyond individual
discovery^([5](#bib.bib66)). But a chain of minds is a fragile medium.
Each time a skill passed from one mind to another, a step could be
dropped. An instruction could be misremembered. A recipe could be
repeated without the circumstance that had made it work. Preserving
improvements through such handovers is central to cumulative
culture^([15](#bib.bib62), [50](#bib.bib63)).

Writing gave knowledge a durable medium. A record on clay, papyrus or
paper outlived its author, travelled beyond its author’s reach, and
could be consulted unchanged a century later. Printing multiplied the
copies and lowered the cost of spreading them. More scholars in
different cities could consult and compare copies of the same
text^([18](#bib.bib64)). Journals such as the seventeenth-century
_Philosophical Transactions_ organized a continuing public record. Over
subsequent centuries, conventions of authorship, methods reporting,
editorial review and citation developed around that
record^([19](#bib.bib65)). Each of these steps carried knowledge across
a boundary: from mind to page, from manuscript to press, from private
letter to public record. At each boundary, meaning could leak away until
a convention was invented to hold it in place. A manuscript without an
author could not be questioned, an experiment without a method could not
be repeated, and a claim without a citation could not be traced. Science
became trustworthy because of these conventions, not only because of the
medium.

For the literature, the present is a moment of déjà vu: it is crossing
such a boundary again (Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Fig. 1")). Papers are now
digital and searchable, while FAIR principles make the findability,
provenance and reuse of scientific data explicit^([78](#bib.bib67)).
More and more of these records are read by machines, and the transition
this time runs from papers written for human readers to structured
records that machines act on. Research agents built on large language
models (LLMs) read prior work, form hypotheses, plan and run experiments
and choose the next
experiment^([46](#bib.bib29), [80](#bib.bib30), [22](#bib.bib31), [70](#bib.bib60), [52](#bib.bib61), [4](#bib.bib32), [71](#bib.bib34)).
Where experiments are themselves code, an agent can write a script, run
it and read the
answer^([46](#bib.bib29), [28](#bib.bib55), [10](#bib.bib57)). In
chemistry and materials science the situation is different. The
knowledge an agent needs is spread over tens of thousands of papers.
Each paper mixes prose, tables, figures and supporting files. Turning
those reports into structured data is a separate information-extraction
task^([14](#bib.bib15), [79](#bib.bib16)). Consider perovskite solar
cells (PSCs), whose efficiency has advanced from early sensitized
devices to certified research cells^([39](#bib.bib5), [55](#bib.bib4)).
Progress depends on composition^([31](#bib.bib6), [62](#bib.bib7)),
interfaces^([84](#bib.bib9), [72](#bib.bib10)), processing
chemistry^([49](#bib.bib12), [77](#bib.bib13)), tandem
integration^([42](#bib.bib11)) and grain-boundary
control^([26](#bib.bib14)). The paper describes several devices; its
best efficiency is in a table, the recipe for the light-absorbing layer
in the methods, and the stability test in a supplementary figure with
its own temperature, humidity and light. A solvent ratio mentioned in
passing applies to one variant only. Each number belongs to one device,
one step and one set of conditions; measurement and stability-reporting
guidelines require those links for meaningful
comparisons^([13](#bib.bib71), [36](#bib.bib49)). A language model can
recover the numbers while losing these links. It writes plausible wrong
values: a reversed ratio, a layer attached to the wrong device, a
stability figure separated from its protocol. Such a record passes a
glance and fails in use, and its errors pass into every plan built on
top of it. The alternative, curating the literature by hand, takes years
of expert labour^([29](#bib.bib2)). Automated databases have captured
device properties and selected fabrication
details^([2](#bib.bib1), [75](#bib.bib3)); we extend this line of work
to the complete record of each experiment, verified field by field.

This work addresses this challenge by converting each paper into a
verified record that functions as a machine-readable account of the
experiment, exposing its devices, procedures and measurements as fields
that carry their evidence and their measured reliability. Language
models extract materials data from scientific
prose^([14](#bib.bib15), [58](#bib.bib39), [24](#bib.bib17));
text-mining systems recover chemical entities and synthesis
descriptions^([69](#bib.bib37), [37](#bib.bib50)), while literature
embeddings capture associations between materials^([74](#bib.bib38)).
Long-context benchmarks nevertheless show that access to a document does
not ensure reliable use of evidence throughout it^([44](#bib.bib75)).
Studies of LLM judges measure agreement with people and expose
consistency and correctness
limits^([83](#bib.bib35), [73](#bib.bib58), [41](#bib.bib48)).
Research-agent benchmarks assess experimentation, data-driven discovery
and paper
reproduction^([28](#bib.bib55), [48](#bib.bib56), [12](#bib.bib52), [68](#bib.bib54), [67](#bib.bib53)).
Scientific fact-checking and claim–evidence benchmarks assess support
for claims, including evidence dispersed across full
papers^([76](#bib.bib51), [30](#bib.bib59)); attribution and citation
benchmarks test the support of generated statements by identified
sources^([60](#bib.bib77), [20](#bib.bib76)). Each of these addresses a
part of the task. We preserve device, procedure and measurement identity
across an entire experimental record under field-specific scientific
rules, with verifiers calibrated against experts and reliability
estimates that stay with the records.

In this work, we introduce VerifiedLab, a multi-agent harness framework
for this human-to-machine transition of knowledge (Fig.
[2](#S0.F2 "Fig. 2")). We built the harness for perovskite solar cells
and tested it at each stage, from the reading of a paper to the use of
the resulting records in scientific reasoning. Frontier models recover
most of a record but lose its procedures: current agents misplace values
that are tied to several procedural steps in experimental materials
science and chemistry, and they share the same failure patterns. Written
rules close much of this gap on the verifier side, bringing verifiers to
expert-level agreement regardless of model size. Run over the recent
literature of the field, the harness returns a database in which every
category of field carries a judged accuracy. On this database we place a
team of AI scientists with distinct roles. A principal investigator
plans the study, a librarian computes the evidence directly from the
records, specialists draft the claims, and a gate and a critic check
each claim against the measured reliability of its data before the
harness decides whether to release or withhold it. Reasoning only over
the evidence, the team recovers known physical trends and anticipates
devices reported a year later, and it withholds the claims, on stability
for instance, whose data are not yet reliable enough to carry them.

### Verified Harness: Record, Policy and Check

The verified harness has three parts: a record, a policy and a check.
The record is a schema; a generator agent fills it, and a verifier agent
checks every filled value against evidence, both under written policies
(Fig. [2](#S0.F2 "Fig. 2")a and Methods). The schema was written by
experimentalists. Its fields, nearly a hundred of them, describe a
paper’s best-performing device in seven groups: reference data, cell
definition, device stack, material synthesis, device fabrication,
current–voltage (J–V) metrics and stability metrics. Each field has a
fixed name and a convention for its value. Units stay attached to
numbers. Layer stacks are written in order, as in TiO2/ZrO2/carbon. Null
has one meaning: the paper does not say.

The scientific policy lives in versioned Skills rather than in prompts
tied to one model. This makes an important dependency of the pipeline
explicit, a concern also identified in work on technical debt in
machine-learning systems^([65](#bib.bib83)). Rigour is written down
there, in the reviewing consensus of experts, the equivalence rules of
chemistry and the boundary conditions of physics, and applied to every
field. It is not left to a model that cannot be inspected or corrected.
The annotation Skill gives a generator the rule for choosing the
champion device, the priority among sources of evidence, the alignment
of layers with values, the assignment of each measurement to its device
and the conditions for writing Null. It forbids filling a field from
general knowledge or from a different device. The verification Skill
defines, for a verifier, the equivalence of two values. Chemical
aliases, compatible units, numerical tolerances and ordered sequences
are accepted. Reversed ratios, reordered layers and values borrowed from
another device are rejected. For each field the verifier sees the schema
entry, the candidate value, the expert reference and the rule, and it
returns one of five labels: the values agree, both are empty, the
candidate is missing, or the candidate is unsupported or wrong.
Decisions are made field by field, so each can be audited on its own,
and a wrong rule is caught by the audit and fixed in the next version.
The score of a generator never assumes that the automated judge is
right, because the judge is itself measured against expert decisions.
The reference for those measurements comes from two expert-curated
collections of PSC papers (Methods). One was annotated by us against the
full schema; the other was drawn from a community-curated
database^([29](#bib.bib2)). Experts annotated independently and resolved
disagreements before any automated verifier was run, separating
annotation, adjudication and evaluation in line with established
quality-management practice^([38](#bib.bib78)). Many fields are
legitimately Null, so a record left entirely empty already scores well
above zero. This empty-record baseline is the floor against which
generators are read. Figure
[6](#S0.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns") shows the whole workflow
in use by a team of agents that reaches the literature only through the
harness; we return to it after the benchmarks.

### Procedural Information Hampers Recovery

The first test asks how much of a record today’s readers recover, and
which part of it they lose. We ran eighteen generators, proprietary and
open-weight, through the fixed harness, so that differences between runs
reflect the model alone. A calibrated verifier, described in the next
section, scored them (Fig.
[3](#S0.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ Procedural Information Hampers Recovery")a and
Supplementary Fig. 1). With the annotation Skill, the strongest
proprietary generators recover most of each record. The two open-weight
generators sit close to the empty-record floor, because most of their
errors are fields they leave blank. The proprietary generators fail
differently. For most of them, the largest class of error is a value
that is present but wrong, ahead of omissions and ahead of values that
the paper never gave.

The errors gather where the information is procedural (Fig.
[3](#S0.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ Procedural Information Hampers Recovery")b and
Supplementary Fig. 2). Bibliographic fields are at ceiling for every
model. Stability and synthesis fields are where models part company, and
in the stability fields the usual mistake is to invent a condition that
the paper left unspecified (Fig.
[3](#S0.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ Procedural Information Hampers Recovery")c). To
separate knowledge from binding, we grouped the fields by what each one
asks of the reader. Localized scalars, which a paper states once in one
place, are recovered best. Conditions tied to a single processing step
or test come next. Relational fields, whose value is an ordered or
multi-part structure attached to a layer or a device, are recovered
worst, and they come back wrong rather than empty. A sizeable share of
reported layer stacks and precursor-concentration lists do not match the
reference. A spin-coating speed or time almost always does. These
failures are shared, not model-specific. The ranking of fields by
difficulty is nearly the same for every generator, and the same small
set of fields sits at the bottom for strong and weak models alike:
additive and precursor concentrations, the atmosphere of each processing
stage and the stability protocol. A stronger model lifts every category
but does not remove these modes. Every frontier model knows perovskite
chemistry. None of them reliably attaches a value to the device, the
step and the condition that produced it, and a stronger reader narrows
this gap without closing it. Procedural information, not chemical
knowledge, is the part of the record that hampers recovery.

Prompting does not close it either. Switching the annotation Skill on,
with everything else fixed, helps every generator (Fig.
[3](#S0.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ Procedural Information Hampers Recovery")a), but
the help is uneven. Fields defined by an encoding convention, such as a
flag for sequential deposition^([7](#bib.bib73)), improve sharply.
Fields that require the model to align evidence across the paper, such
as the list of precursor compounds or the atmosphere of a particular
step, can get worse under the same instructions. Policy text tells an
agent how to write a value down, but not which device or step the value
belongs to. This is why the harness makes verification a separate role
rather than trying to prompt its way to reliability. For deployment we
chose the generator on the accuracy–cost frontier, a small proprietary
model that gives up little accuracy against the best reader for a
several-fold saving in cost per paper (Methods).

### Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement

The second test moves from the agent that writes the record to the agent
that checks it. A verifier is not ground truth, and its authority has to
come from somewhere. In the verified harness that source is the written
rule, and we tested whether rules, rather than the size of the model
behind them, can bring a verifier to agreement with experts. We tested
this in three steps: we calibrated proprietary verifiers against expert
decisions, ran ten open-weight verifiers with and without the rules,
and, within each model family, set the smallest checkpoint with rules
against the largest without. Compared with the expert decisions on both
collections (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")a),
three proprietary verifiers already agree with the experts most of the
time without rules. With the verification Skill, their agreement rises
to within a few decisions per thousand, and the gain is largest for the
weakest model. The strongest configuration, audited field by field, is
the reference verifier used for the generator benchmark above and for
the open-weight study that follows.

To see the mechanism, we ran ten open-weight verifiers from four model
families on the same field decisions, with and without the Skill, and
scored their agreement with the audited reference (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")b
and Supplementary Fig. 3). The Skill improves almost every verifier. It
pulls the checkpoints closer together and helps most where the starting
point is weakest. Most non-empty pairs are identical once trivially
normalized and are decided correctly either way. The Skill acts on the
remainder, where the candidate and the reference say the same thing in
different words, and nearly every decision it changes turns a rejection
into an acceptance (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")c
and Supplementary Table 1). The gains land where surface form misleads:
journal aliases, synonyms for a deposition method, notation for
humidity, units for a scan rate. Two fields regress because of defects
in the policy, which the next version of the Skill corrects. Ordered
layer stacks improve but remain the hardest decision. The models do not
lack chemistry; without rules, a verifier rejects an equivalent value
because it is written differently, and the Skill tells it that the two
are the same.

Scale does not order verifier reliability (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")d
and Supplementary Table 2). In three of the four families, the larger
checkpoint is less accurate without rules than the smaller one, and the
smaller checkpoint with the Skill beats the larger one without it, by
margins whose bootstrap intervals exclude zero. In the fourth family the
largest model without rules still edges the smallest with rules, by a
margin that cannot be told from zero. Two checkpoints that the Skill
does not help show the limit of what rules can do. They already accept
almost every equivalent pair and fail instead by accepting values that
are wrong, and rules that license equivalences cannot make a verifier
stricter (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")c).
Rules replace scale for verifiers whose errors are lexical rejections,
which is the common case, but not for verifiers whose errors are
permissive acceptances. The reference labels are the decisions of one
proprietary model, so closer agreement may in part mean adopting that
model’s conventions; the expert audit bounds this risk for the reference
verifier itself. In practice, a laboratory can run an expert-grade
verifier on a small open-weight model if the rules exist, and it can fix
a wrong rule in the next version of the Skill, neither of which a larger
model offers. The harness, not the model, is what brings these AI
scientists to expert agreement.

### Scaling Inference-time Compute Guarantees Reliability

Reliability at the scale of a field is bought with inference-time
compute. After the generator has written a record, every field of it is
checked again, by two independent judges of different capability, and
the cost of these checks is the price of a measured reliability. A
reliability measured on a hundred and fifty papers is useful only if it
survives this deployment and is delivered together with the data. We ran
the harness over the peer-reviewed experimental PSC literature of the
past five years, with the frontier-cost generator chosen above. The
judges verified every field of every record under the verification Skill
(Methods). Their disagreement is a cheap check on the evaluation itself.
The harness returned one record per paper and a judged accuracy for
every category of field (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The two judges differ
by less than one accuracy point in any category.

The pattern across categories matches where the information sits in a
paper, and it is the pattern the benchmark predicted. Fields that are
local and lexically anchored are near ceiling: the reference data, the
cell definition, the fabrication steps and the J–V metrics. Fields that
require following a procedure across sections are harder: the device
stack and the material synthesis. Stability fields are hardest, because
a stability protocol mixes conditions, durations and relative values
that are often reported only in a figure or in the supporting
information. Recall exceeds precision overall and in every category but
one (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The generator rarely misses information
that is present; its usual failure is an incorrect value, the same
profile the benchmark found on the expert-curated papers. Low-precision
categories can propagate incorrect values into later analysis, an
instance of the downstream effects of data-quality problems documented
in other AI pipelines^([63](#bib.bib70)).

The database covers literature through April 2025 and complements the
manually curated resource^([29](#bib.bib2)) and earlier automatically
extracted datasets^([2](#bib.bib1), [75](#bib.bib3)). Annotating the
whole corpus cost a small fraction of the expert time behind the
manually curated database. Category reliability is released alongside
the records, extending the documentation of intended uses and evaluation
conditions advocated by datasheets and model
cards^([21](#bib.bib68), [54](#bib.bib69)). These category scores do not
provide confidence estimates for individual values. From it follows a
rule for anyone who uses the data, person or agent: a field may enter
automated analysis only if its category reliability is high enough. Our
discovery experiments obey this rule, and neither uses stability, the
least reliable category.

### From Knowns to Unknowns

The last test is the use of the records in scientific reasoning, from
the knowns of the field to its unknowns. We ran it at three levels: the
recovery of trends that the field has established by other means, the
prediction of devices that no model has seen, and the use of the records
by a team of AI scientists under the reliability rule.

Figure [5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")a–c shows the J–V
landscape read from the high-reliability categories alone. The
distribution of reported efficiency has the shape the field would
expect, and so do the distributions of open-circuit voltage,
short-circuit current density and fill factor. High-efficiency devices
cluster in a narrow band of bandgaps below the Shockley–Queisser
limit^([51](#bib.bib19), [66](#bib.bib20)) (Fig.
[5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")b). Efficiency falls and
spreads as the device area grows^([81](#bib.bib21)) (Fig.
[5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")c). Within this corpus,
regular (_n–i–p_) devices have higher peak efficiencies than inverted
ones; this is a property of the sampled years, not a universal ordering
of architectures^([82](#bib.bib22)). Composition and surface-treatment
associations are consistent with the broader literature on mixed-cation
absorbers and interfacial
modification^([9](#bib.bib23), [43](#bib.bib24), [33](#bib.bib74)).
Recovering these relationships supports aggregate use of the records,
without establishing the accuracy of every value or a causal effect of
treatment. Each record is a paper’s champion device, so these
distributions describe what was reported, not every device that was
made.

For an AI scientist, a literature-derived dataset matters only if the
knowledge it encodes transfers to devices it has not seen. Following
LLM4Mat-Bench’s comparison of task-specific and general-purpose
predictors^([56](#bib.bib40)), we posed a temporally held-out task:
predict a device’s efficiency from its recipe alone, the fields
describing the cell, the stack, the synthesis and the fabrication.
Models learn from papers published up to one year, are tuned on the next
and are scored once on the year after that. Every performance, stability
and bibliographic field is withheld to reduce target leakage
(Methods)^([34](#bib.bib80)). We compared gradient-boosted
trees^([35](#bib.bib46)) and a tabular foundation
model^([27](#bib.bib44)), text encoders fine-tuned on a plain-text
serialization of the
recipe^([25](#bib.bib42), [59](#bib.bib43), [61](#bib.bib41)), and a
general-purpose LLM^([23](#bib.bib45)) whose training data end before
any test paper appeared, used either through frozen embeddings or
prompted with retrieved examples (Fig.
[5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")h). Every trained model
clearly beats the predictor that always guesses the training mean, even
though reported efficiencies drift upward from the training years to the
test year. A tree model on a short list of expert-chosen descriptors
already separates good devices from poor ones. Giving it every recipe
field improves it further, so the full schema that the harness
reconstructs carries information that expert descriptors leave out. The
fine-tuned text encoders, which read the record as a few lines of text
and need no feature engineering, do best of all, and most of their
remaining error sits on low-efficiency devices (Fig.
[5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")d–g; every model is shown
in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). With the architecture held fixed,
formamidinium content and bandgap rank highly among the descriptors.
Studies of composition and formamidinium absorbers make these
associations plausible^([31](#bib.bib6), [53](#bib.bib8)), but feature
importance alone does not establish a mechanism. A general-purpose LLM
is no substitute for training on the records: prompted without examples
it predicts nearly the same efficiency for every device, and the
examples it is handed by retrieval account for most of what it gains.
Residual error may reflect omitted processing choices, laboratory
differences, extraction errors and model limitations; measurement
conventions are another documented source of
variability^([13](#bib.bib71)).

At the third level we sketched the Verified Lab, a team of LLM agents

reaches the literature only through the harness (Fig.

principal-investigator agent plans the study and convenes three
specialists (a device physicist, a materials chemist and a data
scientist) and a scientific critic. A librarian agent, which is the
harness itself, answers their queries with records that carry their
category reliability and their provenance, and computes the evidence
directly from those records. A reliability gate admits only the
categories above the threshold, and the critic checks each drafted claim
against the evidence before the harness releases or withholds it. A
forecaster, the text encoder trained above, scores candidate recipes. In
a six-phase walk-through of a design question (Fig.
[6](#S0.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")b), the team confines its
reasoning to the categories the gate admits. It retrieves the champion
devices with a reported efficiency, recovers that the best of them sit
in a narrow bandgap window and that efficiency falls with area, and
returns recipes from held-out papers whose forecast efficiencies lie
close to the reported values. Each field is linked to its DOI, its rule
and its verifier label. One design round is laid out as a pipeline (Fig.
[6](#S0.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")c). Two specialists move a
recipe from a single-cation absorber to a formamidinium-based one with
an additive and a surface treatment. The librarian returns the matching
records with their labels. The critic strikes the one clause that rests
on an unreliable category. The dialogue is illustrative, but every
number in it is computed from the released records. The demonstration
shows the contract we propose for AI scientists, not a validated
discovery; its running implementation, a research workspace released
with the code, is shown in Supplementary Figs. 7–15. The same
abstractions serve a human laboratory and an AI scientist (Fig.
[2](#S0.F2 "Fig. 2")b): a typed record in place of the PDF, a versioned
Skill in place of a model-specific prompt, a reliability-scoped query in
place of recall, and a verifier call and a provenance call in place of
the colleague who re-derives a number by hand. Together, these results
indicate that the verified records carry the knowns of the field in a
form from which its unknowns can be approached, at all three levels, and
that the reliability rule is what makes each level credible. The trends
are read from categories whose accuracy is known, the forecaster is
trained on them, and the critic enforces them.

## Discussion

We set out to carry the experimental literature across its newest
boundary with its meaning intact, and to measure the agents that do the
carrying. Separating the writing of a record from its checking,
governing both with written scientific policy and holding everything
else fixed turned the output of a language model into a measured
artefact. The result is a benchmark that shows where research agents
fail, a verifier whose agreement with experts is known, and a field-wide
database in which every category of field carries a judged accuracy. The
same error profile appears on the benchmark and in the deployment.
Agents recover isolated facts, fail when a value has to be tied to a
device, a step or a condition, and fail by writing a plausible wrong
value rather than by leaving a blank. These are the errors that an
aggregate accuracy hides and that pass silently into an AI scientist’s
plans.

Three principles follow, and each runs against a common assumption about
LLM-driven science. First, the bottleneck is binding, not knowledge.
Existing systems demonstrate scientific text extraction and
tool-augmented
reasoning^([14](#bib.bib15), [58](#bib.bib39), [47](#bib.bib33)). These
successes leave a separate question: whether each extracted value
remains attached to the correct experiment. Every frontier model fails
in the same procedural places. A stronger model narrows the gap without
closing it, and prompting fixes conventions but not alignment. Second,
rules are worth more than scale, and rigour should be written down
rather than hoped for. A verifier’s authority can come from calibration
against experts under explicit, versioned rules rather than from the
size of its backbone. The gain falls on exactly the decisions that
string matching cannot settle, and the defects the audit exposed were
fixed in the next version of the Skill. Writing the reviewing consensus
of experts, the equivalence rules of chemistry and the boundary
conditions of physics into versioned Skills makes verification
inspectable, correctable and portable across models, none of which a
larger model provides. The limit of rules is equally clear: they license
equivalences, so they repair verifiers that are too strict and not
verifiers that are too lenient. Third, reliability travels with the
data. A reliability measured once on a benchmark and then set aside
protects no one. Measured per category and attached to every record, it
becomes the rule by which any later analysis, human or automated,
decides what to trust. Seen against the history we began with, these
principles are the rules for the newest boundary. They do for the
machine-read record what review and citation did for print.

The harness has limitations that we consider inherent in current
LLM-based pipelines, and each points to a remedy. The reliability of the
full database is judged by language models rather than by complete
expert annotation, and one judge shares the generator’s backbone and may
favour its output^([57](#bib.bib36)). The expert audit bounds this risk
for the calibrated verifier and the disagreement between the two judges
keeps it visible, but a sample of deployed records should be re-audited
as the database is reused. The verification Skill describes equivalences
in more detail than non-equivalences, which is why rules help
over-strict verifiers and not lenient ones; explicit guardrails against
false acceptance are the next addition. We studied one materials domain,
so the schema and category scores need not transfer. Documenting the
corpus and its intended use is necessary before generalizing to another
setting^([3](#bib.bib79)). Each record is a paper’s champion device and
positive results are more likely to be published, so the database
describes reported best devices. The trends and forecasts are
associations tested on one held-out year; they suggest hypotheses and do
not establish causes, consistent with cautions about interpreting
data-driven materials models^([8](#bib.bib25), [64](#bib.bib26)). The
weakest categories, synthesis and stability, require following a
procedure across a long document and reading figures and supplements.
Multimodal inputs and targeted human review of these fields are the most
direct ways to close the gap. Finally, the harness relies on commercial
models whose versions change; we pin every version and release the
records as the reference artefact.

Human expertise remains part of the design. Experts write the schema,
author and version the Skills, adjudicate the reference records and
audit the verifier. The harness scales their judgement to a whole field
and reports, category by category, how far that scaling can be trusted.
The error profile suggests a division of labour that we have motivated
but not yet tested end to end. Localized scalars and exact matches can
be accepted automatically, conditions and relational fields need
verification under calibrated rules, and ordered sequences, multi-part
concentrations and stability protocols stay with the experts.

Looking ahead, just as journals now expect data and code to be
available, we anticipate that research agents will expect the literature
to arrive as verified records with an explicit reliability. The ease
with which a paper can be turned into such a record will itself become a
measure of how reproducibly it was written. Every earlier medium of
knowledge acquired its conventions after the fact, once the cost of
unprotected transmission had become visible. A verified-literature
harness offers AI scientists in the experimental sciences those
conventions in advance: the data they reason over, and a measure of how
far that data can be trusted. As agents move from answering questions to
choosing what to synthesize and measure, that measure becomes a
prerequisite.

## Methods

### Harness design

#### Task.

Given a paper package $`d`$ (main text plus supporting information) and
a schema $`\mathcal{S}=\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}`$ of $`m=97`$ fields, the
harness outputs one record $`r(d)`$ describing the paper’s
best-performing device, with every field filled by a string value or an
explicit Null. Selecting one device per paper gives a comparable unit
across heterogeneous reports; it also introduces a selection effect that
we account for in the discovery analyses. The 97 fields fall into seven
categories (reference data, cell definition, device stack, material
synthesis, device fabrication, J–V metrics and stability metrics).

#### Versioned Skills.

Scientific policy is separated from the code that executes it. The
annotation Skill $`A^{\alpha}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C})`$ (released as
psc-annotate-v1@1.0.0) couples the schema with a policy $`\mathcal{C}`$
for champion-device selection (rank by explicitly reported scan PCE, not
stabilized output, certification or module efficiency), evidence
priority (prefer explicit device-specific statements; map sample codes
across article and supplement before combining fabrication and
performance values), layer alignment (report the complete electrically
active stack; align slash-delimited properties with layer order),
measurement identity and the use of Null (when a value is absent,
ambiguous between devices or only inferable). The verification Skill
$`V^{\beta}=(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{L})`$ (psc-verify-v1@1.1.0) supplies
field rules $`\mathcal{R}=\{r_{j}\}`$ defining chemical aliases,
compatible units, numerical tolerances, ordered-sequence semantics and
device-specific comparisons, together with the allowed label set
$`\mathcal{L}`$. A machine-readable registry pins the full semantic
version used in every run; model changes preserve the evaluation
contract, whereas changes to a schema, tolerance or decision rule
require a new Skill version. The model-facing views materialized from
both Skills are released with the code.

#### Execution boundary.

The harness $`H`$ parses the paper package, materializes the annotation
Skill for a model adapter, validates candidate keys and types (rejecting
malformed records), pairs each candidate value with the expert reference
and field rule into $`q_{j}=(s_{j},\hat{y}_{j},y^{*}_{j},r_{j})`$,
invokes the verifier on each unit, validates the returned label against
$`\mathcal{L}`$, aggregates metrics and writes an audit bundle
containing document, candidate, reference, policy versions, model
versions and decision provenance. Every generator and verifier traverses
this same boundary.

#### Generator and verifier agents.

A generator agent reads the full paper package, identifies the
best-performing device and emits a JSON record conforming to
$`\mathcal{S}`$. A verifier agent receives, for each field, the
candidate value, the reference value and the field rule and returns one
of TN (both empty), FP (reference empty, candidate filled), FN
(reference present, candidate empty), TP (both present and
scientifically equivalent) or FP*(mismatch) (both present and
non-equivalent). Accuracy, precision, recall and $`F*{1}`$ follow the
standard definitions with the false-positive count equal to
$`\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FP}\_{\mathrm{mismatch}}`$. Under this metric an
entirely empty record scores 36.4% accuracy on Collection A, which we
report as the empty-record baseline.

### Expert-curated testbed

The reference comprises two collections. Collection A contributes 100
papers annotated by us against all 97 fields; Collection B contributes
50 papers with 73 overlapping fields drawn from the Perovskite
Database^([29](#bib.bib2)). For each paper, domain experts inspected the
article and supplement, annotated independently against the pinned
annotation Skill, identified field-wise disagreements and resolved them
by discussion and re-inspection before any automated verifier was
evaluated. The collections total 13,350 field decisions; an average
paper package is 23,231 input tokens and an average record 1,586 output
tokens.

### Generator benchmark

We evaluated 16 proprietary and 2 open-weight generators (release date,
pinned version and context length). Each generator produced one record
per Collection A paper with and without the annotation Skill, with the
harness, schema, source text, parser and output validator held fixed;
the o3 verifier with the verification Skill, calibrated as described
below, provided the field decisions. We report accuracy, precision,
recall and $`F_{1}`$ over 9,700 decisions per generator (Supplementary
Fig. 1) and accuracy by subfield (Supplementary Fig. 2). Error
composition decomposes decisions into omissions (reference present,
candidate Null), wrong values (both present, non-equivalent) and
over-prediction (reference Null, candidate filled), each conditioned on
the relevant reference state so that subfields with different null
shares remain comparable. The three field classes (localized scalar, 24
fields; relational, 30; condition, 43) are an analysis grouping of the
schema by the competence a field exposes, assigned from the schema
definitions, not labels in the dataset. Per-field accuracy ranks were
compared across generators with pairwise Spearman correlation. Request
cost per paper for each proprietary generator was taken from the
provider’s list price at the pinned version; the two open-weight
generators have no API price and are shown without cost.
Annotation-Skill variants with increasing field-specific detail were
materialized for gpt-5-mini and gpt-4o-mini to measure how much domain
policy contributes beyond the base model; these are experimental
variants, not interchangeable versions of the released Skill.

### Verifier calibration against experts

Three proprietary backbones (o3, gpt-5-mini and gpt-5-nano) verified
generator records against the expert references on both collections,
with and without the verification Skill, and each automated label was
compared with the expert decision for the same field (Fig.
[4](#S0.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ Verified Harness Brings AI Scientists to Expert Agreement")a).
The o3 configuration with the Skill was then audited field by field and
used as the reference verifier for the generator benchmark and the
open-weight study.

### Open-weight verifier study

Ten open-weight checkpoints (Qwen3-4B, -8B, -14B; Gemma-3-4B, -12B;
Ministral-3-3B, -8B, -14B; Phi-4-mini (3.8B), Phi-4 (14B)) verified the
same deterministic sample: a paper-round-robin pass selecting one
evaluated generator record from each of the 100 Collection A papers,
giving 9,700 field decisions per condition (Supplementary Fig. 3).
Audited o3 decisions with the Skill provided the comparison labels
(4,622 TP, 3,107 TN, 548 FP*(mismatch), 323 FP and 1,100 FN).
Candidate–reference missingness assigns TN, FP or FN before inference,
so the verifier decides only the 5,198 non-empty comparisons (TP versus
FP*(mismatch)); the other 4,502 fields form the missingness stratum,
whose accuracy (0.9636) is identical in both conditions. We report
overall accuracy over all 9,700 decisions and semantic accuracy over the
5,198 non-empty decisions. All runs used temperature 0, seed 42,
bfloat16 inference, a 16,384-token context and JSON-schema-constrained
decoding through xgrammar in vLLM 0.12.0^([40](#bib.bib82)) on NVIDIA
A100 40GB GPUs, with Qwen3 thinking disabled; each run configuration
pins model and tokenizer revisions, prompt hashes, source digests,
package versions and GPU metadata, and all 200 outputs per backbone were
recoverable with no invalid record or field. The Skill effect is the
paired difference
$`\Delta=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{with}}-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{without}}`$;
we resampled the 100 records with replacement, retaining all 97 fields
within each sampled record, and report 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles over
200 resamples for the per-model deltas and over 2,000 resamples for the
within-family scale-versus-rules comparisons (Supplementary Table 2).
Acceptance and rejection rates (Supplementary Table 1) are accuracies on
the non-empty pairs the reference labels TP and FP\_(mismatch),
respectively. The field-wise audit pools each field across the ten
checkpoints.

### Corpus and deployment

We collected 10,258 peer-reviewed experimental PSC papers published
between 2021 and April 2025 and indexed in Web of Science, each as main
text plus supporting information. gpt-5-mini (gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07)
served as the generator under the annotation Skill; it was chosen
because it lies on the accuracy–cost frontier of the generator
benchmark. Every record was then judged field by field by two
independent verifier agents, gpt-5-mini and o3 (o3-2025-04-16), under
the verification Skill. We report accuracy, precision, recall and
$`F_{1}`$ per field category as the mean over the two judges, with their
standard deviation as a check on the evaluation itself. The released
records carry per-record provenance linking each record to its source
digital object identifier (DOI), and category-level reliability scores.
The benchmark builds on MavaLM, published as a NeurIPS 2026 AI4Science
workshop poster^([16](#bib.bib47)).

### Reliability-scoped discovery

Following the reliability rule, neither discovery analysis uses
stability fields. The rediscovery analysis reads the high-reliability
categories (cell definition, device stack, device fabrication and J–V
metrics); the predictive models additionally read material-synthesis
fields as inputs. Distributions in Fig.
[5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns") use all records with a
parsable value for the plotted field; the Shockley–Queisser curve in
Fig. [5](#S0.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ From Knowns to Unknowns")b is the
detailed-balance limit for a 5,778 K blackbody Sun normalized to 1,000
W m⁻² and a 300 K cell.

### Temporal prediction benchmark

#### Data and split.

The target is the reverse-scan PCE, parsed as the first number in the
field. We kept the 8,450 records with a publication year and
$`0<\mathrm{PCE}\leq 30\%`$, which removes tandem devices and garbled
values. Records from papers published up to 2023 form the training set
(5,422), 2024 the validation set (2,170) and 2025 the test set (858).
The validation set was used only for early stopping and for choosing the
embedding layer and ridge penalty; the test set was scored once. The
share of devices above 20% PCE rises from 43.5% (train) to 58.2%
(validation) and 65.5% (test).

#### Inputs.

Models see the 71 recipe fields of the schema (cell definition, device
stack, synthesis and fabrication). Bibliographic and provenance fields,
all J–V fields and all stability fields were removed, because PCE equals
$`V_{\mathrm{OC}}\,J_{\mathrm{SC}}\,\mathrm{FF}/P_{\mathrm{in}}`$,
forward-scan, stabilized and certified PCE nearly copy the target, and
610 titles contain a percentage, usually the efficiency.

#### Tabular features.

The expert descriptors are 18 variables: presence of formamidinium (FA),
methylammonium (MA) and Cs on the A site, Pb and Sn on the B site, and
I, Br and Cl on the X site, parsed from the composition string;
perovskite bandgap; absorber thickness; measured cell area; whether a
perovskite additive, a surface treatment and an electron-transport-layer
(ETL) additive are reported; substrate (ITO, FTO, other); ETL deposition
method (spin coating, chemical bath, vapour phase or atomic layer
deposition, other); hole-transport-layer (HTL) deposition atmosphere
(N₂, air, other); and sequential spin coating. For all recipe fields,
fields with fewer than 10 training values were dropped; a field was
treated as numeric if at least 60% of its training values contain
exactly one number and was mapped to that number; otherwise its training
values with at least 10 occurrences became categories and the rest
mapped to “other”. The eight composition-site flags were appended,
giving 77 features.

#### Models.

LightGBM used a learning rate of 0.03, 31 leaves, row and column
subsampling of 0.8 and early stopping on validation MAE (200 rounds).
TabPFN v2 (v2.1.3) used default settings on the all-field table. Text
models read a deterministic serialization of the non-empty recipe fields
as “field name: value” lines, absorber fields first, built without any
LLM. MatSciBERT (512 tokens) and the T5-base encoder (1,024 tokens) were
fine-tuned with mean pooling, a linear head and L1 loss on the
standardized target for 10 epochs (AdamW^([45](#bib.bib84)), batch size
16, learning rates $`5\times 10^{-5}`$ and $`10^{-4}`$, 10% warm-up),
keeping the epoch with the lowest validation MAE. For the
frozen-embedding baseline, mean-pooled hidden states of
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct from layer 16 or 32 fed a ridge regression, with
layer and penalty chosen on validation. Prompted Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
decoded greedily for up to 48 tokens; five-shot prompts added the five
training records most similar to the query under TF-IDF. An answer was
valid only if it was a bare number or ended with a statement of the form
“…to be $`X`$%”; invalid answers were scored as the training mean and
their rate is reported.

#### Metrics and statistics.

We report MAE; the ratio of the mean absolute deviation of the test
targets to the MAE (MAD:MAE, as in LLM4Mat-Bench^([56](#bib.bib40));
values above 1 beat predicting the mean); $`R^{2}`$; and the ROC AUC for
PCE $`>20\%`$ computed with the predicted PCE as the score.
Stochastically trained models were run with three seeds and are reported
as mean $`\pm`$ s.d. Significance between models uses a one-sided paired
bootstrap^([17](#bib.bib81)) on test MAE (10,000 resamples) over
seed-averaged predictions.

#### Controlled-subset analysis.

As a complementary analysis holding the device architecture fixed, we
modelled the 2,445 devices with an SnO₂ ETL and a Spiro-OMeTAD
HTL^([32](#bib.bib72), [1](#bib.bib18)), described by the 18 expert
descriptors, with 5-fold cross-validation and grid-searched
hyperparameters within each fold. Regression models were gradient
boosting, XGBoost^([11](#bib.bib28)), LightGBM, random
forest^([6](#bib.bib27)), extra trees, decision tree, AdaBoost and
$`k`$-nearest neighbours; classification models (PCE $`>`$ 20%) were
random forest, LightGBM, XGBoost, gradient boosting, extra trees and
decision tree. Feature importance is gain-based for boosted and
impurity-based for bagging ensembles; importance rankings from any
single model should be read as hypotheses, not mechanisms.

## Ethics statement

The database is built from peer-reviewed publications and does not
involve human subjects or personal data. LLM-extracted scientific data
can contain confident but incorrect values, and our results show that
incorrect values, not omissions, are the dominant error. Users should
consult the per-category reliability scores before relying on any field
and should not treat model predictions as substitutes for experimental
validation or safety assessment.
