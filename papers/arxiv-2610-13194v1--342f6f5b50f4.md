###### Abstract

Long-horizon autonomous research has shown promise in accelerating
scientific progress through sustained cycles of hypothesis generation,
implementation, and validation. However, this Generate-Execute-Feedback
paradigm relies on reactive trial-and-error, where costly and
time-consuming executions introduce severe feedback delays, throttling
evolutionary progress under explosively expanding search space. To
address this challenge, we propose JANUS, a framework that unifies
future outcomes forecasting and past experience reflection through a
Predict-Verify-Evolve loop driven by a co-evolving world model. Within
an evolving graph, the research agent proposes candidates while world
model scores them by forecasting value improvement, information gain,
and exploration utility, then accordingly selects, refines, or prunes
branches prior to execution. After receiving ground-truth feedback,
JANUS distills experiences into evolving skills: hierarchical skills for
research agent, exploration-strategy skills for world model, and their
coordination protocols. This mechanism enables continual co-evolution:
the research agent sharpens candidate generation while the world model
refines its predictive judgment over extended horizons. Extensive
evaluations on MLS-Bench-Lite and real research tasks demonstrate that
JANUS significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving
both higher evolving efficiency and ceiling.

## 1 Introduction

Autonomous research (AutoResearch) ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib23);
[Gaddipati et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Qian et al., 2026](#bib.bib44);
[Liu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib2)) operationalizes fully automated,
end-to-end scientific discovery ([Team, 2026](#bib.bib22); [Novikov et
al., 2025](#bib.bib25)) through an iterative loop, wherein agents
generate candidate solutions, evaluate outcomes, incorporate execution
feedback, and use accumulated evidence to guide subsequent iterations.
Such discovery loops have driven substantial progress across diverse
domains, including algorithm design ([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib25);
[Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib40)), automated model
training ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Nam et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)), open-ended scientific problem solving ([Georgiev et
al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib41)), and systems
design ([Jaber and Jaber, 2026](#bib.bib43); [Cao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39)).

Recent AutoResearch frameworks have made significant progress by
leveraging agentic workflows ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Team,
2026](#bib.bib22); [Liu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib37); [Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib36)) or execution-driven heuristic search ([Jiang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46)). These frameworks typically follow a
“Generate-Execute-Feedback” loop, wherein the system iteratively refines
code based on runtime output ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)). However,
this paradigm suffers from a severe execution bottleneck, as physical
execution is computationally expensive and time-consuming, often
requiring hours of model training, simulation, and compilation. Such
expensive trial-and-error is severely magnified under expansive search
spaces: as ongoing experiments continually spawn new hypotheses, the
space of possible research trajectories branches combinatorially over
time.

Fundamentally, overcoming this bottleneck requires going beyond the
incremental gains in conventional iterative refinement. Instead, the
core objective of AutoResearch poses a _scaling_ problem: as research
compute expands, how should a system navigate long-horizon exploration
to maximize cumulative progress? We formalize this relationship between
research budget and achievable progress as the evolving scaling law of
autonomous research. A desirable research system should improve both its
_evolving efficiency_—how rapidly progress accumulates—and its _evolving
ceiling_—how far continued exploration advances. To achieve the desired
scaling behavior in AutoResearch, we advocate for a paradigm shift from
reactive trial-and-error to metacognitive exploration: (i) proactively
forecasting outcomes before physical execution, (ii) adaptively
allocating execution resources during search, and (iii) systematically
turning past experiences into reusable research intuition.

two-faced god who looks simultaneously toward the future and the past.
Similarly, our framework embodies this duality: one face looks forward
as a world model to forecast experimental outcomes prior to execution,
while the other looks backward to assimilate ground-truth feedback,
driving the co-evolution of the research agent, the world model and
their coordination. , a framework that unifies future outcome
forecasting and past experience reflection through a
“Predict-Verify-Evolve” loop driven by a co-evolving world model.
Specifically, we formalize an evolving research graph to serve as the
unifying foundation, enabling dynamic branch exploration, structured
experience management, and topology-aware performance attribution.
Within this graph, JANUS first forecasts before acting: the orchestrator
generates candidate proposals to expand the graph, while the world model
evaluates them via an adaptive exploration strategy balancing value
improvement, information gain, and exploration utility—then selecting,
refining, or pruning branches prior to execution. Upon receiving
execution feedback, JANUS then reflects on experience, distilling raw
execution logs into hierarchical and role-specific evolving skills. This
closed-loop process drives the continual co-evolution of the research
agent, the world model, and their coordination protocols, empowering the
system to continuously learn and evolve while transcending myopic
trial-and-error across complex, non-monotonic research spaces.

Empirically, we comprehensively evaluate JANUS across
MLS-Bench-Lite [Lyu et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib11) and real-world research
tasks covering algorithm optimization, system design, and scientific
discovery. We demonstrate that JANUS significantly outperforms
state-of-the-art baselines, consistently generating valid and
competitive solutions across various backbone models. We further trace
its evolving scaling laws, showing that JANUS achieves superior evolving
efficiency with lower compute budgets and a higher evolving ceiling
before plateauing. Ablation studies further validate the superiority of
both the adaptive exploration strategy and experience-driven
co-evolution.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose JANUS, a framework that unifies future outcome forecasting
  and past experience reflection through a “Predict-Verify-Evolve” loop
  driven by a co-evolving world model.
- •
  We introduce an evolving research graph, upon which we design a
  world-model-guided adaptive exploration strategy to navigate research
  toward superior trajectories and an experience-driven co-evolution
  mechanism for the research agent, world model, and their coordination.
- •
  We demonstrate that JANUS achieves state-of-the-art performance across
  MLS-Bench-Lite and real-world research tasks, exhibiting superior
  _evolving efficiency_ and a higher _evolving ceiling_ as additional
  search compute is invested.

## 2 Related Works

### 2.1 Autonomous Research Agent

#### Pipeline-Based Systems.

Early systems organize research as a sequence of explicit stages. The AI
Scientist [Lu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib27) established an end-to-end
idea-to-paper workflow, while Agent Laboratory [Schmidgall et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib35) structured a similar process around specialized
research assistants and human supervision. Subsequent systems
incorporate richer tool use and domain-specific capabilities [Weng et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib34); [Mitchener et al. (2025)](#bib.bib28); [Yuan et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib31); [Jansen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30); [Zhang et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib32); [Meng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib45). Such
pipelines are modular and interpretable, but they can propagate early
errors and offer limited flexibility for revisiting alternative research
directions.

#### Search-Based Systems.

Search-based research agents advance through explicit solution
exploration and refinement. AIDE [Jiang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib19)
performs iterative code-space search, while MLE-STAR [Nam et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib15) refines targeted pipeline components. AIRA [Toledo
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16) studies greedy, MCTS, and evolutionary search
policies, and AI Scientist-v2 [Yamada et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29) extends
tree search to research plans and experiments. ML-Master [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17) and MARS [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14) further
extend exploration through parallel experience reuse and budget-aware
search. These systems rely on costly execution feedback and lack
adaptive prioritization of research directions. Janus instead uses an
evolving world model to select promising directions before execution.

#### Multi-Agent Systems.

Multi-agent systems decompose research across agents with complementary
responsibilities. Early systems use specialized roles to structure
research [Schmidgall et al. (2025)](#bib.bib35); [Tang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib21), while R&D-Agent [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20)
couples researcher and developer agents through experimental feedback.
Recent systems further introduce persistent memory and
self-evolution [Lyu et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib33), or adversarial
executor–reviewer collaboration for reliable scientific reasoning [Yang
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib36). Arbor [Jin et al. (2026)](#bib.bib46)
instead uses a hypothesis tree coordinated by a long-lived agent and
executed by short-lived agents. However these systems still face
challenges in efficient information exchange and role-specific
experience utilization. Janus addresses this through a shared evolving
research graph and an experience-driven co-evolution mechanism.

### 2.2 LLMs as World Models

Recent work suggests that LLMs can function as implicit or explicit
world models for planning and decision making. RAP [Hao et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib1) frames reasoning as planning with an LLM world model,
while complementary approaches induce structured domain models (e.g.,
PDDL) from language for classical planning [Guan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib7).  [Gkountouras et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4) integrate
LLM agents with causal world models that explicitly capture environment
dynamics. Recent works [Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12); [Fang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib13); [Chae et al. (2025)](#bib.bib5); [Shen et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib6) further use LLM-based world models to simulate action
outcomes and evaluate candidate trajectories for agentic planning. Most
existing approaches target relatively fixed tasks and do not continually
update the world model. Janus instead enables the world model to evolve
with the research process, allowing its ability to improve over time.

## 3 The JANUS Framework

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

#### Evolving Research Graph.

As autonomous research unfolds over long horizons, systems suffer from
severe context drift and intractable credit assignment across
iterations. To address these challenges, we explicitly maintain an
evolving directed acyclic graph (DAG), enabling branch exploration,
experience reuse, and topology-aware performance attribution, denoted
(at step $`t`$) as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{G}_{t}=(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t}),\qquad v_{i}=(x_{i},\hat{V}_{i},y_{i},e_{i})\in\mathcal{V}_{t},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}\times\mathcal{V}_{t}.
``` |  | (1) |

Each node $`v_{i}`$ is partitioned into an Open or Closed state. An Open
node records a proposal $`x_{i}`$ and its world-model-estimated value
$`\hat{V}_{i}`$, whereas a Closed node additionally records its
implementation $`y_{i}`$ and experimental results $`e_{i}`$. An edge
$`(v_{i},v_{j})`$ indicates that the proposal $`x_{j}`$ is obtained by
directly modifying $`x_{i}`$, extending $`x_{i}`$, or combining
mechanisms from $`x_{i}`$ and other nodes. As research progresses, the
graph dynamically expands with new proposals, continually updating value
estimates from the world model and validating with experimental results,
thereby characterizing the full lifecycle evolution of autonomous
research.

#### AutoResearch as POMDP.

The iterative process of AutoResearch can be formulated as a
budget-constrained partially observable Markov decision process
(budget-constrained POMDP), denoted by
$`\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{T},\Omega,\mathcal{R})`$.
We define states, observations, and actions at step $`t`$ as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}=(\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},z_{t})\in\mathcal{S},\qquad o_{t}=(\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t})\in\mathcal{O},\qquad a_{t}=(a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}},a_{t}^{\mathrm{exe}})\in\mathcal{A}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`\mathcal{G}_{t}`$ is the evolving research graph introduced
above, $`B_{t}`$ is the remaining budget, and $`z_{t}`$ captures
unobserved factors such as attainable performance limits, test
distribution, and underlying execution mechanisms. Conditioned on
$`o_{t}`$, the research policy $`\pi`$ selects an action comprising
proposal generation $`a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}`$ by orchestrator and the
corresponding implementation and execution $`a_{t}^{\mathrm{exe}}`$. The
transition model $`\mathcal{T}`$ and observation model $`\Omega`$ define
$`s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t})`$ and
$`o_{t+1}\sim\Omega(\cdot\mid s_{t+1},a_{t})`$. We define the immediate
reward at step $`t`$ as the improvement in the best empirical
performance so far:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{t}:=\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})=\max_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{t+1}}r(e_{i})-\max_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}r(e_{i}),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\mathcal{V}_{t}`$ is the node set of the research graph at step
$`t`$, and $`r(e_{i})`$ denotes the empirical performance score obtained
from experimental results $`e_{i}`$, with larger values indicating
better performance.

#### Research World Model.

We introduce a research world model to estimate the value of candidate
research actions and optimize the research policy to enable
value-guided, pre-execution filtering and refining for more effective
exploration. Given the observation $`o_{t}`$ and an orchestrator action
$`a^{\mathrm{orc}}_{t}`$, the research world model
$`\mathcal{W}_{\phi}`$ estimates its expected cumulative reward,
integrating both the immediate yield of the action and the long-term
return from subsequent research under the remaining budget:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{V}_{t}(o_{t},a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{\tau=t}^{T-1}\mathcal{R}_{\tau}\,\middle|\,o_{t},a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right],\qquad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{\tau=t}^{T-1}\kappa_{\tau}\leq B_{t},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\kappa_{\tau}`$ is the research cost at step $`\tau`$ and $`T`$
is the terminal iteration. Using this assessment, the world model
optimizes candidate actions and guides their selection, shaping the
actions decided by research policy toward higher expected final
performance.

![Refer to caption](2610.13194v1/method.png)

Figure 2: The overall framework of JANUS. In each iteration, the
orchestrator generates new proposals, the world model scores and refines
them to guide selection, and the code executor implements and evaluates
them. Experimental feedback drives hierarchical skill abstraction,
exploration-strategy reflection, and coordination updates, enabling the
research agent and world model to co-evolve.

### 3.2 The Predict-Verify-Evolve Loop

Building on the evolving research graph, JANUS introduces the
Predict-Verify-Evolve loop to overcome the high cost and latency of
traditional feedback. As illustrated in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Research World Model. ‣ 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models"),
it comprises four key steps: expansion (by orchestrator), prediction and
refinement (by world model), implementation and execution (by code
executor), and co-evolution. The details of each step are described as
below.

#### Expansion.

At iteration $`t`$, the orchestrator expands the research space by
sampling actions $`\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}`$, where each action
first selects a parent node set $`\mathcal{U}_{t}^{(k)}`$ that provides
reusable knowledge and then generates a corresponding candidate proposal
$`x_{t}^{(k)}`$ by modifying, extending, or combining them:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}=\left\{a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc},(k)}=\left(\mathcal{U}_{t}^{(k)},x_{t}^{(k)}\right)\right\}_{k=1}^{N_{t}},\qquad a_{t}^{\mathrm{orc},(k)}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{orc}}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right),\qquad\mathcal{U}_{t}^{(k)}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}.
``` |  | (5) |

The expansion process $`\pi_{\mathrm{orc}}`$ is conditioned on the
current evolving graph $`\mathcal{G}_{t}`$, remaining budget $`B_{t}`$,
and available research skills $`\mathcal{H}_{t}`$ (detailed along with
its evolution mechanism in
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Experience-Driven Co-Evolution ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")),
and $`N_{t}`$ denotes the number of generated proposals. By connecting
these proposals to their parents as Open nodes, the research graph
systematically expands its exploration frontier.

#### Prediction and Refinement.

Drawing on experimental evidence in $`\mathcal{G}_{t}`$ and relevant
prediction skills from $`\mathcal{H}_{t}`$, the world model
$`\mathcal{W}_{\phi}`$ estimates the research value of each candidate
proposal $`x_{t}^{(k)}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{V}_{t}(x_{t}^{(k)})=\mathcal{W}_{\phi}\bigl(x_{t}^{(k)}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\bigr).
``` |  | (6) |

This assessment considers both immediate improvement and potential
future gains of candidate proposals within the remaining budget. Beyond
estimating research value, the world model debates with the orchestrator
via $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{wm}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{orc}}`$ to refine
proposals $`\mathcal{X}_{t}`$ and research values $`\hat{V}_{t}`$ when
necessary. Then the world model selects the proposal $`x_{t}^{\star}`$
with the highest estimated research value:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{\prime},\hat{V}_{t}^{\prime})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{wm}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{orc}}(\mathcal{X}_{t};\hat{V}_{t}),\qquad x_{t}^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{t}^{\prime}}\hat{V}_{t}^{\prime}(x).
``` |  | (7) |

The value estimates are recorded in the corresponding graph nodes, and
the selected proposal is passed to the executor.
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 World Model Guided Exploration Strategy ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")
details the assessment, refinement, and selection strategy.

#### Implementation and Execution.

Given the selected proposal $`x_{t}^{\star}`$, the code executor
produces an implementation $`y_{t}`$, executes it in the task
environment, and updates the remaining budgets:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{t}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{code}}(\cdot\mid x_{t}^{\star},\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}),\qquad e_{t}=\operatorname{Eval}(y_{t}),\qquad B_{t+1}=B_{t}-\kappa_{t}.
``` |  | (8) |

where $`e_{t}`$ denotes the empirical feedback obtained from the
environment, and $`\kappa_{t}`$ is the total research cost incurred
during this iteration. JANUS records $`y_{t}`$ and $`e_{t}`$ in the node
associated with $`x_{t}^{\star}`$. Therefore, the updated graph
$`\mathcal{G}_{t+1}`$ and budget form the next observation $`o_{t+1}`$
and state $`s_{t+1}`$.

#### Co-evolution.

JANUS reflects on the updated research graph $`\mathcal{G}_{t+1}`$,
examining how proposal design and code implementation contributed to the
experimental outcomes and reassessing the world model’s pre-execution
judgements in light of this evidence and related experiments.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{H}_{t+1}=\operatorname{Evolve}\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{t},\mathcal{G}_{t+1}\bigr).
``` |  | (9) |

Through continual learning from experimental experience, the research
agent improves proposal generation, and the world model refines its
research judgement, enabling their co-evolution throughout the research
process. The skill evolution mechanism is detailed in
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Experience-Driven Co-Evolution ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").

### 3.3 World Model Guided Exploration Strategy

The core challenge in open-ended research lies in balancing immediate
gains against long-term exploration. JANUS leverages its world model to
evaluate these complementary value drivers, dynamically adjusting their
weights based on recent progress and remaining budget. These priorities
govern proposal refinement and selection, steering research exploration
trajectory across iterations.

#### Research Priority Scoring.

The world model evaluates each proposal along three complementary
dimensions, with their formal definitions provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Formal Definitions of Research Priority Criteria ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models"):

- •
  Value improvement: expected immediate gain of empirical performance
  upon execution.
- •
  Information gain: expected uncertainty reduction about which proposal
  should be pursued next.
- •
  Exploration utility: expected utility of promising follow-up
  interventions unlocked by the proposal under the remaining budget.

Rather than assigning absolute scores independently, JANUS evaluates the
candidate proposals $`\mathcal{X}_{t}=\{x_{t}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_{t}}`$
through a sparse pairwise tournament. Let $`d`$ index one of the three
research criteria, $`d\in\{\mathrm{VI},\mathrm{IG},\mathrm{EV}\}`$,
corresponding to value improvement, information gain, and exploration
utility. For each compared pair $`(x_{t}^{(m)},x_{t}^{(n)})`$, the world
model predicts a soft preference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{t,d}^{(m,n)}=P_{\phi}\!\left(x_{t}^{(m)}\succ_{d}x_{t}^{(n)}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\rho_{t,d}^{(m,n)}`$ denotes the predicted probability that
$`x_{t}^{(m)}`$ is preferable to $`x_{t}^{(n)}`$ under criterion $`d`$.
To keep the tournament sparse, JANUS adaptively selects informative
proposal pairs, prioritizing those with high comparison uncertainty. Let
$`\mathcal{C}_{t}`$ denote the set of paired indices of proposals. For
each criterion $`d`$, JANUS estimates the latent Bradley-Terry score of
$`x_{t}^{(k)}`$, denoted as $`g_{t,d}(x_{t}^{(k)})`$, by fitting a soft
Bradley–Terry model [Bradley and Terry (1952)](#bib.bib3):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname*{min}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\mathrm{BCE}\!\left(\rho_{t,d}^{(m,n)},\sigma\!\left(g_{t,d}(x^{(m)})-g_{t,d}(x^{(n)})\right)\right),
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is the sigmoid function and $`\mathrm{BCE}`$
denotes binary cross-entropy. The fitted scores are then normalized to
guarantee $`g_{t,d}(x_{t}^{(k)})\in[0,1]`$.

#### Adaptive Exploration.

The world model adaptively steers the research exploration trajectory by
jointly adjusting research direction and optimizing candidate space.
Given the tournament-derived criterion scores, the research priority of
proposal $`x_{t}^{(k)}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{V}_{t}\!\left(x_{t}^{(k)}\right)=\frac{g_{t,\mathrm{VI}}\!\left(x_{t}^{(k)}\right)+\beta_{t}g_{t,\mathrm{IG}}\!\left(x_{t}^{(k)}\right)+\gamma_{t}g_{t,\mathrm{EV}}\!\left(x_{t}^{(k)}\right)}{1+\beta_{t}+\gamma_{t}},
``` |  | (12) |

where $`\beta_{t},\gamma_{t}\geq 0`$ are dynamically adjusted according
to recent research progress, the remaining budget and the corresponding
skills $`\mathcal{H}_{t}`$. These priorities further guide the
refinement process in
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In Prediction and Refinement. ‣ 3.2 The Predict-Verify-Evolve Loop ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").
The world model maintains a promising research frontier by dynamically
pruning low-value proposals according to these priorities. And when
necessary, particularly under sustained stagnation, it engages the
orchestrator in debate to refine promising proposals then re-evaluates
them.

### 3.4 Experience-Driven Co-Evolution

To efficiently leverage sparse feedback from costly experiments and
enable cross-task experience reuse, we introduce a hierarchical skill
abstraction for research agent, an exploration-strategy reflection
mechanism for world model, and an evolving coordination protocol for
their communication.

#### Hierarchical Experience-to-Skill Abstraction.

To enable structured knowledge transfer, the research agent organizes
raw execution logs into a three-level hierarchy: raw experiences
($`\mathscr{E}`$), intra-task skills ($`\mathcal{H}_{\text{intra}}`$),
and cross-task skills ($`\mathcal{H}_{\text{cross}}`$). Specifically,
raw successful and failed trajectories
$`\mathscr{E}_{k}=\mathcal{T}_{k}^{+}\cup\mathcal{T}_{k}^{-}`$ are
distilled into specialized knowledge (e.g., domain-specific error
mitigation) for task $`k`$, and then generalized into principles (e.g.,
hypothesis-driven ablation study) across tasks:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathscr{E}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}_{T}}\;\mathcal{H}_{\text{intra}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}_{G}}\mathcal{H}_{\text{cross}},\ \text{where }\mathcal{H}_{\text{intra}}^{(k)}=\mathcal{M}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_{k}^{+}\cup\mathcal{T}_{k}^{-}\right),\;\mathcal{H}_{\text{cross}}=\mathcal{M}_{G}\!\left(\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{H}_{\text{intra}}^{(k)}\right).
``` |  | (13) |

Here, $`\mathcal{M}_{T}`$ and $`\mathcal{M}_{G}`$ denote the
abstraction-and-update operators for intra-task and cross-task skills.

#### Exploration-Strategy Reflection Mechanism.

To mitigate prediction bias and refine exploration guidance, the world
model retrospectively examines its prior judgments against accumulated
experimental evidence. Given the execution experience
$`\mathscr{E}_{i}`$ of proposal $`x_{i}`$, JANUS derives prediction
skills $`\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pre}}`$ by reflecting on the predicted
value improvement of $`x_{i}`$ and the information gain and exploration
utility of its ancestors $`\mathrm{Anc}(x_{i})`$, and exploration skills
$`\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}`$ from recent adaptive exploration
strategies across the reflection window $`[t^{\prime},t]`$, tracking
$`\beta_{\tau}`$, $`\gamma_{\tau}`$, and the remaining budget
$`B_{\tau}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pre}}=\mathcal{M}_{P}\!\left(\mathscr{E}_{i},g_{\mathrm{VI}}(x_{i}),\left\{g_{d}(x_{j})\right\}_{x_{j}\in\mathrm{Anc}(x_{i})}^{d\in\{\mathrm{IG},\mathrm{EV}\}}\right),\ \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}=\mathcal{M}_{E}\!\left(\mathcal{G}_{t},\Big\{(\beta_{\tau},\gamma_{\tau},B_{\tau})\Big\}_{\tau=t^{\prime}}^{t}\right).
``` |  | (14) |

Here $`\mathcal{M}_{P}`$ and $`\mathcal{M}_{E}`$ denote
abstraction-and-update operators for prediction and exploration skills.

#### Evolving Coordination Protocol.

To improve collaboration between the research agent and world model,
JANUS further abstracts shared coordination skills
$`\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{coord}}`$ from previous debate and refinement.
Specifically, it reflects on when debate was invoked, how the candidate
proposals and their value estimates were revised, and whether these
revisions were supported by subsequent evidence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{coord}}=\mathcal{M}_{C}\!\left(\left\{(\mathcal{G}_{\tau},B_{\tau}),\,(\mathcal{X}_{\tau},\hat{V}_{\tau})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{wm}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{orc}}}(\mathcal{X}_{\tau}^{\prime},\hat{V}_{\tau}^{\prime}),\,\mathscr{E}_{\tau}\right\}_{\tau=t^{\prime}}^{t}\right).
``` |  | (15) |

Here, $`\mathcal{M}_{C}`$ denotes the abstraction-and-update operator
for coordination skills, and $`[t^{\prime},t]`$ denotes the recent
reflection window covering prior debate, refinement, and their
subsequent outcomes.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Benchmarks.

We primarily evaluate JANUS on MLS-Bench-Lite [Lyu et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib11), comprising 30 research tasks across 12 domains.
Each task requires agents to improve one targeted component of an ML
system and demonstrate the improvement generalizes across controlled
settings. To further examine JANUS’s capacity for end-to-end discovery
in real-world research, we consider three additional tasks: nanoGPT
training optimization from AutoResearch [Karpathy (2026)](#bib.bib23),
MinGPT attention kernel optimization from KernelBench [Ouyang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib10), and genotype-to-phenotype prediction task from real
world. Together, these tasks span algorithm optimization, system design,
and scientific discovery. Detailed task descriptions are provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Benchmarks ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").

#### Baselines.

We compare JANUS with strong baselines including AutoResearch [Karpathy
(2026)](#bib.bib23), AIDE [Jiang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib19),
MLEvolve [Du et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9), AIBuildAI [Zhang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib24), Arbor [Jin et al. (2026)](#bib.bib46), and
Codex [OpenAI (2025)](#bib.bib26). Each method is allowed to inspect
files, modify code within the permitted scope, and iteratively run
experiments until its budget is exhausted.

#### Metrics.

We report six metrics on MLS-Bench-Lite. Valid Sub. measures the
percentage of tasks whose final submissions successfully complete
evaluation and pass validity checks. Beat Any, Beat Median, and Beat All
measure the percentages of tasks that outperform at least one, the
median-performing, and all human baselines, respectively. Dev Score and
Test Score report the mean normalized task scores under the development
and test evaluation settings. For the three real research tasks, we
report validation bits per byte (Val. BPB) for nanoGPT, speedup over
PyTorch eager execution (Speedup) for MinGPT Causal Attention, and
macro-averaged Pearson correlation (Macro $`r`$) for
genotype-to-phenotype prediction. Detailed metric definitions are
provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Metrics ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").

#### Implementation details.

For each run, the agent is allocated a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. On
MLS-Bench-Lite, we allocate a 5-hour wall-clock budget per task for the
iterative research process. We also use the same initial setups, task
objectives, and evaluation protocols across all methods to ensure a fair
comparison. We primarily use DeepSeek-V4 Flash as the backbone,
supplemented by DeepSeek-V4 Pro and GPT-5.6 Sol. Further implementation
details are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Implementation Details ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").

### 4.3 Ablation Study

#### Adaptive exploration strategy.

We ablate adaptive proposal prioritization and refinement by comparing
JANUS with Exploitation-only, which prioritizes immediate improvement;
Exploration-only, which emphasizes exploration utility; and w/o
Refinement, which disables world-model-guided refinement of candidate
proposals. As shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Evolving Efficiency and Ceiling. ‣ 4.2 Performance Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models"),
both Exploitation-only and Exploration-only reduce performance across
the three tasks, indicating that a fixed research exploration strategy
is insufficient. The w/o Refinement variant makes less progress under
sustained stagnation, highlighting the importance of revising candidate
proposals to optimize the research frontier. Overall, the full framework
achieves the strongest final performance across all three tasks, showing
that adaptive priority weighting and candidate-space refinement provide
complementary benefits.

#### World model and co-evolution.

We further analyze the roles of the world model and co-evolution. Here,
w/o World Model removes the world model together with the evolution
mechanism, while w/o “component(s)” Evolve disables reflection and skill
summarization for them. (1) As shown in
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models"),
removing the world model leads to a substantial performance drop across
all tasks, demonstrating the importance of world-model guidance. (2)
Disabling the evolution of either component also degrades performance,
indicating that the two evolution processes are both necessary and
complementary. (3) Their effects are also qualitatively different:
without world-model evolution, progress is more likely to stall during
plateaus, whereas without research-agent evolution, improvement becomes
slower throughout the research process. Together, their co-evolution
sustains research progress by continuously turning feedback into better
guidance and research behavior.

### 4.4 Framework Analysis

#### Beyond baseline imitation.

Following MLS-Bench-Lite [Lyu et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib11), we examine
whether research gains primarily arise from imitating strong baselines.
As shown in
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")(a),
JANUS maintains strong performance even at low similarity-weighted
baseline quality, suggesting that its gains extend beyond direct
baseline imitation (details shown in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Analysis of Baseline Imitation ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")).

#### Domain generalization.

We evaluate whether research gains transfer to out-of-distribution (OOD)
settings across the three tasks. As shown in
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")(b),
JANUS achieves the highest final scores on ID and OOD settings, while
maintaining the smallest ID–OOD gap. This suggests that its research
gains generalize beyond the original evaluation distribution (further
details shown in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Out-of-Distribution Evaluation Setup and Analysis ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")).

#### Dynamic research strategy.

We analyze how the research strategy evolves throughout the nanoGPT
research process. We use GPT-5.6 Sol to score each proposal along three
dimensions—exploitation, information gain, and exploration (details
shown in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Analysis of Adaptive Research Strategies ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")).
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")(c)
shows four patterns. (1) Exploration is emphasized early, while
exploitation gradually increases as promising directions emerge. (2)
When performance plateaus, exploration rises and exploitation declines,
followed by improvement in best-so-far performance. (3) Increases in
information gain are followed by major performance breakthroughs,
indicating that resolving uncertainty helps drive subsequent progress.
(4) As the remaining budget decreases, exploitation increases while
exploration declines. Together, these patterns show a dynamic research
strategy throughout the research progress.

## 5 Conclusion

We present JANUS, a framework for long-horizon autonomous research that
unifies future outcome forecasting and past experience reflection
through a Predict-Verify-Evolve loop. Over an evolving research graph,
the world model guides proposal refinement and selection by balancing
value improvement, information gain, and exploration utility. JANUS
distills experimental feedback into research agent, world-model, and
coordination skills, driving system-wide co-evolution. Evaluations on
MLS-Bench-Lite and three real-world research tasks demonstrate that
JANUS outperforms strong baselines, achieving greater evolving
efficiency and a higher evolving ceiling.

## Appendix A Formal Definitions of Research Priority Criteria

This section provides the formal definitions of the three research
criteria introduced in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3.SSS0.Px1 "Research Priority Scoring. ‣ 3.3 World Model Guided Exploration Strategy ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").
Let

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{I}_{t}=\left(\mathcal{G}_{t},B_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)
       ```                                                                 |     |

denote the current research context. For a candidate proposal
$`x\in\mathcal{X}_{t}`$, the world model predicts possible empirical
feedback according to $`p_{\phi}(e\mid\mathcal{I}_{t},x)`$, where $`e`$
denotes the feedback that would be returned by the environment after
implementing and evaluating $`x`$.

#### Value improvement.

Let $`R^{\star}(\mathcal{G})`$ denote the best empirical performance
among all evaluated solutions contained in research graph
$`\mathcal{G}`$. Value improvement captures the expected increase in the
best empirical performance after executing a proposal:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{VI}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{e\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{I}_{t},x)}\left[R^{\star}\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{t+1}^{(x,e)}\right)-R^{\star}(\mathcal{G}_{t})\right],
``` |  | (16) |

where $`\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{t+1}^{(x,e)}`$ denotes the hypothetical
successor graph after executing $`x`$ and receiving feedback $`e`$. This
criterion favors proposals that are likely to directly improve the
current best solution.

#### Information gain.

A proposal can be valuable even without directly improving the incumbent
if its outcome helps resolve uncertainty that affects subsequent
research. Let $`Z_{t}`$ denote the research direction that should be
pursued next under the world model’s current belief. We define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{IG}_{t}(x)=\mathrm{H}\!\left(Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{e\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{I}_{t},x)}\left[\mathrm{H}\!\left(Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{I}_{t},x,e\right)\right],
``` |  | (17) |

where $`\mathrm{H}(\cdot)`$ denotes entropy under the world model’s
belief. For instance, a controlled experiment may provide little
immediate performance gain but distinguish between competing hypotheses,
thereby clarifying which mechanism or research direction should be
pursued next.

#### Exploration utility.

Beyond clarifying existing choices, a proposal may create new
opportunities for subsequent research. Let $`\mathcal{D}_{t}(x)`$ denote
the follow-up proposals already accessible after selecting $`x`$, and
let $`\mathcal{N}_{t}(x,e)`$ denote additional proposals enabled by the
feedback obtained from executing $`x`$. We define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{EV}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{e\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{I}_{t},x)}\left[Q\!\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}(x)\cup\mathcal{N}_{t}(x,e);\mathcal{I}_{t}^{x,e}\right)-Q\!\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}(x);\mathcal{I}_{t}^{x,e}\right)\right],
``` |  | (18) |

where $`\mathcal{I}_{t}^{x,e}`$ denotes the hypothetical research
context after incorporating the feedback from $`x`$ and deducting its
resource cost, and $`Q(\mathcal{D};\mathcal{I})`$ denotes the estimated
future improvement attainable by pursuing proposals in $`\mathcal{D}`$
under the remaining budget specified by $`\mathcal{I}`$. For instance,
an experiment may offer limited immediate improvement but validate a new
mechanism, representation, or implementation strategy that enables a
broader family of promising follow-up interventions.

The three criteria therefore capture complementary sources of research
value: direct performance improvement, reduction of uncertainty over
future decisions, and expansion of the subsequent research space. The
world model uses these formal definitions as the scoring criteria for
assessing each candidate proposal along value improvement, information
gain, and exploration utility. They provide the semantic basis for the
soft pairwise judgments used by the tournament-based scoring mechanism
in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3.SSS0.Px1 "Research Priority Scoring. ‣ 3.3 World Model Guided Exploration Strategy ‣ 3 The JANUS Framework ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models").

## Appendix B Baselines

We compare our framework with AutoResearch [Karpathy
(2026)](#bib.bib23), AIDE [Jiang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib19),
MLEvolve [Du et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9), AIBuildAI [Zhang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib24), Arbor [Jin et al. (2026)](#bib.bib46), and
Codex [OpenAI (2025)](#bib.bib26), using the configurations reported in
the main text. For a fair comparison, methods evaluated on the same task
receive identical initial files, task objectives, evaluator access, and
resource budgets. Each method may inspect the provided workspace, modify
code within the permitted scope, and execute experiments iteratively
until its budget is exhausted. When comparing research frameworks under
a common backbone, we use the same underlying language model so that
performance differences can be attributed to the framework rather than
model capability.

The original implementations of these systems were developed for
different execution environments and task interfaces. We therefore adapt
their public codebases to both MLS-Bench-Lite and our three research
tasks. These adaptations are restricted to task-facing infrastructure,
including input/output adapters, workspace initialization, evaluator
bindings, dependency wrappers, and stopping conditions. We preserve each
method’s original research-loop design, agent-role decomposition,
proposal and revision procedures, memory mechanism, and candidate
selection policy. In particular, we do not introduce components from our
framework, task-specific heuristics, or additional scientific knowledge
into the baselines. Thus, the adapted implementations retain the
framework design and algorithmic ideas of the original methods while
exposing all systems to a common experimental interface.

problems covering language-model training, GPU-system optimization, and
scientific prediction.

For nanoGPT training optimization, adapted from AutoResearch [Karpathy
(2026)](#bib.bib23), the agent improves a compact single-GPU GPT
pretraining system. The editable file contains the model architecture,
attention and MLP blocks, optimizer construction, learning-rate
schedules, batching strategy, and training loop, while the data
preparation, tokenizer, validation split, and metric implementation
remain fixed. Training uses a BPE-tokenized text corpus and is evaluated
on a pinned validation shard. The objective is to obtain the lowest
validation bits per byte (BPB) within a fixed training-time budget. BPB
measures the negative log-likelihood assigned to the validation text per
byte rather than per token, making comparisons less sensitive to
tokenization and vocabulary size.

For MinGPT Causal Attention optimization, we use KernelBench Level 3,
Task 43 [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10). The reference operator is a
complete MinGPT masked multi-head self-attention layer. It applies a
joint linear projection to construct queries, keys, and values, reshapes
them into attention heads, computes scaled query–key products, applies a
lower-triangular causal mask and softmax, aggregates the values, and
finally applies an output projection. The agent must implement a drop-in
ModelNew with the same constructor and forward semantics. Candidate
implementations may replace the PyTorch computation with fused PyTorch
operators, compilation strategies, Triton kernels, or custom CUDA code,
but must preserve the numerical output. The objective is to maximize
execution speedup over the PyTorch eager reference after passing all
correctness checks.

For genotype-to-phenotype (G2P) prediction, the input for a maize hybrid
consists of its SNP dosage sequence together with the environmental
conditions under which it is grown. The benchmark contains 4,269
hybrids, 7,577 MAF/LD-pruned SNP markers, and 185 site–year
environments. Environmental inputs are daily time series describing
factors such as mean, maximum, and minimum temperature, dew-point
temperature, solar and photosynthetically active radiation,
precipitation, evapotranspiration, humidity, soil wetness, frost, wind
speed, and vapor-pressure deficit. A prediction is made for each
hybrid–environment pair, allowing the model to capture genetic effects,
environmental effects, and genotype–environment interactions.

The output covers eight agronomic traits: plant height, ear height,
grain moisture, yield in Mg/ha, yield per plant, root lodging, stalk
lodging, and test weight. Equivalently, for a hybrid–environment input
$`(g,e)`$, the prediction can be written as

|     |                                                                |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       f(g,e)=\bigl(\widehat{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\widehat{y}^{(8)}\bigr).
       ```                                                             |     |

Because the traits have different physical units and ranges, performance
is computed independently for each trait and then macro-averaged. This
prevents traits with more observations or larger numerical scales from
dominating the final score.

### C.2 MLS-Bench-Lite.

Our main benchmark evaluation uses MLS-Bench-Lite [Lyu et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib11), a challenging 30-task subset of MLS-Bench covering
all 12 research domains. The domains include language models,
reinforcement learning, robotics, vision and generation, deep learning,
ML systems and efficient ML, AI for science, time-series forecasting,
structured and causal reasoning, trustworthy learning, classical and
adaptive learning, and optimization and theory. Representative problems
include pretraining-optimizer design, diffusion-model sampling,
robot-policy learning, post-training quantization, sparse attention,
time-series imputation, causal discovery, clustering, privacy-preserving
training, and stochastic optimization.

Each task instantiates a community-recognized research question as an
executable problem. An agent receives a task description, a controlled
code scaffold, strong reproduced baselines, designated editable regions,
and an evaluation interface. Training protocols and evaluators are
protected, and tasks involving model components additionally impose
capacity constraints. A submission is evaluated over multiple datasets,
environments, model scales, or random seeds, rather than on a single
development instance. The benchmark therefore measures whether an agent
can produce an improvement in the targeted algorithmic component that
remains effective across the prescribed evaluation settings.

## Appendix D Metrics

### D.1 MLS-Bench-Lite Metrics

#### Score normalization.

We follow the normalization and within-task aggregation protocol of
MLS-Bench [Lyu et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib11). Each raw metric is first
transformed into a higher-is-better quantity $`z`$ using the
task-specified preprocessing and metric direction. Let
$`z_{\mathrm{floor}}`$ and $`z_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ denote the corresponding
worst and best reproduced baseline values. The normalization assigns
zero to the worst-baseline anchor and calibrates the best-baseline
anchor to $`0.5`$.

For a metric with a finite theoretical upper bound
$`z_{\mathrm{bound}}`$, the normalized score is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(z)=\left[\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{z-z_{\mathrm{floor}}}{z_{\mathrm{bound}}-z_{\mathrm{floor}}},0,1\right)\right]^{\gamma},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\log(0.5)}{\log\left(\frac{z_{\mathrm{ref}}-z_{\mathrm{floor}}}{z_{\mathrm{bound}}-z_{\mathrm{floor}}}\right)}.
``` |  |

For metrics without a finite theoretical upper bound, the normalized
score uses a calibrated sigmoid:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(z)=\max\left\{0,\,2\sigma\left(\frac{z-z_{\mathrm{floor}}}{\lambda}\right)-1\right\},\qquad\lambda=\frac{z_{\mathrm{ref}}-z_{\mathrm{floor}}}{\log 3},
``` |  |

where $`\sigma`$ is the logistic sigmoid function.

#### Within-task aggregation.

For task $`t`$, evaluation setting $`k`$ contains normalized metric
scores $`s_{t,k,i}`$ with task-specified weights $`w_{t,k,i}`$. The
setting score is their weighted arithmetic mean:

|     |                                                               |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       S_{t,k}=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{t,k,i}s_{t,k,i}}{\sum_{i}w_{t,k,i}}.
       ```                                                            |     |

The task score is obtained by taking the geometric mean across its
$`K_{t}`$ evaluation settings:

|     |                                                          |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       S_{t}=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K_{t}}S_{t,k}\right)^{1/K_{t}}.
       ```                                                       |     |

This aggregation penalizes uneven performance across settings. Invalid
submissions receive a task score of zero.

#### Development and test scores.

We construct development and test splits for our experiments. Dev Score
and Test Score evaluate the final submission on these respective splits
using the normalization and within-task aggregation described above. Let
$`S_{t,\mathrm{dev}}^{(m)}`$ and $`S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(m)}`$ denote
the resulting task scores for method $`m`$. We average over all 30 tasks
and report scores on a $`0`$–$`100`$ scale:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{DevScore}^{(m)}=\frac{100}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}S_{t,\mathrm{dev}}^{(m)},\qquad\operatorname{TestScore}^{(m)}=\frac{100}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(m)}.
``` |  |

#### Submission validity.

Let $`v_{t}^{(m)}`$ indicate whether the final submission of method
$`m`$ on task $`t`$ successfully completes test evaluation and satisfies
the benchmark’s validity requirements. The valid-submission rate is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{ValidSub}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}v_{t}^{(m)}.
``` |  |

#### Comparison with human baselines.

All comparisons with human baselines use test results. For task $`t`$,
let $`\mathcal{B}_{t}`$ denote the set of provided human baselines, each
evaluated on the same test split and scored using the same procedure as
the agent submission. We define the comparison thresholds as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau_{t}^{\mathrm{Any}}=\min_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(b)},\qquad\tau_{t}^{\mathrm{Median}}=\operatorname{median}_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(b)},\qquad\tau_{t}^{\mathrm{All}}=\max_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(b)}.
``` |  |

Beat Any, Beat Median, and Beat All report the percentages of tasks on
which a valid final submission strictly exceeds the corresponding
threshold:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Beat}_{c}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}v_{t}^{(m)}\mathbf{1}\!\left[S_{t,\mathrm{test}}^{(m)}>\tau_{t}^{c}\right],\qquad c\in\{\mathrm{Any},\mathrm{Median},\mathrm{All}\}.
``` |  |

Here, $`\mathbf{1}[\cdot]`$ is the indicator function. All rates are
reported as percentages, with all 30 tasks included in the denominator.
Invalid submissions and ties do not count as wins.

### D.2 Evaluation on the Three Research Tasks

We first define the task metrics independently of the evaluation
setting.

#### Validation bits per byte.

Let $`y_{i}`$ be the $`i`$-th validation token, let

|     |                                             |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \ell_{i}=-\log p_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})
       ```                                          |     |

be its negative log-likelihood in nats, and let $`b_{i}`$ be the number
of bytes represented by that token. Tokens with zero byte length, such
as special control tokens, are excluded. Validation BPB is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{BPB}=\frac{\sum_{i:b_{i}>0}\ell_{i}}{\log 2\sum_{i:b_{i}>0}b_{i}}.
``` |  |

It is the average number of bits required to encode one byte of
validation text under the trained model, so lower values indicate better
language modeling.

#### Kernel speedup.

Let $`\bar{t}_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ and $`\bar{t}_{\mathrm{cand}}`$ denote
the mean CUDA-event runtimes of the eager reference and candidate
implementation, respectively. The KernelBench metric is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Speedup}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\bar{t}_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\bar{t}_{\mathrm{cand}}},&\text{if all correctness checks pass},\\[8.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

A value greater than one indicates that the candidate is faster than the
reference. Failed compilation, runtime errors, shape mismatches, or
numerical correctness failures receive zero.

#### Macro Pearson correlation.

For trait $`j`$, let $`y_{j,n}`$ and $`\widehat{y}_{j,n}`$ be the
ground-truth and predicted phenotypes of evaluation observation $`n`$.
Its Pearson correlation is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{j}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{j}}(y_{j,n}-\bar{y}_{j})(\widehat{y}_{j,n}-\bar{\widehat{y}}_{j})}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{j}}(y_{j,n}-\bar{y}_{j})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{j}}(\widehat{y}_{j,n}-\bar{\widehat{y}}_{j})^{2}}}.
``` |  |

The G2P score is the unweighted mean over the eight traits:

|     |                                                             |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{MacroPearson}=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{8}r_{j}.
       ```                                                          |     |

Higher values indicate that the predictions better preserve phenotype
variation across hybrid–environment observations.

#### Sample-efficiency speedup.

We quantify search efficiency by comparing the number of experiments
required by Janus and a competing method to reach the same performance
threshold. Let $`y_{m,t}(k)`$ denote the score obtained by method $`m`$
on task $`t`$ after experiment $`k`$, where $`k=0`$ corresponds to the
initial solution. To accommodate metrics with different optimization
directions, we define

|     |                                                             |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d_{t}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if lower scores are better},\\
       +1,&\text{if higher scores are better}.\end{cases}
       ```                                                          |     |

The direction-aligned best-so-far trajectory is

|     |                                                 |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       F_{m,t}(k)=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}d_{t}y_{m,t}(j).
       ```                                              |     |

An unsuccessful experiment consumes one experiment but does not update
$`F_{m,t}`$. For a direction-aligned performance threshold $`u`$, the
first-hitting time of method $`m`$ is

|     |                                                      |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tau_{m,t}(u)=\min\left\{k:F_{m,t}(k)\geq u\right\}.
       ```                                                   |     |

For Janus $`J`$ and baseline $`b`$, we evaluate thresholds over their
jointly reachable interval

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{U}_{t,b}=\left(u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{lo}},u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{hi}}\right],
``` |  |

where

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{lo}}=\max\{F_{J,t}(0),F_{b,t}(0)\},\qquad u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{hi}}=\min\{F_{J,t}(N_{t}),F_{b,t}(N_{t})\}.
``` |  |

The lower endpoint is excluded because both methods may have zero
hitting time at the initial score. The threshold-wise sample-efficiency
speedup is

|     |                                                    |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \rho_{t,b}(u)=\frac{\tau_{b,t}(u)}{\tau_{J,t}(u)}.
       ```                                                 |     |

Thus, $`\rho_{t,b}(u)>1`$ indicates that the baseline requires more
experiments than Janus to reach threshold $`u`$. The *average speedup*
reported under *Avg.* in Table 2 is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Avg}_{t,b}=\frac{1}{u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{hi}}-u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{lo}}}\int_{u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{lo}}}^{u_{t,b}^{\mathrm{hi}}}\frac{\tau_{b,t}(u)}{\tau_{J,t}(u)}\,\mathrm{d}u.
``` |  |

This quantity averages the experiment-count ratio uniformly over all
jointly reachable performance thresholds, rather than over experiment
indices. The *maximum speedup* reported under *Max.* is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Max}_{t,b}=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{t,b}}\frac{\tau_{b,t}(u)}{\tau_{J,t}(u)}.
``` |  |

The essential supremum excludes ratios that occur only at an isolated,
zero-width threshold.

## Appendix E Implementation Details

#### Common setup.

Each experimental run is assigned a single NVIDIA A100 PCIe GPU with
40 GB of memory. The primary comparisons use DeepSeek-V4-Flash as the
common language model backbone; experiments with the other backbones
reported in the main text serve as supplementary robustness comparisons.
Within each common-backbone comparison, we keep the model endpoint,
reasoning configuration, task prompt, initial workspace, action
permissions, evaluator, and research budget fixed. Different methods are
not allowed to share trajectories, intermediate candidates, evaluator
outputs, or memories.

All evaluations are performed by immutable external harnesses. A
candidate is accepted only if it modifies files inside the declared
editable surface and completes without violating the task-specific
resource or correctness constraints. Compilation failures, timeouts,
invalid outputs, and non-finite metrics are recorded as failed
experiments rather than silently discarded.

#### MLS-Bench-Lite.

Each agent is given a 5-hour wall-clock research budget per task. Since
our experiments use A100 GPUs, we relax the default per-task evaluator
timeouts to accommodate hardware-dependent differences in execution
speed relative to the H100 setup reported in MLS-Bench [Lyu et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib11). This adjustment allows the prescribed evaluation
workloads to complete without premature termination caused by slower
execution on our hardware. The same timeout policy is applied to all
compared methods, while the agent’s research budget remains unchanged.
Under the fixed wall-clock budget, the world model can select top-ranked
proposals for parallel execution on the allocated GPU when computational
resources permit, improving the utilization of the available research
time. To further ensure comparability across methods and backbones, we
predefine and hold fixed a common validation split for each task, rather
than allowing individual agents to construct their own splits, so that
differences in validation performance more directly reflect their
ability to optimize under the same feedback.

We retain the benchmark’s task-specific validity constraints. For tasks
involving editable model components, the original parameter-budget
checks remain enforced, and submissions exceeding the prescribed
capacity ceiling are rejected. Edits remain restricted to the designated
component, and shared training-protocol settings, such as training
epochs and batch size, remain within their protected ranges.
Method-defining hyperparameters are editable only where permitted by the
task. The evaluation data, settings, seed policies, correctness checks,
and scoring procedures are also preserved. The additional evaluator
runtime therefore accommodates the original workload while maintaining
the benchmark’s controls on model capacity and permissible
modifications.

#### nanoGPT Training.

Our experiments use a fixed training budget of 1,200 seconds per
candidate, excluding startup, compilation, and the initial warm-up
iterations. The sequence length is 2,048, and the tokenizer vocabulary
contains 8,192 entries. The validation set is the pinned final shard of
the ClimbMix-400B-Shuffle corpus, with BPB evaluated over 20,971,520
validation tokens. Training and validation use the same fixed tokenizer,
dataloader, document-packing procedure, and validation shard for all
methods. Mixed-precision training uses BF16 autocasting, while metric
accumulation is performed by the protected evaluation routine. Only
train.py is editable; the data preparation and evaluate_bpb
implementations are read-only.

#### MinGPT Causal Attention Optimization.

For the MinGPT Causal Attention optimization task from KernelBench
Level 3, Task 43, the official workload uses a batch size $`B`$ of 128,
a sequence length $`T`$ of 512, and an embedding dimension $`C`$ of 768.
The model has eight attention heads, supports a maximum sequence length
of 1,024, and uses zero attention and residual dropout. Inputs and
outputs are FP32 tensors. Parameters are initialized deterministically,
and correctness is checked on five freshly generated inputs using fixed
seeds. On every trial, the candidate output must match the reference
tensor shape and pass an elementwise closeness check with absolute and
relative tolerances both set to 0.0001.

Performance is measured with CUDA events after three warm-up executions.
We clear the allocator cache and perturb the L2-cache state before timed
executions, discard the first timing observation, and report the mean of
100 retained trials. The clean eager-reference latency is frozen at
$`32.6`$ ms and is used as the numerator of the speedup score,
preventing a candidate from altering its own reference timing through
global backend flags. Only solution.py may be modified, and it must
expose a top-level ModelNew class compatible with the reference
constructor.

#### Genotype-to-Phenotype.

The genotype-to-phenotype (G2P) prediction evaluator exposes a
phenotype-independent genotype matrix containing 7,577 single-nucleotide
polymorphism (SNP) dosages per hybrid and two fixed representations of
each environment: a basic statistical summary and a richer temporal
summary of the daily weather sequence. The latter includes global
statistics, quantiles, temporal trends, and stage-wise summaries. The
split is shared across all eight traits so that multi-trait models
cannot obtain information through inconsistent trait-specific
partitions.

For the default strict-M3 protocol, hybrids and environments are
partitioned into disjoint training, validation, and test groups with a
target observation ratio of $`70\%/10\%/20\%`$. The resulting entity
partitions contain $`2{,}232/844/1{,}193`$ hybrids and $`97/37/51`$
environments, respectively. The evaluator withholds phenotype targets
for all evaluation rows. Only train.py is editable, and all
preprocessing influenced by labels or sample distributions must be
fitted from the supplied training pool. Each candidate evaluation has a
900-second timeout. The principal score is macro Pearson; per-trait
Pearson, Spearman correlation, and within-environment Pearson
correlation are retained only as diagnostic statistics.

## Appendix F Out-of-Distribution Evaluation Setup and Analysis

We define one controlled distribution shift for each of the three
research tasks. The OOD settings are specified before evaluating the
final candidates. Each shifted setting retains the original evaluator
and primary metric while changing one scientifically meaningful axis.

#### Kernel workload shift.

The default attention workload uses a batch size $`B`$ of 128 and a
sequence length $`T`$ of 512. In the shifted setting, we reduce the
batch size to 64 and increase the sequence length to 1,024. The
embedding dimension $`C`$ remains 768, with eight attention heads, FP32
precision, and unchanged attention semantics. Both settings contain
65,536 input tokens per batch, but the longer sequences increase the
quadratic attention workload and alter the balance among projection,
attention, memory, and kernel-launch costs. Correctness is checked on
five inputs with absolute and relative tolerances both set to 0.0001. We
then perform three warm-up runs followed by 100 CUDA-event timing
trials. The eager reference latency in this setting is 45.9 ms.

#### Cross-corpus transfer.

For nanoGPT, we replace the original ClimbMix corpus with
TinyStories [Eldan and Li (2023)](#bib.bib8). We construct fixed,
document-disjoint training and evaluation partitions for the new corpus.
Each discovered method is frozen and retrained from scratch on the new
training partition, without additional method search or hyperparameter
tuning. The 1,200-second training budget, original tokenizer,
2,048-token context length, hardware, random-seed policy, and BPB metric
remain unchanged. We evaluate the initial and final methods, together
with the unmodified baseline, under this shared setting to assess
whether improvements found on the original corpus transfer to a
different text distribution.

#### Cross-variety transfer.

For G2P, we evaluate method transfer to a separate cohort of maize
hybrids excluded from the original research process. We retain each
discovered method’s modeling design and hyperparameters, retrain its
parameters on the new cohort’s training partition, and evaluate it on a
test partition containing disjoint hybrid identities. The SNP and
daily-weather feature definitions, eight target traits, environmental
evaluation protocol, training budget, and macro-Pearson metric remain
unchanged. The initial and final methods and the unmodified baseline are
evaluated on the same partitions. This setting examines whether the
discovered modeling improvements remain effective across different
hybrid cohorts without further research or tuning on the target cohort.

#### Cross-task score calibration.

Because the native evaluation functionals of the three tasks differ in
scale, orientation, and physical interpretation, we first transform each
raw measurement into a task-oriented gain and then calibrate that gain
against a fixed reference scale. Let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}(x)\coloneqq\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\,|z-x|=\max\{0,x\}
``` |  |

denote projection onto the non-negative real line. For a task
$`\tau\in\mathcal{T}=\{\mathrm{kernel},\mathrm{nanoGPT},\mathrm{G2P}\}`$,
we define the normalized score functional

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}\bigl(\mathsf{M}_{\tau};\mathsf{M}^{(0)}_{\tau}\bigr)\coloneqq 100\,\frac{\Pi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\left(\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\bigl(\mathsf{M}_{\tau};\mathsf{M}^{(0)}_{\tau}\bigr)\right)}{\Lambda_{\tau}},
``` |  |

where $`\mathsf{M}_{\tau}`$ is the candidate’s raw metric,
$`\mathsf{M}^{(0)}_{\tau}`$ is the corresponding setting-specific
reference metric, $`\mathcal{G}_{\tau}`$ is a direction-aware gain
transformation, and $`\Lambda_{\tau}>0`$ is a task-specific calibration
constant.

The gain transformations are instantiated as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\bigl(\mathsf{M}_{\tau};\mathsf{M}^{(0)}_{\tau}\bigr)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{t_{0}}{t},&\tau=\mathrm{kernel},\\[11.00008pt]
b_{0}-b,&\tau=\mathrm{nanoGPT},\\[6.00006pt]
r-r_{0},&\tau=\mathrm{G2P},\end{cases}
``` |  |

where $`t_{0}/t`$ is the candidate’s speedup relative to the
setting-specific reference runtime, $`b_{0}-b`$ is its absolute
reduction in validation BPB,, and $`r-r_{0}`$ is its absolute
improvement in macro Pearson correlation.

The calibration constants are fixed once and shared across all methods,
optimization stages, and evaluation distributions. Consequently, a
four-fold KernelBench speedup, an absolute nanoGPT BPB reduction of
$`0.05`$, and an absolute G2P macro-Pearson improvement of $`0.15`$ each
define the task-specific 100-point reference level. The projection
suppresses negative directional gains, while no upper saturation is
applied; hence scores greater than 100 remain admissible whenever a
method surpasses the corresponding calibration target.

Finally, the cross-task aggregate is defined as the expectation under
the uniform measure over the task index set:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{aggregate}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{T})}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}\right]=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}.
``` |  |

This calibration places the heterogeneous task metrics on commensurate
numerical scales, thereby making their uniformly weighted arithmetic
average meaningful despite differences in their native units and
optimization directions.

#### OOD generalization analysis.

Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")(b)
compares the aggregate ID and OOD scores of the initial and final
research solutions produced by each method. The OOD settings are defined
independently of the research process, and the discovered methods are
evaluated under the corresponding shifts without further research or
tuning. Therefore, movement from the initial to the final point reflects
whether improvements obtained during research remain effective beyond
the original optimization setting.

JANUS exhibits the strongest transfer among the compared methods. Its
final solution reaches the highest aggregate score under both ID and OOD
evaluation, showing that the improvements discovered during research are
not confined to the distribution on which they were developed.
Importantly, its final point remains close to the diagonal, indicating
that the gain achieved on the original tasks is largely preserved after
distribution shift. In contrast, several baselines show a larger
separation between their final ID and OOD scores, suggesting that part
of their research-time improvement is more sensitive to changes in
workload, corpus, or evaluation cohort.

The same behavior is particularly meaningful because the three OOD
settings exercise different forms of transfer: a workload shift for the
attention kernel, cross-corpus transfer for nanoGPT, and cross-variety
transfer for G2P. Thus, JANUS’s robustness does not depend on a single
type of distribution shift. Together, these results indicate that JANUS
tends to discover research solutions whose improvements persist under
changes to the evaluation distribution, rather than narrowly optimizing
for the original research setting.

## Appendix G Analysis of Baseline Imitation

#### Similarity-weighted baseline performance.

We further analyze whether the performance obtained by each research
agent can be explained by reproducing solutions from strong
human-designed baselines. For each evaluated submission, we measure its
code similarity to every baseline $`i`$ using 1/3-gram Jaccard
similarity, denoted by $`s_{i}`$. Let $`\beta_{i}`$ denote the
performance of baseline $`i`$. We define the similarity-weighted
baseline performance as

|     |                                                 |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       q=\frac{\sum_{i}s_{i}\beta_{i}}{\sum_{i}s_{i}}.
       ```                                              |     | (19) |

Intuitively, $`q`$ is high when a submission more closely resembles
high-performing baselines, and low when its similarity is concentrated
on weaker baselines or is less aligned with the strongest known
solutions. To make the quantities comparable across tasks, both $`q`$
and the corresponding agent performance are normalized by the best
baseline performance of that task.

For each research agent, we fit the relationship between its normalized
performance and similarity-weighted baseline performance across
evaluated submissions, as shown in

## Appendix H Analysis of Adaptive Research Strategies

#### Trajectory and assessment.

We analyze all selected candidates with completed formal evaluations in
the nanoGPT trajectory, ordered by their actual execution sequence. For
each candidate $`t`$, we recover its proposal text $`h_{t}`$ as recorded
when it was proposed. We use GPT-5.6 Sol as an LLM judge to assess the
proposal’s research emphasis under a shared rubric. The assessment
excludes the candidate’s own evaluation outcome and all subsequent
results. References to earlier experiments already present in the
proposal are retained, as they describe the evidence available when the
proposal was formulated. The analysis therefore characterizes the stated
intent of selected proposals independently of their eventual success.

#### Strategy dimensions.

We characterize each proposal along three non-exclusive dimensions.
Exploitation measures how strongly it builds on previously supported
mechanisms to pursue immediate improvement, such as refining a promising
configuration or combining effective modifications. Information gain
measures its emphasis on reducing uncertainty about which proposal or
research direction should be pursued next, through controlled
comparisons, replication, or mechanism isolation. Exploration measures
its emphasis on investigating unverified mechanisms or alternative
approaches that may open promising directions for subsequent research.
The resulting scores are denoted by $`X_{t},I_{t},E_{t}\in[0,1]`$. They
need not sum to one, since a proposal can pursue several objectives
simultaneously. For example, testing a new mechanism can both reduce
uncertainty about its effectiveness and reveal promising follow-up
directions.

#### Temporal smoothing.

To reveal changes in research emphasis while reducing
experiment-to-experiment fluctuations, we smooth each strategy dimension
using a centered seven-experiment moving average. Let $`s_{t,d}`$ denote
the original score at experiment $`t`$ for dimension $`d\in\{X,I,E\}`$.
The smoothed score is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\overline{s}_{t,d}=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{k=-3}^{3}s_{\pi(t+k),d},\qquad\pi(j)=\min\{N,\max\{1,j\}\},
``` |  |

where $`N=50`$. The index mapping repeats endpoint values near the
trajectory boundaries. In
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ World model and co-evolution. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Janus: Enhancing Long-Horizon AutonomousResearch with Co-Evolving World Models")(c),
faint colored lines and points show the original per-proposal scores,
while thick curves show their moving averages.

#### Normalized performance trace.

The purple staircase provides experimental context by showing the
cumulative improvement in validation BPB. Let $`b_{0}`$ denote the
initial baseline BPB and $`b_{t}`$ the evaluation result of candidate
$`t`$. Since lower BPB is better, the incumbent performance is

|     |                                                 |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       b_{t}^{\star}=\min\{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}.
       ```                                              |     |

We normalize its improvement relative to the total improvement achieved
within the displayed trajectory:

|     |                                                        |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{t}=\frac{b_{0}-b_{t}^{\star}}{b_{0}-b_{N}^{\star}}.
       ```                                                     |     |

This maps the initial baseline to zero and the final incumbent to one.
````
