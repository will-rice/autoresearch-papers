###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to automate AI
research, including language-model (LM) development, offering a
potential path toward recursive self-improvement. However, turning
experimental feedback into a learned ability to propose effective
improvements across models faces two challenges. First, training such an
ability with real feedback is expensive: reinforcement learning (RL)
requires evaluating tens of thousands of proposals, each potentially
requiring a full model-training run. Even at ten GPU-hours per run,
reward collection alone would consume hundreds of thousands of
GPU-hours, motivating a cheaper surrogate. Second, surrogate feedback
can become unreliable as policy optimization favors overestimated
proposals when the proposal distribution shifts. To address these
challenges, we propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate
effective LM improvement proposals. To provide scalable feedback, we use
a research world model (RWM), a frozen LLM that predicts proposal gains
from real experimental records provided in context, as the reward model
for RL. To maintain feedback reliability, we periodically execute
current-policy proposals ranked highly by the RWM and add their measured
outcomes to its context. Across eight autoregressive and diffusion LM
development environments, real-execution evaluations on held-out tasks
show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform GPT-6-sol, even when
GPT-6-sol is given 300 real experimental records in context, while
requiring 33% lower data-generation cost. LM2LM also generalizes to a
model family unseen during training.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) [Radford et al. (2018)](#bib.bib37);
[Radford et al. (2019)](#bib.bib38); [Touvron et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39)
are increasingly being adopted as agents for automated AI research
(AutoResearch) [Karpathy (2026)](#bib.bib42), where they propose ideas,
implement changes, and execute experiments [Si et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib34); [Novikov et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43). A promising
application is language-model development (LM development), where agents
explore architectural, optimization, and training choices and measure
their effects on model performance [Chi et al. (2026)](#bib.bib40);
[Yang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib41). These experiments generate feedback
that could help LLMs learn how to improve language models. Developing
this capability could accelerate model development and support progress
toward recursive self-improvement (RSI) [Chi et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib40) (Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(a)).

![Refer to caption](2610.23344v1/figures/fig_intro_v4_1.png)

Figure 1: Overview of automated LM development.

Despite these advances, deciding what to try, i.e., _research judgment_,
remains a bottleneck for LLM-based research agents. Agents can implement
and execute complex experiments, yet still struggle to identify changes
that yield meaningful improvements [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib36); [Si
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34); [Chi et al. (2026)](#bib.bib40). In LM
development, effective choices depend on the target model and its
training configuration: a change that benefits one setting may not
benefit another. With expensive training experiments, selecting
promising changes is essential to making effective use of limited
experimental resources. We therefore ask: how can language models learn
to make better model-improvement decisions and transfer this capability
to new models and training configurations?

A natural way to improve research judgment is to learn from past
experiments. Experimental records can inform an LLM’s decisions through
in-context learning (ICL) [Brown et al. (2020)](#bib.bib31); [Novikov et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib43); [Si et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34), and can also
improve predictions of experimental outcomes and the selection of
promising proposals [Wang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib46). This suggests
using experimental knowledge as feedback for parameter learning to
improve an LLM’s proposal-generation capability beyond in-context
learning. However, learning from this feedback raises two key challenges
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(b)-(c)).

First, _how can we obtain cheap and scalable training feedback?_
Training LLMs with reinforcement learning (RL) to generate better
proposals requires feedback on numerous sampled proposals. The most
direct feedback comes from executing each proposal and measuring its
outcome [Si et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34), but this requires costly
model-training runs. For example, training a 486M-parameter model on 8B
tokens takes approximately 45 B200 GPU-hours. A GRPO-style RL [Shao et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib45) setup with 100 updates, 32 training states per
update, and 8 proposals per state would require 25,600 proposal
evaluations. Executing these proposals at the same per-run cost would
consume approximately 1,152,000 GPU-hours, costing over \$16 million.
This motivates low-cost surrogate feedback without executing every
proposal. Second, _how can we keep surrogate feedback reliable as
learning progresses?_ Accurate predictions on past experiments do not
ensure reliable feedback throughout training. As learning changes which
proposals the LLM generates, the surrogate must evaluate proposals that
its experimental records may not cover. RL may then favor overestimated
proposals, increasing surrogate rewards without corresponding real
gains [Gao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib33); [Zhang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib32). A scalable learning approach must therefore keep
its feedback grounded in real outcomes on the proposals the current LLM
generates.

To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, a framework that trains
LLMs with RL to generate effective proposals for LM development. To
obtain cheap and scalable training signals, we use a frozen LLM as a
research world model (RWM) [Wang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib46) to predict
experimental gains relative to a reference configuration without
executing proposals. Grounded in an initial set of real experimental
records provided as in-context knowledge, the RWM supplies predicted
gains for constructing RL rewards [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib47). To
keep this feedback reliable as training progresses, we introduce
_on-policy calibration_: we periodically execute a subset of the current
policy’s proposals that the RWM ranks highly and incorporate their
measured outcomes into its in-context knowledge. This targets real
execution at proposals favored by RL, where overestimation can misdirect
learning, and helps maintain prediction reliability as LLMs’ proposals
change.

Our contributions are: (i) Method. We propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs
with RL using RWM-based rewards grounded in real records and calibrated
through targeted real execution; (ii) Experiments. Extensive
real-execution experiments on held-out environments show that LM2LM
enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and
surpass baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower
data-generation cost; (iii) Analysis. We show that RWM-based feedback
provides informative rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy
calibration mitigates overestimation and largely preserves ranking
quality as training progresses, unlike a fixed-record RWM that
eventually performs worse than random.

## 2 Related Work

AutoResearch with LLM Agents. AutoResearch aims to automate empirical AI
research through iterative idea generation, experimental implementation,
execution, and analysis. Early work shows LLM agents’ ability to
generate research ideas and conduct end-to-end experiments [Baek et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib28); [Si et al. (2025)](#bib.bib35); [Lu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib27); [Weng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib26), with benchmarks
evaluating these capabilities in machine-learning engineering and AI
R&D [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib25); [Chan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib24);
[Wijk et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23); [Chi et al. (2026)](#bib.bib40). To
improve research outcomes, some methods iterate on candidate solutions
with execution feedback while keeping the underlying agent fixed [Jiang
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22); [Novikov et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43);
[Karpathy (2026)](#bib.bib42). Others instead train the agent on
execution feedback, for machine-learning engineering [Yang et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib41) and LLM model development [Si et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib34). However, these methods rely on costly real
execution to obtain training signals, limiting learning at scale. In
contrast, we train LLMs with feedback from a world model that predicts
experimental outcomes, enabling scalable learning with fewer real
executions. Full details of related works are in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A More Details of Related Works ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

### 3.1 Preliminaries

AutoResearch for LM Development. We consider a set of AutoResearch tasks
$`\mathcal{E}`$, where each task $`e\in\mathcal{E}`$ seeks to improve a
target language model by modifying its architecture, training objective,
or optimization. Each task specifies a model family, model size,
pretraining budget, and a reference configuration with validation loss
$`b_{e}`$.

A research campaign on task $`e`$ involves two LLM-based roles: a
_proposer_ $`\pi_{\theta}`$, which generates proposals, and a _builder_
$`\mathcal{B}`$, which translates them into executable modifications to
the reference configuration. A campaign proceeds through repeated
proposal-and-execution steps. At step $`t`$, the proposer observes the
research state $`s_{t}=(e,h_{t})`$, where $`h_{t}`$ records the
proposals executed in previous steps and their measured outcomes, and
generates a proposal $`a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})`$. The
builder implements $`a_{t}`$, and the resulting training run yields
validation loss $`\ell_{t}`$. The experimental gain is defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       g(s_{t},a_{t})=b_{e}-\ell_{t},
       ```                             |     | (1) |

where $`g(s_{t},a_{t})>0`$ indicates an improvement over the reference
configuration. The proposal and its measured outcome are appended to the
history, producing $`h_{t+1}`$ and the next research state
$`s_{t+1}=(e,h_{t+1})`$.

Problem Statement. An AutoResearch campaign consists of a sequence of
research steps, where each step requires the proposer to decide what
proposal to explore next based on the current task and previous
experimental outcomes. In this paper, our goal is then to improve this
decision-making capability by training a proposer $`\pi_{\theta}`$ to
generate proposals with high experimental gain at each step.

Formally, let $`\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{train}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{test}}`$ denote disjoint sets of training and
held-out tasks, respectively, and let $`\mu_{\mathrm{train}}`$ and
$`\mu_{\mathrm{test}}`$ denote the corresponding distributions over
research states $`s=(e,h)`$. For
$`\nu\in\{\mathrm{train},\mathrm{test}\}`$, we define the expected
experimental gain as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
J_{\nu}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{\nu},\,a\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)}\left[g(s,a)\right],
``` |  | (2) |

where $`J_{\nu}(\theta)`$ measures the expected experimental gain of
proposals generated by $`\pi_{\theta}`$ across research states sampled
from $`\mu_{\nu}`$. We seek to maximize $`J_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta)`$
using training tasks, with the aim of achieving high
$`J_{\mathrm{test}}(\theta)`$ on held-out tasks.

RL with Experimental Feedback. Since the experimental gain $`g(s,a)`$
directly measures the quality of a proposal, it can serve as a
verifiable reward, making reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
(RLVR) a natural approach to optimizing
$`J_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta)`$ [Lambert et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49). A
representative algorithm is DAPO [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib47): for a
state $`s\sim\mu_{\mathrm{train}}`$, it samples a group of $`G`$
proposals $`\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$ from the current policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid s)`$, scores them with rewards
$`\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, and maximizes

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{DAPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[`$ | $`\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sum_{i}|a_{i}|}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{k=1}^{|a_{i}|}`$ |  | (3) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\min\big(\rho_{i,k}\hat{A}_{i},\,\bar{\rho}_{i,k}\hat{A}_{i}\big)\Big],`$ |  |  |

where $`|a_{i}|`$ is the number of tokens in $`a_{i}`$,
$`\rho_{i,k}=\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,k}\mid s,a_{i,<k})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{i,k}\mid s,a_{i,<k})`$
is the token-level probability ratio,
$`\bar{\rho}_{i,k}=\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,k},1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}})`$,
and the advantage
$`\hat{A}_{i}=\big(R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})\big)/\mathrm{std}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})`$.
Groups with no reward variation are discarded.

### 3.2 Scalable Feedback: Research World Model as the Verifier

RLVR typically relies on inexpensive verification, e.g., checking the
correctness of a mathematical answer [Cobbe et al. (2021)](#bib.bib48).
However, directly applying RLVR to AutoResearch for LM development is
computationally expensive, as evaluating each proposal requires training
the target model to measure its experimental gain. Since RL samples
numerous proposals across research states and training updates,
obtaining execution-based rewards at scale becomes prohibitively costly.
To address this challenge, we use a research world model (RWM) to
predict experimental gains without executing every proposal, and train
the proposer with DAPO on rewards built from these predictions.

Research World Model. An RWM predicts the outcomes of AI research
experiments without executing them [Wang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib46). We
instantiate it as a frozen LLM $`W`$. To improve prediction reliability,
we ground it in real experimental records. Specifically, we collect
$`N_{0}`$ experimental records from training tasks to form the initial
record set $`M_{0}`$. Each record $`(e,a,g)`$ contains a task, an
executed proposal, and its measured gain. Formally, given a task $`e`$,
proposal $`a`$, and experimental records $`M`$, the RWM predicts the
experimental gain $`\hat{g}(e,a;M)`$ as

|     |                          |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{g}(e,a;M)=W(e,a;M),
       ```                       |     | (4) |

where $`M`$ is provided as in-context evidence. Thus, evaluating a
proposal requires only an LLM call rather than a model-training run.

Reward Design. A straightforward approach is to use RWM-predicted gains
directly as RL rewards. This poses two challenges. First, predicted
gains vary in scale across tasks and may contain extreme values,
allowing tasks with larger gain scales or a few extreme predictions to
dominate policy optimization. Second, high predicted gains do not
necessarily imply useful proposals, as proposals may be invalid,
repetitive, or insufficiently diverse.

To address the first challenge, we normalize predicted gains by a
task-specific scale $`\tau_{e}`$ and clip them at a threshold $`c`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(s,a;M)=\mathrm{clip}\big(\hat{g}(e,a;M)/\tau_{e},\,-c,\,c\big),
``` |  | (5) |

where $`e`$ is the task associated with research state $`s`$, and
$`\tau_{e}`$ is determined by reference seed noise and the dispersion of
RWM-predicted gains. Normalization makes rewards comparable across
tasks, while clipping limits the influence of extreme predictions on
policy updates.

To address the second challenge, we incorporate proposal validity,
novelty, and diversity into the final rewards. For a group of $`G`$
proposals sampled from state $`s`$, a proposal is *invalid* if it cannot
be implemented by the builder, *repeated* if it reproduces the reference
configuration or a proposal in the history $`h`$, and *valid* otherwise.
Let $`\mathcal{V}`$ denote the set of valid proposals within the group.
We assign

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{i}=\begin{cases}r(s,a_{i};M)+b_{i},&a_{i}\in\mathcal{V},\\[2.0pt]
\min_{j\in\mathcal{V}}R_{j}-\delta,&a_{i}\text{ repeated},\\[2.0pt]
-c,&a_{i}\text{ invalid},\end{cases}
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\delta>0`$ is a repetition margin and
$`b_{i}=\lambda\sigma_{G}\tilde{d}_{i}`$ is a diversity bonus. Here,
$`d_{i}=1/n_{i}`$, where $`n_{i}`$ is the number of valid proposals
sharing the same mechanism direction as $`a_{i}`$ within the group;
$`\tilde{d}_{i}`$ is the within-group standardized value of $`d_{i}`$,
$`\sigma_{G}`$ is the standard deviation of normalized predicted rewards
within the group, and $`\lambda`$ controls the bonus strength. The
diversity bonus favors less common mechanism directions to encourage
broader exploration during RL. Repeated proposals provide no new
experimental information but may receive high predicted gains, so
$`\delta`$ ranks them below all valid proposals. More reward details are
in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Reward Design ‣ Appendix B Methodology Details of LM2LM ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

Policy Optimization. With the rewards in
Eq. ([6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.2 Scalable Feedback: Research World Model as the Verifier ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")),
we train the proposer using DAPO
(Eq. ([3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models"))).
Since rewards are already normalized by $`\tau_{e}`$ to be comparable
across tasks, we use group-mean-centered advantages
$`\hat{A}_{i}=R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})`$ without
dividing by the group’s standard deviation [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib29), preserving reward dispersion across groups.

Beyond providing RL rewards, the RWM also enables low-cost expansion of
RL training states. Each RL training state $`s=(e,h)`$ pairs a training
task $`e`$ with a history $`h`$ sampled from
$`\{(e^{\prime},a,g)\in\mathcal{P}:e^{\prime}=e\}`$, where the record
pool $`\mathcal{P}`$ is initialized as $`M_{0}`$. Since $`M_{0}`$
contains only a few records per task and scaling up $`\mathcal{P}`$ with
real outcomes requires costly execution, we periodically add the
state–proposal pairs $`\mathcal{D}`$ sampled during RL to
$`\mathcal{P}`$, with their RWM-predicted gains as synthetic outcomes
(Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Synthetic Training-State Expansion ‣ Appendix B Methodology Details of LM2LM ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{P}\cup\big\{\big(e,a,\hat{g}(e,a;M)\big):\big((e,h),a\big)\in\mathcal{D}\big\}.
``` |  | (7) |

Thus, both RWM-based rewards and synthetic training states enable
scalable RL without executing every sampled proposal.

### 3.3 Reliable Feedback: On-Policy Calibration of the RWM

Prediction Bias under Policy Shift. While the RWM provides scalable
feedback, RL increasingly favors highly rated proposals that may not be
adequately covered by the initial records $`M_{0}`$, making it
susceptible to reward overoptimization [Gao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib33);
[Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib32). To characterize this risk, we
decompose the normalized real objective as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{J}(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[r(s,a;M)\big]}_{\hat{J}_{M}(\theta)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{g}(s,a)-r(s,a;M)\big]}_{\Delta_{M}(\theta)},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\bar{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\bar{g}(s,a)]`$,
$`\bar{g}(s,a)=\mathrm{clip}(g(s,a)/\tau_{e},-c,c)`$, and the
expectations are over $`s\sim\mu_{\mathrm{train}}`$ and
$`a\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)`$. Here, $`\hat{J}_{M}(\theta)`$ is the
expected normalized predicted gain, and $`\Delta_{M}(\theta)`$ is its
prediction bias under the current proposer. As RL optimizes RWM-based
rewards without observing this bias, $`\hat{J}_{M}`$ may increase
without a corresponding increase in $`\bar{J}`$.

Targeted Calibration with Real Execution. To mitigate this risk, we
introduce on-policy calibration: rather than keeping the RWM’s records
fixed, LM2LM periodically executes high-rated proposals from the current
proposer and incorporates their real outcomes into the records to
improve RWM predictions as the proposer evolves.

Specifically, at each update point $`u`$, let $`\mathcal{C}_{u}`$ denote
the current proposer’s samples on training states, excluding proposals
whose outcomes are already in $`M_{u}`$. We select $`B_{u}`$ high-rated
proposals:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{Q}_{u}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{u},\;|\mathcal{Q}|=B_{u}}\;\sum_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{Q}}r(s,a;M_{u}).
``` |  | (9) |

The builder implements and executes these proposals, and their measured
outcomes are added to the records:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{u+1}=M_{u}\cup\big\{(e,a,g(s,a)):(s,a)\in\mathcal{Q}_{u}\big\},
``` |  | (10) |

where $`e`$ is the task of $`s`$. Subsequent RL steps compute rewards
with $`W(\cdot\,;M_{u+1})`$. Calibration occurs between RL training
segments, keeping the RWM feedback fixed within each segment.

Since calibration updates the RWM through in-context records without
retraining, it requires only $`\sum_{u=0}^{U-1}B_{u}`$ additional real
executions, independent of the number of proposals sampled during RL.

## 4 Experiments

We then conduct ablation studies to understand the contribution of

and analyze why RWM feedback can guide RL and how calibration sustains

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Environments. We construct language-model training environments spanning
four autoregressive (AR) families: OLMo-3 [Olmo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib12), Qwen3 [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib11),
NanoChat [Karpathy (2025)](#bib.bib10), and Marin [Hall et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib7); and four diffusion families: E2D2-SW, E2D2 [Arriola
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9), MDM [Nie et al. (2025)](#bib.bib6), and
BD3LM [Arriola et al. (2025)](#bib.bib8). Each task specifies a model
family, model size, training budget, and reference training recipe. Each
experiment trains the target model from scratch. Further details are in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Model Families ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

Data Construction. We split the tasks into 40 training and 10 held-out
tasks. We curate and execute 300 proposals from a library of
training-recipe mechanisms on the training tasks, yielding 300
experimental records. Of these, 100 serve as the initial records
$`M_{0}`$ of LM2LM, grounding the RWM. We construct initial RL training
states $`s=(e,h)`$ with histories $`h`$ containing 0–8 records of task
$`e`$ from $`M_{0}`$, expanded during RL
(Eq. ([7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 3.2 Scalable Feedback: Research World Model as the Verifier ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models"))).
For evaluation, we construct 60 AR and 18 diffusion held-out research
states using only real experimental histories. More details are in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Data Construction ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

|  |  |  |  |  |  |

### 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines

Main Results. We compare LM2LM with baselines on proposal quality across
held-out research states, following the evaluation protocol in
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
reports the results. We observe: (i) *Past experimental experience
helps, but how it is used matters.* With 300 experimental records,
Base-ICL improves Avg@4 over Base from $`-7.94`$ to $`4.04`$ on AR tasks
and from $`-4.18`$ to $`4.03`$ on diffusion tasks, demonstrating the
value of past experimental experience. In contrast, SFT and RFT-Real
fail to consistently improve proposal quality, suggesting that directly
fine-tuning on a few hundred past proposals is insufficient to learn
effective proposal generation. (ii) *LM2LM outperforms all baselines.*
It achieves the highest Avg@4 and Best@4 on both AR (17.06/23.40) and
diffusion (7.10/9.93) tasks, outperforming Base on 93.3% and 100% of
states, respectively. This validates LM2LM’s effectiveness. (iii) *RL
reduces harmful proposals while enabling larger gains.* Compared with
Base, LM2LM reduces negative-gain proposals from 70.4% to 39.2% on AR
tasks and from 59.7% to 7.6% on diffusion tasks. Despite a higher
negative-gain rate than GPT-6-sol-ICL on AR tasks (39.2% vs. 29.6%),
LM2LM achieves substantially higher Avg@4 (17.06 vs. 6.15), suggesting
that it explores higher-gain proposals rather than simply avoiding
negative outcomes.

we evaluate checkpoints at steps 16, 40, 64, 96, and 128, corresponding

executions.* At step 96 ($`M_{3}`$), LM2LM achieves Avg@4 of 17.06 and

### 4.3 Generalization to Unseen Model Families

We further evaluate whether LM2LM generalizes to an unseen model family.
Specifically, we hold out the entire Qwen3 family and train the proposer
on the remaining families, excluding all Qwen3 records from training and
RWM grounding. (i) *LM2LM generalizes to unseen model families.* Without
Qwen3 records, LM2LM achieves Avg@4 and Best@4 of 53.72 and 86.69,
substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (21.63 and 25.27 for
GPT-6-sol-ICL), demonstrating its ability to generate effective
proposals for an unseen model family. (ii) *Out-of-family in-context
records provide limited benefits.* Base-ICL performs similarly to Base
(2.97 vs. 3.53 Avg@4), while GPT-6-sol-ICL only slightly improves over
GPT-6-sol (21.63 vs. 20.19). This suggests that directly using
out-of-family records offers limited benefits, highlighting the
advantage of LM2LM’s RWM-based RL.

### 4.4 Ablation Studies

To understand the contributions of on-policy calibration and the impact
of its proposal selection strategy, we conduct ablation studies by
comparing LM2LM with variants that differ only in how the RWM records
are constructed, with all other settings following
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

Impact of On-Policy Calibration. We compare LM2LM with two static
variants whose records stay fixed: $`M_{0}`$ (100 initial records) and
$`M_{0}^{\prime}`$ ($`M_{0}`$ plus 100 random records, matching LM2LM’s
200 real runs). As shown in
Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(a),
LM2LM achieves higher AR Avg@4 and Best@4 than both variants, and
outperforms $`M_{0}`$ and $`M_{0}^{\prime}`$ in Avg@4 on 74.2% and 70.0%
of the held-out AR states, respectively. With the same budget of 200
real runs, calibration outperforms adding random records (17.06 vs.
14.76 Avg@4), highlighting the importance of proposal selection for RWM
grounding.

Impact of Calibration Strategy. We further assess the impact of
top-rated proposal selection in
Eq. ([9](#S3.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 3.3 Reliable Feedback: On-Policy Calibration of the RWM ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models"))
by comparing LM2LM with a variant that uniformly samples valid proposals
whose outcomes are not yet in the records, while keeping the same
initial records and three calibration rounds (200 real runs). As shown
in
Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(b),
random calibration lowers AR Avg@4 and Best@4 from 17.06 and 23.40 to
10.84 and 18.60. We attribute this to top-rated selection correcting
overestimated changes that RL is about to reinforce, whereas random
selection can miss them or add misleading records. Case studies are in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Case Studies: Impact of Calibration Strategies ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

### 4.5 In-depth Dissection of LM2LM

We further analyze why RWM feedback can guide RL and how calibration
keeps it reliable. For this analysis, we extend training from 96 to 128
steps with a fourth calibration of LM2LM (236 real runs) and compare it
with the static variant whose RWM keeps $`M_{0}`$ throughout; each
variant is scored by the RWM it is trained with.

Why Can RWM Feedback Guide RL? We examine the alignment between
RWM-predicted and real-execution feedback by measuring the cosine
similarity of group-relative advantages and the within-state pairwise
ranking accuracy over four executed proposals per held-out AR research
state. As shown in
Figs. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(a)
and (b), LM2LM maintains advantage cosine of 0.50–0.71 and pairwise
accuracy of 0.61–0.69, above chance levels of 0 and 0.5. Since DAPO
optimizes group-relative advantages, this indicates that the RWM
provides informative relative feedback for policy optimization.

How Does On-Policy Calibration Help? To understand the gains from
calibration in
Sec. [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Ablation Studies ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models"),
we examine how the feedback of the static variant evolves as RL changes
the proposer’s outputs
(Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
We observe that (i) *Calibration maintains reliable relative feedback.*
As shown in
Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(a),
the static RWM’s advantage cosine declines from 0.69 at step 16 to
$`-0.42`$ at step 128, so its predicted advantages become negatively
aligned with real ones, whereas LM2LM keeps it positive. Likewise, its
pairwise accuracy drops from 0.72 to 0.37, below chance (0.5), while
LM2LM maintains 0.61
(Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(b)).
We further observe that scoring the same LM2LM proposals with $`M_{0}`$
instead of calibrated records lowers pairwise accuracy from 0.61 to 0.46
at step 96
(Appendix [I.2](#A9.SS2 "I.2 Effect of Updating Records on Identical Proposals ‣ Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
These results indicate that on-policy calibration keeps the predicted
advantages that DAPO optimizes aligned with real ones. (ii) *Calibration
limits reward overestimation.* As shown in
Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(c),
the predicted–real reward gap of the static RWM rises sharply after step
64, reaching about 2.0 at step 96, whereas LM2LM’s gap stays below 1 and
decreases from 0.85 at step 96 to 0.17 at step 128 after the fourth
calibration. Thus, calibration also keeps predicted rewards close to
real ones, mitigating reward overoptimization [Gao et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib33). For example, our case studies in
Appendix [I.3](#A9.SS3 "I.3 Case Studies ‣ Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
show that the initial RWM overestimates a NanoChat optimizer change
(predicted $`+0.7`$ to $`+1.25`$ vs. real $`-1.46`$), so the static
variant raises its share of NanoChat training proposals from 30% to 79%
by step 64. In contrast, the first calibration of LM2LM adds its real
outcomes to the records, and the share falls to 8%. More metrics and
case studies are in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").

## 5 Conclusion

We presented LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effective
proposals for LM development, using a frozen LLM grounded in real
experimental records as an RWM and keeping it reliable through on-policy
calibration. Extensive experiments across autoregressive and diffusion
LM environments show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform
frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and achieve higher gains than
baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower data-generation
cost. Our analysis shows that RWM-based feedback provides informative
rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy calibration mitigates reward
overestimation and preserves ranking quality as training progresses.
These findings highlight RWM-guided RL as a promising paradigm for
learning research judgment from experimental feedback, offering a
potential path toward RSI.

## 6 Limitations

Our study focuses on proposal-level improvements in LM development
rather than end-to-end research campaigns. Although the RWM provides
informative rankings and substantially reduces the need for real
execution during RL, its predictions remain imperfect, and on-policy
calibration still requires a limited number of additional model-training
runs. Future work could extend our framework to broader research
environments, including LM post-training, and evaluate its effectiveness
in longer, end-to-end research campaigns.

## 7 Ethics Statement

This work studies the use of LLMs to automate research on language-model
development. Such systems may help reduce the cost of experimentation
and accelerate scientific progress, but they also introduce risks,
including unreliable experimental conclusions, increased computational
resource consumption, and the potential misuse of automated
model-improvement capabilities. Our framework evaluates proposals in
controlled training environments and uses real experimental outcomes to
calibrate surrogate feedback. Nevertheless, predicted rewards and
experimental gains do not guarantee the safety or broader usefulness of
proposed model changes. We therefore emphasize the importance of human
oversight, independent evaluation, and appropriate safeguards when
applying automated research systems to more capable models or
higher-stakes settings.

## Appendix A More Details of Related Works

### A.1 World Models for LLM Agents.

A world model predicts the consequences of an agent’s actions, enabling
planning or learning without interacting with the real
environment [Sutton (1991)](#bib.bib21); [Ha and Schmidhuber
(2018)](#bib.bib20); [Hafner et al. (2023)](#bib.bib19). Recent work
uses LLMs as world models for reasoning [Hao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18),
web navigation [Chae et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17); [Gu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16), text-based simulation [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib13), and agent training in synthetic environments [Chen
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14). In AutoResearch, concurrent work WMRL [Yang
et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib15) uses a frozen LLM as the world model to
predict execution outcomes as RL rewards and rescales them with a
monotone mapping fitted on real executions at every training step. In
contrast, LM2LM uses a frozen LLM as the world model that learns
research knowledge from real experimental records in context [Wang et
al. (2026b)](#bib.bib46), and improves its predictions on the current
policy’s proposals by adding the outcomes of a few top-rated ones to
these records.

### A.2 Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45); [Liu et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib29); [Lin et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1); [Yu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib47); [Lin et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib53); [Wu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib54) has become an effective approach to improving LLM
capabilities, particularly through reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR) [Lambert et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49). Methods
such as GRPO [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45) and DAPO [Yu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib47) optimize LLM policies using group-relative feedback
without a learned value function. While RLVR typically relies on
inexpensive verification, such as checking mathematical answers or
executing code, LM development requires costly model-training
experiments to measure proposal quality. Recent work applies RL to
automated AI research using execution feedback [Yang et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib41); [Si et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34), but obtaining
rewards through real execution at every training step limits
scalability. In contrast, LM2LM uses RWM-predicted experimental gains as
RL rewards, while periodically calibrating the RWM with selected real
executions to maintain feedback reliability.

### A.3 Self-Evolving LLMs and Agents

Self-evolving methods improve a model or agent with data or feedback it
generates itself. One line trains LLMs on self-generated solutions that
are filtered or rewarded by verifiers or by the model itself [Zelikman
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib56); [Gulcehre et al. (2023)](#bib.bib30); [Yuan
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57). Another line lets agents modify their own
code, prompts, or workflows and keeps the changes that improve benchmark
performance [Lin et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib61); [Yin et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib60); [Robeyns et al. (2025)](#bib.bib58); [Lin et al.
(2026c)](#bib.bib55); [Lin et al. (2026d)](#bib.bib59). These methods
rely on feedback that is cheap to obtain, such as answer checking, code
execution, or benchmark scores. In LM development, measuring a single
proposal requires a full model-training run. LM2LM addresses this
setting by learning from RWM-predicted gains and calibrating the RWM
with a small number of real executions, and it targets the ability to
improve LM development, which recursive self-improvement ultimately
requires.

## Appendix B Methodology Details of LM2LM

### B.1 Reward Design

Reward Scale. The task-specific reward scale is
$`\tau_{e}=\max(\sigma_{e},\,1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}_{e})`$, where
$`\sigma_{e}`$ is the standard deviation of three reference runs and
$`\mathrm{MAD}_{e}`$ is the median absolute deviation of the
RWM-predicted gains for task $`e`$ from previous rollouts. It is
recomputed at the start of each training segment and held fixed within
the segment; for the first segment, $`\mathrm{MAD}_{e}`$ is estimated
from RWM predictions on proposals of a preliminary run.

Validity, Repetition, and Bonuses. A proposal is valid if it can be
translated into an executable modification of the task’s reference
configuration, which is checked without running it. A proposal that
changes nothing, i.e., all of whose modifications are inert, receives
$`-3`$ instead of $`-c`$. Repetition is decided on the resolved
implementation: a proposal repeats the reference configuration if it
resolves to it, the history if it matches a proposal in $`h`$, and an
earlier proposal of its group if their implementations coincide. A
proposal that repeats an earlier proposal of its own group keeps at most
zero reward, and if no proposal of a group is valid and novel, every
proposal of the group receives $`-c`$. In the rollouts whose proposals
are selected for calibration, a valid proposal with a positive predicted
gain also receives a novelty bonus
$`0.2\,\sigma_{G}/\sqrt{1+N_{\mathrm{prev}}}`$ in addition to the
diversity bonus $`b_{i}`$, where $`N_{\mathrm{prev}}`$ is the number of
earlier occurrences of the same change on the task.

Hyperparameters. We use a repetition margin $`\delta=1`$ and a diversity
weight $`\lambda=0.3`$.

### B.2 Synthetic Training-State Expansion

Training states are initially constructed as described in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Data Construction ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models").
After each rollout, i.e., every 8 training steps, we add the proposals
generated in that rollout to the history pool, with their gains
predicted by the current RWM as synthetic outcomes, and resample
histories from the combined pool of real and predicted records to
construct the training states of the next rollout.

## Appendix C Environments and Data

### C.1 Model Families

Each family shares an architecture, training-data distribution, and
evaluation metric across its tasks.

AR Families.

- •
  *OLMo-3* [Olmo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib12): OLMo-3 small models trained
  on samples of Dolma 3 and evaluated by mean bits per byte (BPB) across
  ten downstream tasks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, four MMLU
  subsets, HumanEval, MBPP, and MATH-500).
- •
  *Qwen3* [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib11): The official Qwen3
  architecture and tokenizer. Training data (Dolma 3) and evaluation
  (ten-task BPB) are shared with OLMo-3.
- •
  *NanoChat* [Karpathy (2025)](#bib.bib10): depth-parameterized
  transformers trained on ClimbMix and evaluated by BPB on a held-out
  ClimbMix validation shard.
- •
  *Marin* [Hall et al. (2025)](#bib.bib7): Marin architectures with the
  same training data and evaluation as OLMo-3.

Diffusion Families. All four families are trained on SlimPajama with a
2,048-token context and evaluated by the negative log-likelihood of true
tokens accumulated along the model’s greedy, most-confident-first
unmasking order, measured in nats per token.

- •
  *E2D2* [Arriola et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9): an encoder-decoder masked
  diffusion model with a clean-token encoder and a cross-attention
  decoder for noisy tokens. Noise levels are sampled per block, yielding
  block-autoregressive modeling.
- •
  *E2D2-SW* [Wang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib2): This is an E2D2 variant
  with a token-causal encoder and a decoder trained on packed sliding
  windows of blocks but evaluated on non-overlapping blocks.
- •
  *BD3LM* [Arriola et al. (2025)](#bib.bib8): a block diffusion model
  using a single network over concatenated clean and noisy sequences
  with block-structured attention.
- •
  *MDM* [Nie et al. (2025)](#bib.bib6): a full-sequence bidirectional
  masked diffusion model with one noise level per sequence; it appears
  only among the training tasks and has no held-out task.

Tasks. A task specifies a model family, size, and training budget. Tasks
within a family share the training data, tokenizer, evaluation protocol,
and hardware. We construct tasks across these model families, following
the naming conventions below:

- •
  *OLMo-3*: 100m-1c denotes 100M non-embedding parameters trained with
  $`C=1`$ times the Chinchilla-optimal budget [Hoffmann et al.
  (2022)](#bib.bib5), or $`20C`$ tokens per parameter (2B tokens).
- •
  *Qwen3*: Two model lines are kept separate. q152m-2b has 152.5M
  parameters, 2B training tokens, and an 8,192-token context. q06b-1b
  uses the official Qwen3-0.6B configuration with 1B training tokens and
  a 2,048-token context, appearing only in training tasks.
- •
  *NanoChat*: d12-8k-2p15b denotes a depth-12 model (width 768) with an
  8,192-token context trained on 2.15B tokens; p denotes the decimal
  point.
- •
  *Marin*: 486m-8b denotes 485.9M parameters trained on 8B tokens; 2p9b
  denotes 2.9B tokens.
- •
  *Diffusion families*: 113m-6e18 denotes 113M non-embedding parameters
  trained with $`6\times 10^{18}`$ FLOPs, from which the token budget is
  derived.

Each task uses its family’s default training recipe as the reference
configuration. The reference loss $`b_{e}`$ and seed noise
$`\sigma_{e}`$ are the mean and standard deviation of validation loss
over three runs.

### C.2 Data Construction

Mechanism Library. We curate a library of training-recipe mechanisms
covering model architecture, optimization, and training objectives. Each
reviewed card specifies a hypothesis, expected effects, supporting
evidence, and family-specific configurations (levels) with validity
constraints. The AR library contains 40 accepted cards;
Table [6](#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ C.1 Model Families ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
shows an example.

The library also defines the action space shared by all proposers,
specifying modifiable recipe settings and their valid values. Proposals
may combine any number of mechanisms, choose numerical values within
valid ranges, and propose new variants for allowed categorical settings,
which the builder implements. Model size, training budget, data,
tokenizer, evaluation, seeds, and hardware remain fixed.

Experimental Records. We construct 300 proposal–outcome records across
40 training tasks. To ensure diversity and balanced coverage of
mechanism directions, we sample proposals by instantiating one or two
mechanism-card levels per proposal. Of the 300 executed proposals, 226
involve a single mechanism direction and 74 combine two
(Table [5](#A3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ C.1 Model Families ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
We select 100 records as the initial RWM records $`M_{0}`$.

Training and Baseline Data. Each research state $`s=(e,h)`$ consists of
a task $`e`$ and a history $`h`$ of proposals and their outcomes.
Initial RL histories contain 0–8 task-matched real records sampled from
$`M_{0}`$, and are expanded with RWM-predicted outcomes during RL
(Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Synthetic Training-State Expansion ‣ Appendix B Methodology Details of LM2LM ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
ICL baselines use up to 300 executed records as in-context examples. SFT
uses 300 $`(s,a)`$ pairs constructed from these records, while RFT-Real
retains the 114 pairs with positive measured gains.

Held-out States. We construct 60 AR and 18 diffusion research states
from 10 held-out tasks. All states contain histories of real
experimental outcomes and are fixed across methods. No held-out
experimental records are used for proposer training, RWM grounding, or
calibration.

## Appendix D Evaluation Protocol

### D.1 Metrics

We evaluate all proposers on the same fixed set of 60 autoregressive
(AR) and 18 diffusion held-out research states. For each state, a
proposer generates four proposals, which are implemented by the same
builder and evaluated through real model-training experiments. Invalid
or unchanged proposals receive zero gain, while negative gains are
retained.

Validation Metrics. We measure model performance using:

- •
  BPB (Bits per Byte): Average number of bits needed to encode each byte
  of text, enabling comparisons across tokenizers [Gao et al.
  (2020)](#bib.bib4). We use it for AR models, evaluated on held-out
  text as specified per family
  (Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Model Families ‣ Appendix C Environments and Data ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
- •
  NLL (Negative Log-Likelihood): Negative log-probability assigned to
  validation sequences. For diffusion models, we compute the exact
  negative log-likelihood of the true tokens accumulated along the
  model’s greedy, most-confident-first within-block unmasking order,
  with each revealed position set to the true token, reported in nats
  per token.

Lower values indicate better model performance.

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate proposal quality using:

- •
  Avg@4 / Best@4: Mean / maximum real gain among four proposals per
  state, averaged equally across states.
- •
  Win rate: Fraction of states where one method achieves higher Avg@4
  than another, with ties counted as 0.5.
- •
  Neg.: Fraction of proposals with negative real gains.

Experimental gains are reported in $`10^{-3}`$ BPB (AR) and $`10^{-2}`$
nats per token (diffusion).

### E.2 RL Training

We use DAPO with asymmetric clipping ($`\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}=0.2`$,
$`\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}=0.28`$), a token-level policy-gradient loss,
and no KL or entropy regularization. For dynamic sampling, we sample 277

updates with a global batch size of 256. We use Adam ($`\beta_{1}=0.9`$,
$`\beta_{2}=0.98`$), weight decay 0.1, and an initial learning rate of
$`10^{-6}`$, multiplied by 0.7 after each training segment.

The candidates $`\mathcal{C}_{u}`$ in

## Appendix H Case Studies: Impact of Calibration Strategies

Top-rated calibration executes the proposals that the RWM rates highest
and RL is about to reinforce, whereas random calibration samples
proposals regardless of their predicted gains. The following two cases
illustrate how this difference affects RL on held-out AR families.

Delayed Correction of Overestimated Changes. M-UPD-72 is a NanoChat
change to the number of tokens per optimizer update that the RWM
overestimates: under $`M_{0}`$, it rewards the change with $`+0.7`$ to
$`+0.9`$ in the training reward scale, whereas its real reward on
held-out states is about $`-1.46`$
(Fig. [7](#A7.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ G.2 Total Cost ‣ Appendix G Cost Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
Top-rated calibration executes seven proposals applying it in the first
update (after step 16), five of which degrade the reference
configuration; the RWM then rewards the change with $`-1.66`$, and its
share of NanoChat training proposals falls from a peak of 45% at step 24
to 8% by step 64. Random calibration executes none of them in the first
update, so the RWM keeps rewarding the change until the second update
(after step 40); lagging behind the reward, its share still peaks at 80%
at step 48 and falls to 24% only by step 72. Both shares eventually drop
below 3% by step 96, but random calibration spends substantially more
training on a harmful change before correcting it.

Misleading Records from Random Selection. Random calibration can also
add records that mislead the RWM. Its first batch contains an OLMo
change that replaces local attention with an alternative token-mixing
operator, which happens to improve its training task by 1.43%. With this
record, the RWM starts rewarding the change (mean training reward rising
from about $`-0.6`$ under $`M_{0}`$ to $`+0.8`$ after the first update
and $`+1.2`$ by step 96), and RL raises its share of OLMo training
proposals from 4% to 16%
(Fig. [8](#A7.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ G.2 Total Cost ‣ Appendix G Cost Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")).
On held-out OLMo states, the change accounts for 53% of the proposals at
step 96, with a mean real gain of $`-16.4\times 10^{-3}`$ BPB, lowering
the OLMo Avg@4 from 7.39 under top-rated calibration to $`-6.37`$. Under
top-rated calibration, the RWM keeps penalizing the change ($`-1.2`$ to
$`-0.5`$), and its share stays below 5%.

## Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis

### I.1 Diagnosing RWM Feedback Reliability

Feedback Metric. At each checkpoint, we execute the four proposals of
each of the 60 held-out AR states, whose outcomes never enter the
records. The static variant is scored by $`W(M_{0})`$ throughout, and
LM2LM by the RWM in force: $`W(M_{0})`$ at step 16, $`W(M_{1})`$ at step
40, $`W(M_{2})`$ at steps 48 and 64, $`W(M_{3})`$ at step 96, and
$`W(M_{4})`$ at step 128. Rewards follow the normalization and clipping
used for RL training, $`\mathrm{clip}(g/\tau_{\mathrm{eff}},\pm 6)`$.
For the proposals $`i=1,\dots,G`$ of a state, let $`\hat{r}_{i}`$ and
$`r_{i}`$ denote their predicted and real rewards, and let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{i}=\hat{r}_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}\hat{r}_{j},\qquad A_{i}=r_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j}
``` |  |

be their predicted and real advantages, with $`G=4`$ (states with fewer
than two scored proposals are skipped). We use the following metrics,
with 95% bootstrap intervals over states (2,000 resamples):

- •
  *Advantage cosine*: the cosine similarity between the predicted and
  real advantages, concatenated over all states.
- •
  *Pairwise accuracy*: over all pairs of proposals of a state whose real
  rewards differ, the fraction whose predicted order agrees with their
  real order, where a pair with equal predicted rewards counts as 0.5;
  pairs are pooled over states, so random rewards attain 0.5.
- •
  *Sign agreement*: the fraction of proposals with nonzero
  $`\hat{A}_{i}`$ and $`A_{i}`$ whose signs agree.
- •
  *Wrong-reinforcement rate*: $`\Pr(A_{i}<0\mid\hat{A}_{i}>0)`$, the
  fraction of positively reinforced proposals that perform below their
  group’s real mean.
- •
  *Top-1 hit rate*: over states whose real rewards differ, the fraction
  in which the proposal with the highest predicted reward also has the
  highest real reward.
- •
  *Real advantage of reinforced proposals*: the mean $`A_{i}`$ over
  proposals with $`\hat{A}_{i}>0`$.

We also report the mean predicted and real rewards and their difference
(Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Main Comparison with Baselines ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")(c)).

Results.
Fig. [9](#A9.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ I.3 Case Studies ‣ Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
complements the advantage alignment and ranking results of
Sec. [4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 In-depth Dissection of LM2LM ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
by examining whether individual proposals are reinforced in the correct
direction. Early in training, the static RWM provides feedback
comparable to that of LM2LM, but its reliability deteriorates as the
proposer evolves. From step 16 to step 64, its sign agreement drops from
0.78 to 0.52 and its wrong-reinforcement rate rises from 18% to 46%,
reaching 0.43 and 55% at step 128. In contrast, the sign agreement of
LM2LM is 0.68, 0.65, and 0.62 at steps 16, 64, and 128, and its
wrong-reinforcement rate stays between 30% and 36% at these steps. As a
result, the proposals that the static RWM reinforces have a negative
mean real advantage from step 96 onward ($`-0.31`$), i.e., RL
increasingly favors proposals that underperform their group, whereas
those reinforced by LM2LM remain positive (0.18 to 0.59).
Fig. [11](#A9.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ I.3 Case Studies ‣ Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis ‣ Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models")
shows the same trend at the level of individual proposals.

### I.3 Case Studies

Family-dependent optimizer changes. The RWM rewards an
