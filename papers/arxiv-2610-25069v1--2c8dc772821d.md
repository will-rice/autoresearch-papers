###### Abstract

Autonomous agents are increasingly capable of developing machine
learning (ML) models end to end. In medical practice, however, strong
performance on the target metric does not fully characterize how good
the models are in real-world use cases. For example, medical ML models
should also provide well-calibrated risk estimates and remain robust
across patient populations. Yet autonomous model development is
typically guided by task objectives that emphasize simple predictive
discrimination (e.g. AUROC). This raises a key question: are these
objectives sufficient to guide agents toward models that meet the
broader requirements of real-world medical use? In this study, we
introduce Auto-MD-bench, a benchmark of 15 real-world medical tasks from
10 clinical datasets, spanning survival analysis, classification and
sequential prediction, with external validation across cohorts,
hospitals and time periods. We evaluate four agents: AIDE, AI Scientist
v2, Codex, and Autoresearch. The strongest agents achieve competitive
primary scores, yet their predicted risks are less calibrated than those
of the simple baseline models, which could lead to severe medical
accidents. Moreover, models with nearly identical primary scores can
exhibit markedly different calibration, while performance varies
substantially across population shifts. These findings show that primary
task performance alone is insufficient to assess autonomous clinical
modeling and multiple different metrics widely used in healthcare
practice need to be taken into consideration.

## 1 Introduction

Autonomous research agents can increasingly develop machine learning
models end to end, from data inspection and model selection to
implementation, validation, and iterative refinement ([Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3); [Miller et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). Such capabilities
are particularly consequential in medicine, where prediction models use
patient data to estimate clinical outcomes and inform patient care
([Steyerberg et al., 2009](#bib.bib31)). Automating their development
could reduce the effort required to build models for different outcomes
and patient populations, but it also delegates clinically consequential
modeling decisions to autonomous agents.¹¹ 1 Code:
[https://github.com/Edwin0080/Auto-MD-bench](https://github.com/Edwin0080/Auto-MD-bench).
Data for 10 of the 15 tasks:
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/Edwin0080/Auto-MD-bench](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Edwin0080/Auto-MD-bench).

In real-world medical settings, however, strong performance on a target
metric does not fully characterize a model’s suitability for medical
use. A model may distinguish patients at higher and lower risk while
providing inaccurate estimates of their absolute risk, potentially
misleading screening or treatment decisions ([Van Calster et al.,
2019](#bib.bib35)). Its performance may also change when applied to
patients from another cohort, hospital, or time period ([Royston and
Altman, 2013](#bib.bib29)). These aspects of model quality are not
necessarily captured by the primary task objective that typically guides
autonomous model development. This raises a key question: _are standard
task objectives sufficient to guide agents toward models suitable for
real-world medical use?_

Existing benchmarks evaluate autonomous model development across general
machine learning and biomedical tasks, but largely focus on primary task
performance. A unified evaluation of primary performance, risk
calibration, and external validation is still missing
(Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Evaluation for Medical Use. ‣ 2 Related Work")).
This leaves open whether models optimized for strong task performance
also satisfy broader requirements for medical use. We address this gap
by evaluating these properties together and analyzing how agents’
modeling choices relate to the resulting differences.

We introduce Auto-MD-bench, comprising 15 medical prediction tasks from
10 clinical datasets spanning survival analysis, classification, and
sequential prediction, with external validation across cohorts,
hospitals, and time periods. Agents choose their modeling and validation
procedures using labeled training data, while test outcomes remain
hidden throughout model development. We evaluate the resulting models on
primary task performance, risk calibration, and performance under
population shift, and analyze their code and development records to
characterize how the agents build and revise their models.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction") summarizes the benchmark
workflow.

We evaluate four agents: AIDE, AI Scientist v2, Codex, and Autoresearch.
Both Codex and Autoresearch exceed the simple baseline models in mean
primary score, while their mean calibration errors are roughly three
times as large. Across agents, models with similar primary scores can
exhibit markedly different calibration, and performance varies
substantially across population shifts. Analysis of the developed models
identifies modeling practices associated with these calibration
differences. In an additional experiment on UCI Diabetes, explicitly
including calibration in the stated objective changes agents’ modeling
choices and reduces mean ECE from 0.172 to 0.016 while maintaining
similar AUROC. Together, these results show that the primary task
objective provides an incomplete specification of model quality for
medical use.

Our contributions are:

- •
  We introduce Auto-MD-bench, a benchmark of 15 medical prediction tasks
  from 10 clinical datasets that evaluates autonomous model development
  beyond the primary task metric, including risk calibration and
  external validation across cohorts, hospitals, and time periods.
- •
  Across four agents, we show that strong primary performance can mask
  substantial differences in calibration and performance under
  population shift. We further show in a controlled experiment that
  explicitly incorporating calibration into the objective can
  substantially improve calibration while maintaining similar predictive
  discrimination.
- •
  We extend benchmark evaluation beyond final model outputs by capturing
  agents’ code and development records, enabling systematic analysis of
  how autonomous agents construct, validate, and revise medical
  prediction models.

## 2 Related Work

#### Autonomous Model Development.

Benchmarks for autonomous model development evaluate agents through
executable machine learning tasks and the performance of their resulting
solutions. MLAgentBench measures agents’ ability to improve existing
implementations, while MLE-bench evaluates end-to-end model development
through Kaggle competitions ([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Chan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). DSBench covers both data analysis and
predictive modeling ([Jing et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)). Beyond final task
performance, FML-bench compares research strategies within a common
experimental framework and analyzes how agents explore and select
candidate solutions ([Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)).

#### Automated Medical Model Development.

Automated medical model development spans conventional AutoML pipelines
and LLM-driven systems that generate and revise modeling code.
AutoPrognosis 2.0 automates preprocessing, model selection, and
hyperparameter tuning for diagnostic and prognostic modeling ([Imrie
et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)). More recent work has used LLMs to develop
clinical classifiers from natural language instructions ([Tayebi Arasteh
et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)), construct interpretable clinical scores
([Estévez et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), and revise cardiovascular
prediction models with evaluation on external cohorts ([Dhingra et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)).

#### Biomedical Agent Benchmarks.

Biomedical agent benchmarks cover activities ranging from model
development to coding-intensive data analysis and broader healthcare
workflows. BioML-bench and BioXArena evaluate model development across
diverse biomedical domains, including patient-level prediction ([Miller
et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). MedAgentGym
emphasizes code-centric biomedical data science, whereas
HealthAgentBench and MedAgentBoard evaluate agents across broader
healthcare workflows ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib37); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20); [Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib39)). AutoHealth and
ACP-Bench report calibration analyses alongside predictive performance
([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib36); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)), while
BioXArena includes evaluation across imaging centers ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)).
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Evaluation for Medical Use. ‣ 2 Related Work")
summarizes calibration and external validation in these benchmarks.

#### Evaluation for Medical Use.

Clinical prediction research distinguishes discrimination from risk
calibration ([Steyerberg et al., 2009](#bib.bib31); [Van Calster et al.,
2019](#bib.bib35)). External validation assesses performance across
patient populations ([Royston and Altman, 2013](#bib.bib29)). These
perspectives motivate evaluating agent-developed medical models beyond
primary task scores and relating their performance to modeling
practices.

## 3 The Benchmark

Auto-MD-bench evaluates autoresearch agents that develop models for
medical prediction tasks with a specified primary metric. To test
whether the primary metric adequately specifies model quality for
medical use, we assess primary task performance alongside risk
calibration and performance under population shift. We also examine the
agents’ code and development records to characterize their modeling
choices and relate them to calibration.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction") shows the workflow from
task inputs through autonomous model development to evaluation.

### 3.1 Medical Tasks for Autoresearch Agents

The benchmark comprises 15 medical prediction tasks from 10 clinical
datasets, using structured patient data to predict clinical outcomes.
The tasks cover survival analysis, classification and sequential
prediction. Agents provide survival curves for survival analysis, event
probabilities for classification, and hourly probabilities and alarms
for sequential prediction. The full task list, data sources, outcomes,
sample sizes, and primary metrics are reported in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Datasets")
(Table [8](#A1.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix A Datasets")).

Ten tasks use internal validation on test data from the source
population. Five use external validation on data from another cohort,
hospital or period. One external validation task also provides 500
labeled patients from the target hospital for model development.

### 3.2 Medical Task Workflow

Each task provides a description, labeled training data, test features,
null predictions in the required submission format, and a format
checker. The description specifies the prediction target, primary
performance metric and task rules. Agents choose their modeling
approach, write and execute code, train and validate models using the
training data, and revise their models using execution results and
validation scores. Test labels and test scores are unavailable
throughout model development.

Agents submit prediction files for evaluation ([Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)). For each run, we select one submission using the
agent-specific rules in
Table [10](#A3.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Appendix C Agent Configurations"). After
model development is complete, we score the selected predictions outside
the agent’s development environment. A run is valid if its selected
submission passes the submission format checks; missing, unreadable or
malformed files make it invalid.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Per-Task Results and Invalid Runs") gives
the detailed checks.

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

#### Predictive Performance.

We evaluate survival predictions with adjusted Antolini time-dependent
concordance $`C^{td}`$ ([Antolini et al., 2005](#bib.bib2)).
Classification tasks use AUROC, except for PhysioNet 2012, which uses
the minimum of sensitivity and positive predictive value ([Silva et al.,
2012](#bib.bib30)). Sequential prediction tasks use the PhysioNet/CinC
2019 normalized utility, which rewards timely alarms and penalizes false
and missed alarms ([Reyna et al., 2019b](#bib.bib28)).

We normalize each task’s raw score $`m`$ relative to the score $`m_{0}`$
of its null predictions:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s=\min\!\left(1,\max\!\left(0,\frac{m-m_{0}}{1-m_{0}}\right)\right).
``` |  | (1) |

Here, $`m_{0}=0.5`$ for $`C^{td}`$ and AUROC, and $`m_{0}=0`$ for the
other metrics. Penalized scores assign zero to invalid runs;
non-penalized scores use valid runs only. Both weight tasks equally
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Performance on Medical Tasks ‣ 4 Experiments")).
See Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Per-Task Results and Invalid Runs") for
details.

#### Simple Baseline Models.

We use Cox neural networks implemented in pycox as simple baseline
models for survival analysis ([Katzman et al., 2018](#bib.bib16);
[Kvamme et al., 2019](#bib.bib17)) and scikit-learn’s histogram-based
gradient-boosted trees for classification and sequential prediction.
Each simple baseline model uses fixed hyperparameters and the task’s
training data, and is evaluated with the same metrics and evaluation
code as the agent-developed models. We train each simple baseline model
five times per task and average its predictive scores and calibration
errors across these repetitions.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Simple Baseline Models") gives the model
configurations and training procedures.

#### Risk Calibration.

We use expected calibration error (ECE) to compare predicted
probabilities with observed outcome frequencies
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")).
For survival analysis, we group patients by predicted survival
probability at each of three time points. We compare the mean prediction
in each group with its Kaplan–Meier survival estimate, which accounts
for censoring. For sequential prediction, ECE is computed over
patient-hours.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Calibration and Subgroup Performance")
defines the bins, time points and aggregation.

#### External Validation.

We report normalized scores on the five external validation tasks and
their ratios to the corresponding scores without the population shift
(Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")).
Agents develop models separately for the internal and external
validation tasks. We compare these ratios with those of the simple
baseline models.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Per-Task Results and Invalid Runs") gives
the calculation.

#### Subgroup Performance.

We compare performance across age, sex, and recorded race/ethnicity
groups, using AUROC for classification and time-dependent concordance
for survival analysis.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Calibration and Subgroup Performance")
gives the group definitions and calculations.

#### Modeling Methodology.

For valid submissions, we examine the prediction code and supporting
development records to identify model families, validation strategies,
and approaches to class imbalance and censoring. We use Claude Opus 5.5
for annotation, with each annotation citing supporting code by file and
line number. We assess annotation consistency through repeated
annotation
(Table [21](#A9.T21 "Table 21 ‣ Calibration Differences by Modeling Practice. ‣ Appendix I Modeling Methods and Code Annotation")).
We examine how using averaged ranks as probabilities, class imbalance
handling and recalibration relate to differences in ECE from the simple
baseline models
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")).
Table [6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.5 Machine Learning Model Choice by Different Autoresearch Agents ‣ 4 Experiments")
summarizes the modeling methods, and
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Modeling Methods and Code Annotation")
defines the calibration groups. We also compare the code for sampled
pairs of submission versions to characterize modeling changes during
agent search
(Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.5 Machine Learning Model Choice by Different Autoresearch Agents ‣ 4 Experiments");
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Model Development")).

#### Real-Time Rule and Masking Test.

At hour $`t`$, sequential predictions may use only the patient’s
observations through $`t`$ and information learned from the training
data. To detect violations of this rule, we mask later observations by
removing the end of each patient’s record, rerun the code that produced
the selected submissions, and compare alarms at the retained hours. We
apply this test to valid submissions from AIDE and AI Scientist v2 on
the three sequential prediction tasks.
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Censoring and Real-Time Rule Violations")
describes the procedure and reports the results.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Autoresearch Agents and Harness.

We evaluate four autoresearch agents: Codex, Autoresearch, AIDE, and AI
Scientist v2. Codex is a general-purpose coding agent ([OpenAI,
2025](#bib.bib23)). Autoresearch uses Codex with the autoresearch
protocol ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib15)), which instructs Codex to build
an initial model, then propose and evaluate one change at a time,
retaining changes that improve the primary metric on its validation
data. AIDE uses greedy tree search ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)),
whereas AI Scientist v2 uses best-first tree search organized into four
stages: initial implementation, tuning, experimentation, and ablation
([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)).

#### Experimental Conditions.

In the main experiments, all four agents use Qwen3.8 Flash ([Alibaba
Cloud, 2026](#bib.bib1)). We evaluate each agent three times on each of
the 15 tasks, with a 12-hour wall-clock budget for each repetition. Each
repetition uses a dedicated virtual machine with eight CPU threads and
16 GB of RAM. All agents receive the same task description and data for
a given task. They run in Apptainer containers with the same
preinstalled software and no internet access.

#### Implementation Details.

We adapt each framework’s input interface to provide the benchmark’s
task descriptions and data. For AI Scientist v2, we use its experiment
stages and disable paper writing and review. To apply the 12-hour
budget, we raise AIDE’s iteration limit and allocate time across AI
Scientist v2’s stages in proportion to their original iteration quotas.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Agent Configurations") provides
configuration and implementation details, including software versions, a
task description example, and the autoresearch instructions.

High primary scores do not imply well-calibrated predictions. Codex and
Autoresearch exceed the simple baseline models in mean primary score,
but have roughly three times their average ECE (0.086 and 0.075 versus
0.027;
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")). For
example, two individual UCI Diabetes submissions from Codex and AI

ECE values of 0.006 and 0.154, respectively.

Most of the ECE difference from the simple baseline models is
concentrated in two groups of experiments
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")).
Experiments using averaged ranks as probabilities account for 75% of
this difference and 14% of valid experiments. Among the remaining
experiments, those that handle class imbalance without recalibration
account for a further 18% of the difference and 9% of all valid
experiments. Averaged ranks express relative ordering rather than event
probabilities, while handling class imbalance changes the relative
weight of events and non-events during training and can distort the
resulting probabilities. For experiments that handle class imbalance but
do not use averaged ranks, mean ECE is 0.008 with subsequent
recalibration and 0.160 without it.

To test whether explicitly specifying calibration changes model
development, we compare the main experiment’s AUROC objective on UCI
Diabetes with two alternatives: Brier score, which reflects both
discrimination and calibration, and AUROC with ECE. We specify each
objective in the task description and use three repetitions of each of
the four agents, with the same LLM, data, and time budget. Adding ECE to
the stated AUROC objective reduces mean ECE from 0.172 to 0.016 while
maintaining similar mean AUROC (0.676 versus 0.683;
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")). In
this condition, neither using ranks as probabilities nor handling class
imbalance without recalibration is observed. With Brier score, mean ECE
also decreases, to 0.061, but mean AUROC is lower at 0.640. These
results show that including calibration in the stated objective can
change modeling choices and improve calibration on this task.

### 4.4 Generalization across Different Populations

#### External Validation.

Performance under population shift varies substantially across tasks
(Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments")). On
Rotterdam $`\to`$ GBSG, scores under population shift are 85–86% of the
corresponding scores without the shift for the research agents and
97–99% for the coding agents. Ratios are lower on both sepsis tasks:
41–63% on *Sepsis, new hospital* and 34–67% on *Sepsis, 500 local
labels*. On *SEER, later period* and *eICU, new hospitals*, performance
changes little under the population shift, with ratios close to 100%.

Codex and Autoresearch achieve higher scores under population shift than
the two research agents on all five tasks. Codex exceeds the simple
baseline score on every task, while Autoresearch exceeds it on four and
closely matches it on Rotterdam $`\to`$ GBSG. The corresponding ratios
for Codex and Autoresearch are also close to those of the simple
baseline models. The ratios for AIDE and AI Scientist v2 are
approximately 8 and 9 percentage points lower than the corresponding
simple baseline ratios, respectively, on average. These results show
that performance under population shift is an additional property that
must be evaluated rather than inferred from the primary score alone.

#### Subgroup Performance.

Aggregate scores also mask differences between patient subgroups. In the
four ICU mortality tasks, the oldest age tertile is the lowest-scoring
age group in every valid experiment (Appendix
Table [14](#A6.T14 "Table 14 ‣ Subgroups. ‣ Appendix F Calibration and Subgroup Performance")).
The mean difference between the highest- and lowest-scoring age groups
ranges from 0.065 to 0.084, similar to the 0.065–0.078 differences for
the simple baseline models. Across all tasks, agents check performance
by age or sex in only six of 168 valid experiments.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Calibration and Subgroup Performance")
reports the full subgroup results, including sex and race/ethnicity.

We examine the methods used to build these models to assess their

#### Model Families and Ensembles.

Gradient-boosted trees dominate model choice, appearing in 76–89% of
valid experiments across the four agents
(Table [6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.5 Machine Learning Model Choice by Different Autoresearch Agents ‣ 4 Experiments")).
The agents differ more in how they combine models: Codex and
Autoresearch average predictions across seeds or folds in 89% of
experiments, compared with 62% for AIDE and 31% for AI Scientist v2. AI
Scientist v2 uses a single model in 36% of experiments, compared with
2–13% for the other agents.

#### Validation and Calibration.

AI Scientist v2 also relies on a single holdout split more often: 37% of
experiments, versus 4–13% for the other agents. Probability calibration
is uncommon across all four agents, appearing in 2–22% of experiments.

#### Modeling Changes during Agent Search.

Changes to ensembling are frequent for Codex and Autoresearch, appearing
in 64–89% of sampled pairs
(Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.5 Machine Learning Model Choice by Different Autoresearch Agents ‣ 4 Experiments")).
Hyperparameter tuning is less frequent in late than in early pairs for
all four agents. Feature changes also decline in Codex and Autoresearch,
reaching 3–4% in late pairs. AI Scientist v2, which follows a predefined
four-stage workflow, instead shows more feature changes in later pairs.

#### Censoring and Temporal Information.

Code review identifies errors in handling censored outcomes in eight of
the 79 valid survival submissions. The errors occur in ensemble members
and include treating censored patients as observed deaths or as known
survivors at later time points, and using incorrect censoring weights.
All eight submissions exceed their task’s simple baseline score despite
these errors. Thus, success on the primary score can coexist with errors
in how the underlying medical prediction problem is modeled.

The masking test detects violations of the real-time rule in three of
the 13 valid sequential prediction experiments tested for AIDE and AI
Scientist v2. All three occur in AIDE and use information from later
hours to determine earlier predictions or alarms.
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Censoring and Real-Time Rule Violations")
reports the cases and test results.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced Auto-MD-bench to examine whether the common practice of
guiding autonomous agents with a single primary metric is sufficient for
developing models that satisfy broader requirements for medical use.
Across 15 tasks from 10 clinical datasets, models with similar primary
performance could differ markedly in risk calibration and in performance
under population shift. Our analysis connected these differences to
concrete modeling choices, while explicitly incorporating calibration
into model development substantially improved calibration while
maintaining similar discrimination. These results show that primary task
performance provides only a partial view of the models produced by
autonomous agents. More broadly, the metric used to guide model
development can influence which properties of the resulting model are
prioritized. Evaluating autonomous medical model development should
therefore look beyond a single primary metric and consider the broader
properties required by the intended medical use.

### A.1 Survival Analysis

#### SUPPORT and METABRIC.

These tasks predict time to death from the processed datasets
distributed with pycox ([Connors et al., 1995](#bib.bib5); [Curtis
et al., 2012](#bib.bib6)). SUPPORT has 14 clinical covariates and
follow-up in days; METABRIC has four gene-expression markers and five
clinical covariates, with follow-up in months. Both use generic
covariate names and unstratified patient splits.

#### Rotterdam and Rotterdam $`\to`$ GBSG.

The Rotterdam and GBSG cohorts are distributed together in the R
survival package. Both tasks predict recurrence or death from eight
harmonized clinical covariates ([Royston and Altman, 2013](#bib.bib29)).
For Rotterdam, follow-up is the minimum of the recorded recurrence and
death times in days; either outcome counts as an event. The internal
task splits Rotterdam patients with stratification by event status. The
external task trains on the full Rotterdam cohort and tests on the
independent GBSG cohort, using its supplied recurrence-free survival
time and event status.

#### SEER and SEER, Later Period.

The cohort includes women with a first malignant breast-cancer diagnosis
in 2010–2015. Inputs are 22 coded demographic, tumor and treatment
variables ([Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program(2026),
SEER](#bib.bib33)). The outcome is breast-cancer death, with follow-up
in months; deaths from other causes are censored at death. We exclude
deaths with unknown cause and retain records flagged as having complete
dates and positive follow-up. Both tasks test on the same randomly
selected half of patients diagnosed in 2014–2015. SEER trains on all
remaining patients, whereas *SEER, later period* trains only on
diagnoses from 2010–2013.

#### NHANES.

The task predicts all-cause mortality after examination from the
2011–2016 survey cycles linked to mortality follow-up through 2019
([National Center for Health Statistics Division of Analysis and
Epidemiology, 2022](#bib.bib22)). We retain linkage-eligible adults aged
at least 20 years with positive follow-up and examination weights. The
25 inputs include clinical and demographic variables, survey weight,
stratum, primary sampling unit and cycle. Follow-up is measured in
months, and the patient split is stratified by event status.

#### Survival Time Grids.

The time grid specifies when models must report survival probabilities.
We use the unique quantiles of event times in the training data at 100
evenly spaced levels from 0 to 1, adding time zero. The two SEER tasks
share the grid from the 2010–2013 training data.

### A.2 Classification

#### UCI Diabetes.

The task predicts readmission within 30 days of a hospital encounter
([Strack et al., 2014](#bib.bib32)). We remove three fields with more
than 40% missing values and ordinal-encode categorical variables,
leaving 44 predictors. Patients are split with stratification by whether
they ever have a positive readmission label; all encounters of a patient
remain together. Patient identifiers are retained in training data and
removed from test features.

#### PhysioNet 2012.

The task predicts in-hospital death from irregular observations over the
first 48 hours of an ICU stay ([Silva et al., 2012](#bib.bib30)). Inputs
are supplied in long format with observation times, variable names and
values. Challenge set A supplies training data; sets B and C together
form the test set.

#### MIMIC-IV.

The task predicts in-hospital death for adults whose first ICU stay
lasts at least 24 hours ([Johnson et al., 2023](#bib.bib14)). Its 73
inputs combine first-24-hour vital-sign and laboratory summaries with
demographics and additional clinical variables. The patient split is
stratified by the mortality label.

#### eICU and eICU, New Hospitals.

Both tasks predict in-hospital death for adult ICU stays of at least one
day with APACHE predictor records ([Pollard et al., 2018](#bib.bib26)).
Their 80 inputs combine first-24-hour vital-sign and laboratory
summaries with demographics, clinical assessments and admission
information. We randomly select hospitals until they cover at least 20%
of cohort stays, then use half of their patients for the shared test
set. The eICU task trains on all non-test patients; *eICU, new
hospitals* trains only on the other hospitals. Every stay of a test
patient is excluded from both training sets. Hospital identifiers are
not supplied as predictors.

### A.3 Sequential Prediction

#### Hourly Inputs and Target.

All three tasks use PhysioNet/CinC 2019 records from hospitals A and B
([Reyna et al., 2019b](#bib.bib28)). Each patient-hour has 40 variables
covering vital signs, laboratory measurements and demographic or
administrative information, plus a hospital indicator. The supplied
label becomes positive six hours before sepsis onset and remains
positive thereafter.

#### Internal and External Tasks.

The *Sepsis* task splits patients from both hospitals, stratified by
hospital and whether they ever develop sepsis. The two external tasks
use the hospital-B patients in this test set as their shared test set.
*Sepsis, new hospital* trains on all hospital-A patients. *Sepsis, 500
local labels* adds 500 labeled hospital-B patients sampled from outside
the test set, stratified by whether they ever develop sepsis.
````

## Appendix D Simple Baseline Models

#### Training and Aggregation.

The simple baseline models use the same training data and evaluation
code as the agents’ models. We train each model with random seeds 0–4
and average the five separately computed scores and calibration errors.
Model settings are fixed across tasks within each family.

#### Survival Analysis.

We use the pycox Cox proportional-hazards model with two hidden layers
of 32 units, batch normalization, dropout 0.1 and no output bias.
Training uses Adam with learning rate $`10^{-3}`$, batch size 256 and 64
epochs on the full training set, without early stopping. For SUPPORT, we
standardize columns x0 and x7–x13 using training-set statistics and
retain x1–x6 as supplied. For the other survival tasks, we one-hot
encode nonnumeric columns using the training categories, impute missing
values with training medians, and standardize features. We estimate the
baseline hazard from the training data and evaluate survival curves on
the supplied time grid as right-continuous step functions.

#### Classification.

We use HistGradientBoostingClassifier with 300 boosting iterations,
learning rate 0.1 and early stopping disabled. For UCI Diabetes,
MIMIC-IV and both eICU tasks, the model uses the supplied numeric
features, excluding the outcome, record identifiers and the UCI patient
grouping identifier. The estimator handles missing values. For PhysioNet
2012, we summarize each measured variable by its mean, minimum, maximum
and last value over the observation window and use these summaries as
predictors.

#### Sequential Prediction.

We sort records by patient and ICU hour and forward-fill measurements
within each patient. Features include the current-hour measurements, ICU
hour and a hospital indicator; columns with no observed training values
are excluded. We use HistGradientBoostingClassifier with the
classification settings above and a maximum of 31 leaves per tree. We
reserve 15% of training patients, stratified by whether sepsis occurs,
to select the alarm threshold. Among 40 quantiles from 0.80 to 0.999 of
the holdout probabilities, we choose the threshold with the highest
utility. The model trained on the remaining patients produces the test
predictions. We repeat this procedure for each task, including the 500
labeled target-hospital patients where provided.

## Appendix E Per-Task Results and Invalid Runs

#### Submission Validity.

A run is valid if its selected submission has exactly the required
columns and test-set identifiers, with no duplicate identifiers.
Probabilities must be finite and in $`[0,1]`$, binary predictions must
be in $`\{0,1\}`$, and survival curves must be non-increasing, as
applicable. Missing, unreadable or malformed files make a run invalid.
Masking-test results do not change validity or scores.

#### Score Aggregation.

Penalized scores assign zero to invalid runs. For each agent and
repetition, we average normalized scores equally across tasks, then
report the mean and sample SD of the three averages. Non-penalized
scores first average valid runs within each task, then weight tasks
equally. Their SD is reported only when all runs are valid. Negative
sequential-prediction utility scores are clipped to zero in the reported
primary-score tables. Under each convention, we rank agents within each
task by their mean normalized score. Rank 1 is best, ties receive their
average rank, and the simple baseline model is not ranked.

## Appendix F Calibration and Subgroup Performance

#### Expected Calibration Error.

We compute ECE from the selected predictions in each valid run. For
classification and sequential prediction, we partition probabilities
into ten equal-width bins and compute

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b}\frac{n_{b}}{N}\left|\bar{p}_{b}-\bar{y}_{b}\right|,
``` |  |

where $`n_{b}`$ is the number of observations in bin $`b`$, $`N`$ is the
total number of observations, and $`\bar{p}_{b}`$ and $`\bar{y}_{b}`$
are the bin’s mean predicted probability and event frequency. Empty bins
contribute zero. For sequential prediction, observations are
patient-hours. We evaluate averaged ranks submitted as probabilities
without applying recalibration.

For survival analysis, we use the grid points nearest the 25th, 50th and
75th percentiles of test event times. At each time, we sort patients by
predicted survival into ten equal-size groups and compare mean predicted
survival with the within-group Kaplan–Meier estimate. We weight absolute
differences by group size and average the resulting errors over the
three times. Groups with fewer than five patients are skipped.

For Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.3 Risk Calibration ‣ 4 Experiments"),
we first average valid runs within each task, then weight tasks equally
within each class or across all tasks. All simple baseline calibration
errors are averaged over the same five repetitions per task as the
simple baseline scores.

Classification subgroup scores use AUROC, including for PhysioNet 2012.
We divide age into test-set thirds and retain the four largest
race/ethnicity categories, grouping the remainder as *Other*. Subgroup
metrics require at least 50 observations for AUROC and 80 for survival
concordance. Survival subgroup scores use Antolini concordance without
the adjustment used for the primary score.

### F.1 Effect of Evaluation Objectives

#### Setup.

We compare modeling choices and predictions under three evaluation
objectives on UCI Diabetes, the binary 30-day readmission task. Each
group contains three repetitions of AIDE, Codex, Autoresearch and AI
Scientist v2, all using Qwen3.8 Flash with a 12-hour budget. Group A
reuses the main experiment’s AUROC runs; groups B and C are new runs
with the same data, submission format and task rules. The task
descriptions specify:

- •
  A: maximize AUROC.
- •
  B: minimize Brier score.
- •
  C: maximize AUROC and minimize ECE, using ten equal-width bins.

Group C provides no weights or rule for combining the two objectives.
For Autoresearch, we also change the protocol’s instruction to “maximize
its primary metric” to “minimize its primary metric” in B and “optimize
its primary metrics” in C.

|     |                                                            |     |

       ```                                                         |     |

#### Masking Procedure.

To detect violations of the real-time rule, we remove later records from
each test patient’s time series. For a patient with $`n`$ hourly
records, we draw $`u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0.40,0.90)`$ and retain the
first $`\min(n,\max(4,\lfloor un\rfloor))`$ records in time order. One
masked test file is reused across runs of the same task. Training data
and all other task files, including the sample prediction file, remain
unchanged. We rerun the code producing the selected submission and
compare alarms at retained hours with those from the complete records.

We compute the fraction of changed alarms over all retained rows and
over the last retained row of each patient. A fraction above 0.1% on
either measure flags a violation; zero on both is reported as *no
change*. For the detected violations, rerunning the code on the
unmodified input changed no alarms.

Among the 13 valid AIDE and AI Scientist v2 runs tested, three AIDE runs
violate the rule: one on *Sepsis, new hospital* and two on *Sepsis, 500
local labels*. One AI Scientist v2 rerun on the full input did not
reproduce the selected predictions; its masked and full reruns
nevertheless agreed on all retained alarms.

#### Code Annotation.

We use Claude Opus 5.5 to annotate the code and execution records for
the selected submissions from all 180 runs. Method frequencies and
modeling-practice statistics use the 168 valid runs. For AIDE and AI
Scientist v2, the inputs include the selected node’s script and

and commands that produced the selected prediction file. Each annotation
gives a categorical response and cites supporting evidence by file and
line number.

The codebooks cover model families, features, missing-data handling,
validation, tuning, ensembling and output processing, together with
modeling practices such as handling censoring and class imbalance. We
record practices used to produce the submitted predictions separately
from exploratory checks, such as evaluating demographic subgroups. The
practice codebook permits uncertain and not-applicable responses.
Changes during model development are annotated from sampled version
pairs (Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Model Development")).

runs for modeling methods and 36 for modeling practices
(Table [21](#A9.T21 "Table 21 ‣ Calibration Differences by Modeling Practice. ‣ Appendix I Modeling Methods and Code Annotation")).

instructions not to consult the first annotations. Agreement was 124 of
130 method fields and 273 of 288 modeling-practice fields. The
modeling-practice comparison excludes free-text fields and treats
````
