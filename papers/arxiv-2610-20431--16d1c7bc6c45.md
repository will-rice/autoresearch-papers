###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in scientific research aims to improve
agents’ research capabilities through their own experimental experience.
Scientific agent swarms can pursue this goal when one agent’s findings
and implementations advance another’s research. Two obstacles stand in
the way. A failed experiment does not reveal whether its hypothesis or
its implementation was at fault, and a program without overall gains can
hide components that other agents need. We introduce SwarmRSI, a
framework in which a swarm improves its research by evolving the
research states its agents inherit, including hypotheses, programs and
experimental evidence. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’
feedback to decide whether to revise a hypothesis or its program.
Complementary Swarm Recombination tests retained hypotheses on other
agents’ programs and combines complementary components. Recursive Swarm
Evolution returns every outcome to a shared graph that guides which
state each agent updates next. SwarmRSI achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA)
results on OpenProblems and NatureBench. With Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, it
reaches an average score of 76.2 on OpenProblems, and it matches the
published SOTA on 44.4% of NatureBench tasks, the highest Match-SOTA
rate among compared methods. On MLE-Bench Lite, it wins medals on 81.8%
of tasks, above hypothesis and program evolution agents with the same
backbone. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one seed over all 90 NatureBench
tasks, about 77% of final best programs descend from a rebase, a
crossover or an update triggered by feedback. Biologists reproduce two
of its programs and use them in follow-up analyses of intestinal and
splenic immunity.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Two limitations motivating SwarmRSI. In the top example, a

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in scientific research aims to improve
agents’ research capabilities through their own experimental
experience ([Guo et al., 2026a](#bib.bib22); [Srikanth et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23)). Scientific agent swarms offer a way to pursue this
goal by coordinating idea generation, implementation, and evaluation
across specialized agents ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Tang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib48)). Persistent memories and shared histories retain
experimental experience for later research ([Lyu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21); [Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17); [Weng et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)), and accumulated
histories can also support improvements to exploration
strategies ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)). Together, these
capabilities create an opportunity for agents exploring different
directions to use one another’s findings and implementations in
subsequent research.

However, sharing experience does not ensure that agents use it to make
better research decisions. Program evolution refines executable
candidates through execution feedback ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10);
[Nam et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Liu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)), while hypothesis evolution improves research
directions through iterative generation and evaluation ([Gottweis et
al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib45); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). As
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
illustrates, promising hypotheses are abandoned after inadequate
implementations, and useful components are overlooked in programs that
perform poorly overall.

We hypothesize that these limitations arise from two interrelated
dependencies in scientific research. The value of a hypothesis depends
on how it is implemented and how rigorously it is tested. An
unsuccessful experiment therefore leaves unclear whether the research
direction or its implementation needs revision ([Ma et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31); [Koo et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)), and feedback from
other agents identifies which changes are worth pursuing. Intermediate
results also become stepping stones for other search paths ([Wang et
al., 2019](#bib.bib32); [Wang et al., 2020](#bib.bib33)), and their
value depends on combinations with later findings or implementations.
Their current performance thus underestimates their contribution to
other agents’ future research. This leads to our central question of how
scientific agent swarms can evolve toward recursive self-improvement.

To address this, we introduce SwarmRSI, a framework that evolves
scientific agent swarms for recursive self-improvement. Each agent uses
its hypotheses, programs, experimental evidence, and unresolved needs to
plan further research, and a shared graph records these inheritable
research states. In Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation, agents revise
hypotheses and programs using other agents’ experimental feedback.
Performance differences inform hypothesis revision, and unmet research
needs guide implementation changes. In Complementary Swarm
Recombination, agents revisit retained hypotheses on other agents’
implementations and combine program components with complementary
strengths. Recursive Swarm Evolution evaluates the resulting candidates,
retains useful results, feedback and unresolved needs in the shared
graph, and uses these states to decide which hypotheses agents revisit
and which programs they refine. Each experiment thus updates the states
that later agents start from.

We evaluate SwarmRSI on OpenProblems ([Luecken et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7)), NatureBench ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)) and
MLE-Bench Lite ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)), 118 scientific coding
and machine-learning engineering tasks, with two backbones. It achieves
SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench and outperforms hypothesis
and program evolution agents with the same backbone. With
DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one seed over all 90 NatureBench tasks, about 77% of
its final best programs descend from a rebase, a crossover or an update
triggered by feedback. Biologists reproduce two of its programs and use
them in follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity.

Our main contributions are:

- •
  We formulate RSI for scientific agent swarms as evolving inheritable
  research states, so that each experiment updates the hypotheses,
  programs and evidence that later agents start from.
- •
  We introduce SwarmRSI, which updates these states through
  Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation, Complementary Swarm Recombination and
  Recursive Swarm Evolution.
- •
  SwarmRSI achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench, most
  of its final NatureBench programs build on inherited states, and
  biologists reproduce its programs for immune analyses.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 LLM Agents for Scientific Research

LLM agents automate scientific research through hypothesis generation,
experiment implementation, and result evaluation ([Tang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)). Agents centred on
programs search over or refine code with execution feedback ([Jiang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Nam et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), R&D-Agent
coordinates proposal generation and implementation through specialized
roles ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)), and systems with memory carry
execution experience into later experiments ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12); [Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). Closest to our
setting, Arbor links explicit hypotheses to programs and experimental
evidence in a persistent hypothesis tree ([Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)), and SAGE evaluates competing failure explanations to
direct interventions toward hypotheses, experimental design, or
implementation ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)). SwarmRSI likewise
grounds hypotheses in experiments and lets agents use one another’s
feedback and partial results.

### 2.2 Recursive Self-Improvement of Agents

Recursive self-improvement uses an agent’s own experience to improve the
mechanisms that guide its subsequent activity. AIDE² retains accepted
code modifications as the basis for further self-improvement ([Srikanth
et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)), Dream-RSI refines exploration policies with
replay simulators built from historical discovery trees ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)), MASS alternates workflow evolution with model
fine-tuning ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)), and AQuA uses recorded
experiments to guide later research proposals ([Guo et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib22)). These methods improve the agent’s code, policy,
workflow, or proposals. SwarmRSI instead evolves the research states
that agents inherit, revising them with other agents’ feedback and
reusing retained hypotheses and program components across agents.

## 3 SwarmRSI

SwarmRSI evolves scientific agent swarms by updating the research states
agents inherit. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’
feedback to guide revisions, and Complementary Swarm Recombination
reuses their hypotheses and program components. Recursive Swarm
Evolution evaluates every candidate and retains its outcome, so each
experiment informs subsequent research.
[Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
shows the shared research state and the execution loop that updates it.

### 3.1 Preliminaries

Scientific research task. Given a research goal $`q`$, a starter program
$`p_{0}`$, and budget $`B`$, we seek a valid program with high utility.
A hypothesis $`h`$ specifies a modeling or algorithmic mechanism, and a
program $`p`$ implements it under the task’s constraints. Different
programs may test the same hypothesis. Let
$`\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{valid}}(B)`$ contain the valid programs found and
evaluated within budget. The system returns

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p_{B}^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{valid}}(B)}u(p),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`u(p)`$ is the utility measured under the task’s evaluation
protocol.

Scientific agent swarm. A scientific agent is a research policy for
hypothesis proposal, program development, or evidence analysis. At
research step $`t`$, the acting agent uses a research state

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}=(\mathcal{H}_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t},\mathcal{U}_{t}),
``` |  | (2) |

containing the hypotheses, programs, experimental evidence, and
unresolved needs available for its current action. It assembles this
state from relevant records in the shared graph $`\mathcal{G}_{t}`$,
including other agents’ results, and unresolved research needs. Each
record $`\xi_{i}=(H_{i},P_{i},X_{i},E_{i})`$ contains the tested
hypothesis, parent and resulting programs, outcome, and execution and
implementation evidence. The graph retains failed attempts and attempts
without gain, and its edges track hypothesis–program associations and
parent or reuse relations. Mutation and recombination use $`s_{t}`$ to
generate subsequent candidates.

### 3.2 Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation

To guide revisions of hypotheses and their implementations,
Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses experimental feedback from the
swarm. An unsuccessful run points to an implementation barrier, and
differences across instances point to a revised research direction. At
step $`t`$, the agent uses the selected parent pair $`(h_{t},p_{t})`$
and state $`s_{t}`$, which provides outcomes, implementation evidence,
and unresolved needs from its own and other agents’ records.

The agent proposes a hypothesis and a revision plan:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(\tilde{h}_{t},\delta_{t})=\operatorname{Propose}(q,h_{t},p_{t};s_{t}),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\delta_{t}`$ describes the intended changes and their rationale.
Differences across instances guide hypothesis revisions, and execution
failures or unrealized interventions guide program repair. We call a
revision that changes the hypothesis an *H-mutation*, and one that keeps
it ($`\tilde{h}_{t}=h_{t}`$) and repairs only the implementation a
*P-mutation*. The agent then implements the plan on the parent program
using relevant state information:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{p}_{t}=\operatorname{Implement}(p_{t},\tilde{h}_{t},\delta_{t};s_{t}).
``` |  | (4) |

The resulting candidate $`(\tilde{h}_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})`$ retains its
parent relation and revision rationale in the shared research state. It
then enters the shared evaluation loop, which tests validity and
measures utility.

### 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination

Complementary Swarm Recombination reuses partial results across agents,
including programs whose overall scores did not improve.

Hypothesis reuse. For a hypothesis $`h_{t}`$ with unresolved
implementation needs, an agent selects a valid base $`b_{t}`$ from
another agent by matching these needs to program mechanisms ([Wiegand et
al., 2001](#bib.bib36)). The *rebase* operation keeps $`h_{t}`$ and
adapts its implementation to $`b_{t}`$, giving
$`(\tilde{h}_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})=(h_{t},\operatorname{Rebase}(h_{t},b_{t};s_{t}))`$.

Program recombination. *Crossover* combines programs with complementary
strengths across instances ([Holland, 1975](#bib.bib37); [Goldberg,
1989](#bib.bib38)). For each dataset or subtask $`k`$, let
$`p_{k}^{\star}`$ be the valid program with the highest utility
$`u_{k}`$ observed by step $`t`$. Donors are the programs that
outperform the parent on some instance, ranked by their largest
advantage,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(p_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{p_{k}^{\star}:u_{k}(p_{k}^{\star})>u_{k}(p_{t})\},`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{t}(d)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max_{k:p_{k}^{\star}=d}\bigl[u_{k}(d)-u_{k}(p_{t})\bigr].`$ |  |  |

Each instance takes its components from the highest ranked donor that
outperforms the parent there and keeps the parent’s components
elsewhere. Both operations record their sources and send the new
candidate to the shared evaluation loop.

### 3.4 Recursive Swarm Evolution

Recursive Swarm Evolution decides which update to make next and returns
every outcome to the shared graph. It maintains a hypothesis frontier
$`\mathcal{F}_{H}`$ of active hypotheses and a program frontier
$`\mathcal{F}_{P}`$ of valid program representatives. For *H/P
selection*, which decides whether the next step revises a hypothesis (H)
or a program (P), it scores each feasible frontier $`f\in\{H,P\}`$ with
a discounted-UCB index ([Auer et al., 2002](#bib.bib34); [Garivier and
Moulines, 2011](#bib.bib35))

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{f}=\hat{\mu}_{f}+\beta\sqrt{\frac{\log(1+N_{H}+N_{P})}{N_{f}}}+\gamma\psi_{f},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\hat{\mu}_{f}`$ is recent improvement per token cost, $`N_{f}`$
is the discounted experiment count, and $`\psi_{f}`$ is the saturated
pressure from unresolved needs that $`f`$ can address. The weights
$`\beta`$ and $`\gamma`$ set exploration and request pressure. Untried
frontiers and frontiers that have waited long take priority over the
score.

Each candidate is executed and evaluated under the task protocol.
Execution checks test completion and task compliance, and source and
output comparisons check whether the intended change was implemented.
The record $`\xi_{t}`$ of every attempt, including failed ones and those
without gain, updates the graph to $`\mathcal{G}_{t+1}`$, from which the
next acting agent assembles its state $`s_{t+1}`$. An implementation
failure keeps the hypothesis and requests program repair, mixed gains
and losses across instances request a revised hypothesis, and a new
incumbent requests that retained hypotheses be realized on it. The loop
repeats until budget $`B`$ is exhausted and returns the best valid
program.

applications through case studies: RQ1: How does SwarmRSI perform across
benchmarks and backbones, and at what token cost? RQ2: How do

Recursive Swarm Evolution contribute to performance? RQ3: How do
experimental feedback and reused intermediate results guide the swarm’s
subsequent research within and across tasks? RQ4: What are the main
failure modes, and how frequent are they?

### 4.2 Benchmarks

We evaluate SwarmRSI on scientific coding and machine-learning
engineering tasks across three benchmarks. OpenProblems ([Luecken et
al., 2025](#bib.bib7)) contains six single-cell analysis tasks following
TusoAI ([Turcan et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). NatureBench ([Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6)) contains all 90 scientific coding tasks across six
domains. MLE-Bench Lite ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) contains 22
competitions from its low-complexity split.

### 4.3 Baselines

We compare against program evolution agents (AIDE and MLEvolve),
hypothesis evolution agents (Arbor), domain-specific scientific agents
(TusoAI and Biomni ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib8))), and general
coding agents (Claude Code ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib26)),
Codex ([OpenAI, 2026a](#bib.bib27)) and Gemini CLI ([Google,
2026](#bib.bib28))).

### 4.4 Evaluation Metrics

OpenProblems reports normalized task scores scaled by 100 and their
unweighted mean. NatureBench reports Match-SOTA ($`g\geq 0`$) and
Surpass-SOTA ($`g>0.1`$) rates, where $`g`$ is the official
direction-adjusted improvement over SOTA. MLE-Bench Lite reports Above
Median and medal rates from official grading on the private test split.
Rates retain all tasks in the denominator, including invalid or missing
results. Match-SOTA comparisons with Arbor and AIDE on NatureBench use
95% paired bootstrap confidence intervals and McNemar tests, and other
comparisons are descriptive. Token cost sums uncached input and output
tokens over all LLM calls in a search.

### 4.5 Implementation Details

SwarmRSI and rerun baselines use DeepSeek-V4-Pro ([DeepSeek,
2026](#bib.bib39)) under matched settings for each benchmark. Results
from our runs in
Tables [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")–[3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
are averaged over three random seeds. Published results keep the
settings of their sources. We additionally evaluate SwarmRSI with
Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview ([Google DeepMind, 2026](#bib.bib40)). In each
run, all agents share one backbone and advance lineages that exchange
results through the shared graph.

## 5 Results and Analysis

To answer our research questions, we compare benchmark performance,
conduct component ablations, examine search trajectories, analyze
annotated failures, and report biological case studies.

### 5.1 Main Results (RQ1)

[Tables 2](#S3.T2 "In 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
[2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
and [3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
summarize benchmark performance, and we make three observations.

- •
  SwarmRSI outperforms domain-specific and general coding agents. With
  DeepSeek-V4-Pro, it reaches an OpenProblems average of 74.2 against
  67.1 for TusoAI and exceeds Claude Code with the same backbone by 21.1
  percentage points (pp) in NatureBench Match-SOTA.
- •
  SwarmRSI outperforms hypothesis and program evolution agents. With the
  same backbone, its NatureBench Match-SOTA rate exceeds Arbor by 7.8 pp
  and AIDE by 16.7 pp, both significantly. Its 81.8% Any-Medal rate on
  MLE-Bench Lite also exceeds Arbor and MLEvolve, and, as shown in
  [Section B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Performance and Token Cost ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
  it uses fewer tokens than TusoAI and Arbor.
- •
  SwarmRSI remains strong across backbones. As shown in
  [Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.1 Research Questions ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
  with Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview it reaches the highest OpenProblems
  average of 76.2 and the same 44.4% NatureBench Match-SOTA rate as with
  DeepSeek-V4-Pro, which exceeds every compared method, and it keeps the
  81.8% Any-Medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite.

### 5.2 Ablation Studies (RQ2)

[Table 4](#S4.T4 "In 4.1 Research Questions ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
compares SwarmRSI with five variants on all 90 NatureBench tasks using
DeepSeek-V4-Pro, grouped by the mechanism they remove and otherwise
unchanged. w/o H-mutation only repairs programs under existing
hypotheses, w/o P-mutation only revises hypotheses, w/o recombination
disables rebase and crossover, w/o shared graph removes agents’ access
to one another’s records, and w/o H/P selection replaces adaptive H/P
selection with fixed H/P alternation. As shown in
[Table 6](#A2.T6 "In B.7 Statistical Reliability ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
all variants lower mean $`g`$ with paired 95% CIs excluding zero, and we
make three observations.

- •
  Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation needs both revision modes. Removing H-
  or P-mutation lowers Match-SOTA by 10.0 and 13.3 pp, respectively. The
  two modes are complementary, and program repair contributes more.
- •
  Complementary Swarm Recombination improves solution quality. Disabling
  rebase and crossover lowers Match-SOTA by 6.7 pp and Surpass-SOTA from
  15.6% to 7.8%, and its drop in mean $`g`$ excludes zero. RQ3 traces
  this gain to reused hypotheses and program components in most final
  programs.
- •
  Recursive Swarm Evolution depends on the shared graph and H/P
  selection. Match-SOTA drops by 7.8 pp without the shared graph,
  through which agents use other agents’ records, and by 8.9 pp without
  H/P selection, which also causes the largest drop in mean $`g`$.

### 5.3 Trajectory Analysis (RQ3)

Figure 4: Search behaviour on NatureBench with DeepSeek-V4-Pro. (a)
Failure recovery, the share of failed implementations whose later
refinement yields a new best, over 90 tasks. (b) Nested shares of
proposed hypotheses that are implemented (white), evaluated (light) and
improved (hatched). (c, d) Best-so-far $`g`$ of one recorded run per
method on symmetric log axes.

We examine how SwarmRSI uses feedback and retained research states
during search.
[Figure 4](#S5.F4 "In 5.3 Trajectory Analysis (RQ3) ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")a,b
compares diagnostics with AIDE and Arbor on all 90 NatureBench tasks,
and
[Figure 4](#S5.F4 "In 5.3 Trajectory Analysis (RQ3) ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")c,d
shows one run per method on two tasks.

- •
  SwarmRSI recovers more failed implementations and turns more of its
  hypotheses into improvements. Later refinement turns 35% of failed
  implementations into new best valid programs, compared with 12% for
  AIDE and 5% for Arbor. Of its proposed hypotheses, 62% lead to an
  improvement, against 17% for Arbor.
- •
  SwarmRSI revises with prior feedback. In one NatureBench seed with
  DeepSeek-V4-Pro, updates triggered by performance feedback or
  unresolved implementation requests reach a 28.4% rate of new bests,
  against 21.9% over all updates. On 00917, after nine candidates
  without improvement, two successive hypothesis mutations revise the
  program’s readout and reach Surpass-SOTA.
- •
  SwarmRSI reuses partial results across research trajectories. In the
  same seed, about 77% of final best programs descend from at least one
  rebase, crossover or update triggered by feedback, and these updates
  yield new bests more often than other updates at every stage of
  search. On 00639, crossover combines components from three programs to
  reach Match-SOTA, and a later rebase step activates retained
  message-passing and ranking-loss code that the program had not
  executed, further improving $`g`$. On 00795, rebase tests an
  unrealized hypothesis on a later program, and crossover then reuses a
  component from a program that had not improved overall.
- •
  Inherited research states transfer to new tasks. As shown in
  [Section C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Cross-Task Transfer ‣ Appendix C Additional Trajectory Analyses ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
  the state inherited after three generations of source tasks raises
  mean $`g`$ on 12 held-out NatureBench tasks from 12.01 to 21.89 and
  Match-SOTA from 41.7% to 86.1% over cold start.

### 5.4 Failure Analysis (RQ4)

We annotate 644 failed attempts from 35 SwarmRSI task trajectories that
fall below Match-SOTA on NatureBench or earn no medal on MLE-Bench Lite.
Two annotators judge implementation fidelity, execution success and
improvement over the preceding best valid result, with Cohen’s
$`\kappa=0.92`$. Most failures are faithful interventions without gains.
They account for 93.3% of these failures, and implementation or
execution problems for 6.7%. A valid score can hide such a problem, as
on task 00838, where downstream code overwrites a proposed amino-acid
substitution that the evaluation thus never tests, which is why
Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation reads implementation evidence alongside
scores. On task 02316, by contrast, a negative-binomial mixture with a
Potts spatial prior runs correctly but collapses all locations into one
cluster, a faithful intervention without gain.

### 5.5 Biological Case Studies

![Refer to caption](2610.20431v1/bio_cases_merged.png)

Figure 5: Biological case studies. (a–c) UMAP of the reproduced SwarmRSI
representation for 833 target enterocytes, coloured by source and by
MHC-II/Cd74 and antimicrobial RNA scores (mean normalized log RNA).
Green and pink denote helminth day-10 and Salmonella sources. (d–f)
BALBc-1 spleen with manual annotations, SwarmRSI program partitions, and
expert neighbourhood groups E1 (B/follicular-dendritic-cell-rich), E2
(myeloid/erythroid-rich) and E3 (T-cell-rich).

We examine the scientific utility of programs evolved by SwarmRSI.
Biologists reproduced two of its programs and interpreted them with
measured RNA and protein.

Enterocyte: infection-associated immune expression. On the contrastiveVI
task ([Weinberger et al., 2023](#bib.bib41)), the evolved program
removes from each infected cell the mean of its healthy neighbours
before reducing the residuals. On the Haber intestinal instance ([Haber
et al., 2017](#bib.bib42)), measured enterocyte RNA shows higher MHC-II
scores in helminth day 10 than Salmonella infection, with means of 1.17
and 0.34.
[Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.5 Biological Case Studies ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")a–c
shows the corresponding expression gradients on the reproduced
representation. Within the two helminth day-10 sources, MHC-II scores
are locally more coherent on this representation than on RNA PCA, with
Spearman’s $`\rho`$ after covariate adjustment of 0.77 and 0.69 against
0.65 and 0.40, whereas antimicrobial scores are less locally coherent
than on RNA PCA in each of the four sources.

CytoCommunity: spatial context without consistent marker shifts. On the
CytoCommunity task ([Hu et al., 2024](#bib.bib43)), the evolved program
combines anatomical priors derived from phenotypes with an anatomically
constrained Markov random field, raising $`g`$ from 0.045 to 0.102.
[Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.5 Biological Case Studies ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")d–f
compares its partitions with manual anatomy and experts’ subsequent
grouping of B-cell neighbourhoods by local cell composition. Across
three spleen images, 20–21% of B cells in the manually annotated
marginal zone occupy myeloid/erythroid-rich neighbourhoods. [Goltsev et
al. (2018)](#bib.bib49) reported that B220 and CD79b levels on splenic B
cells vary with the local neighbourhood across the spleen. Within the
marginal zone, after matching these B cells on location and phenotype
within each image, these and four other markers show no consistent shift
between neighbourhoods across the three images, and the B220 effect
changes sign.

## 6 Conclusion

We began with two obstacles for scientific agent swarms. A failed
experiment does not show whether its hypothesis or its implementation
was at fault, and a program without overall gains can still hold a
component that another agent needs. SwarmRSI addresses both.
Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to decide
whether to revise a hypothesis or repair its program, and Complementary
Swarm Recombination carries retained hypotheses and useful components
into other agents’ work. Recursive Swarm Evolution returns every outcome
to the shared graph, so each experiment changes the states that later
agents start from. It achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and
NatureBench and outperforms baselines with the same backbone, most final
NatureBench programs build on such reuse, and biologists reproduce its
programs for follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity. A
swarm thus improves its own research by evolving what its agents
inherit.

### A.1 Recursive Swarm Evolution

The hypothesis frontier $`\mathcal{F}_{H}`$ retains active hypotheses
balanced across mechanism families. The program frontier
$`\mathcal{F}_{P}`$ retains the incumbent $`p^{\star}`$ and the best
program of each realized family. *H-mutation* revises the hypothesis
toward an alternative modeling or algorithmic mechanism, whereas
*P-mutation* keeps the hypothesis and refines an existing program
lineage.

[Table 5](#A1.T5 "In A.1 Recursive Swarm Evolution ‣ Appendix A Method Details ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
lists the shared selection settings for DeepSeek-V4-Pro and Gemini 3.1
Pro Preview across all three benchmarks.

|                                               |       |
|-----------------------------------------------|-------|
|  Parameter                                    | Value |
|  Discount factor $`\rho`$                     | 0.9   |
|  Exploration weight $`\beta`$                 | 0.5   |
|  Request-pressure weight $`\gamma`$           | 0.25  |
|  Request-pressure saturation $`K`$            | 4     |
|  Eligible-wait threshold $`W`$                | 8     |
|  Tokens per cost unit $`T`$                   | 1,000 |
|  Improvement scale $`s`$                      | 1.0   |
|  Active hypotheses / program representatives  | 4 / 8 |
|  Candidate pairs per hypothesis               | 2     |
|  Sources per hypothesis adaptation            | 1     |
|  Scored local continuations / local attempts  | 2 / 4 |
|  Local stagnation patience                    | 1     |
|  Initial root drafts                          | 3     |

Table 5: Shared H/P-selection parameters and search limits across both
backbones and all three benchmarks.

Improvement and computational cost. For an experiment initiated by
frontier $`a_{t}\in\{H,P\}`$, let $`u_{t}`$ be its utility and
$`u_{t}^{-}`$ the preceding incumbent utility. Reward and normalized
token cost are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\min\!\left(1,\max\!\left(0,\frac{u_{t}-u_{t}^{-}}{s}\right)\right),`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle c_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max\!\left(1,\frac{\tau_{t}}{T}\right),`$ |  |  |

where $`\tau_{t}`$ includes the experiment’s planner and coder tokens.
Without a prior incumbent, reward is zero. Failed or unscored outcomes
receive zero reward and keep their cost, and infrastructure failures
leave the statistics unchanged.

Selection score updates. Initialize $`N_{f}=G_{f}=C_{f}=0`$ for both
frontiers. After each experiment other than an infrastructure failure,
update both frontiers:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle N_{f}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\rho N_{f}+\mathbf{1}[f=a_{t}],`$ |  | (8) |
|  | $`\displaystyle G_{f}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\rho G_{f}+\mathbf{1}[f=a_{t}]r_{t},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle C_{f}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\rho C_{f}+\mathbf{1}[f=a_{t}]c_{t}.`$ |  |  |

The controller scores each sampled, feasible frontier using
[Equation 6](#S3.E6 "In 3.4 Recursive Swarm Evolution ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
with $`\hat{\mu}_{f}=G_{f}/C_{f}`$ as recent improvement per cost. For
$`U_{f}`$ unresolved requests addressable by frontier $`f`$, request
pressure is $`\psi_{f}=\min(U_{f},K)/K`$. These requests are generated
by experiments in both frontiers, coupling their allocation. Setting
$`\gamma=0`$ removes request pressure from the index. Discounting
emphasizes recent outcomes, while request pressure saturates at $`K=4`$.

Frontier scheduling. A sole feasible frontier is selected directly. When
both are feasible, frontiers without an updated sample take priority,
with H preceding P. Subsequently, a frontier waiting for $`W`$ eligible
choices takes priority. Otherwise, the highest index wins. Ties favor
lower discounted selection count, then H. Waiting counters advance only
when both frontiers compete. Local program refinement may precede the
next frontier choice, subject to the continuation, attempt, and
stagnation limits in
[Table 5](#A1.T5 "In A.1 Recursive Swarm Evolution ‣ Appendix A Method Details ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm").
The index adapts discounted UCB to allocation between the hypothesis and
program frontiers.

Ablation without H/P selection. The *w/o H/P selection* variant in
[Table 4](#S4.T4 "In 4.1 Research Questions ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
replaces this allocation rule with H/P alternation, starting with H and
selecting the other frontier if the scheduled one is infeasible. Both
frontiers, H- and P-mutation, the shared graph, audits, local
refinement, recombination, and total search budget remain unchanged.

### A.2 Execution and Feedback

The execution harness and search controller perform the online audits
automatically. The code audit uses execution status, artifact validity
and evaluator feedback to check execution and the task contract. The
scientific audit screens implementation evidence by comparing parent and
offspring source code and recorded submitted outputs. It flags unchanged
source or syntax trees, newly added functions without static name
references, or identical submitted outputs when comparable output
records are available. The controller uses these source/output flags to
gate hypothesis credit. A valid scored candidate without such a flag
receives hypothesis credit ([Potter and De Jong, 2000](#bib.bib24)).
Flagged or unscored attempts retain program-level evidence and
unresolved implementation requests. Hypothesis credit records a valid
scored candidate passing the source/output checks. Improvement per cost
drives H/P selection. Failed states and states without gain remain
available in the graph together with their resulting programs, outcomes,
and audit evidence.

An implementation failure retains the hypothesis and emits a *deepen*
request for program refinement. Mixed per-instance gains and losses emit
a *broaden* request for hypothesis exploration. An improved incumbent
also creates deepen requests for hypotheses previously realized on older
programs. These requests retain unresolved needs for later mutation or
recombination.

### A.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination

Rebase selection. Rebase preserves hypothesis content and selects a
different program from $`\mathcal{F}_{P}`$ as its implementation base.
Candidates are ranked by the overlap between the recorded need and
mechanism labels associated with scored, unflagged programs. A request
triggered by a new incumbent directly targets that program. The coder
then realizes the hypothesis on the selected base, so that for a
selected base $`b_{t}`$

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(\tilde{h}_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})=\bigl(h_{t},\operatorname{Rebase}(h_{t},b_{t};s_{t})\bigr).
``` |  | (9) |

Crossover selection.
[Equation 5](#S3.E5 "In 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
defines candidate donors and their priorities from observed per-instance
utilities. At most two donors are selected by their largest per-instance
advantage. Let $`\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{t}`$ contain the selected
donors. Sources are assigned before implementation as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma_{t}(k)=\begin{cases}p_{k}^{\star},&p_{k}^{\star}\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{t}\text{ and }u_{k}(p_{k}^{\star})>u_{k}(p_{t}),\\
p_{t},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (10) |

so the offspring uses donor components on the instances they win and
retains the parent’s components elsewhere. This assignment is fixed from
measured feedback before implementation. Crossover is inactive for
aggregate-only feedback. Both operators produce new candidates evaluated
through the shared execution and audit loop.

State update. After each evaluation, the record $`\xi_{t}`$ updates the
shared graph, the next acting agent assembles its state, and a mutation
or recombination operation generates the next candidate,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Update}(\mathcal{G}_{t},\xi_{t}),`$ |  | (11) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Assemble}(q,\mathcal{G}_{t+1}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle(\tilde{h}_{t+1},\tilde{p}_{t+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Generate}(q;s_{t+1}).`$ |  |  |

$`\operatorname{Update}`$ retains the record and refreshes the
associated research needs, $`\operatorname{Assemble}`$ collects the
records relevant to the next agent, and $`\operatorname{Generate}`$
applies an eligible operation to the selected sources.

## Appendix B Experimental Setup

### B.1 Benchmarks

#### OpenProblems.

OpenProblems follows the six-task single-cell panel used by
TusoAI ([Turcan et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)): denoising, label prediction,
batch integration, spatially variable gene identification, decomposition
and visualization.

#### NatureBench.

NatureBench ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)) includes 90 tasks in
protein biology, cellular omics, physical modeling, molecular design,
relational reasoning and biomedical modeling.

#### MLE-Bench Lite.

MLE-Bench Lite ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) comprises 22
low-complexity competitions involving model construction and competition
submission.

### B.2 Baselines

#### Benchmark-specific comparisons.

On OpenProblems, we compare with expert-designed methods and ChatGPT
reported by TusoAI ([Turcan et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), Biomni ([Huang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), AIDE ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)), and
TusoAI. On NatureBench, we compare with Claude Code ([Anthropic,
2026](#bib.bib26)), Codex and Gemini CLI ([Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6)), Arbor ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), and AIDE.
NatureBench results without $`\dagger`$ in
[Table 2](#S3.T2 "In 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
are taken from the official NatureBench leaderboard ([FrontisAI,
2026](#bib.bib25)) (accessed 7 October 2026). These entries follow
NatureBench’s standard protocol. Comparisons with published results are
descriptive. On MLE-Bench Lite, we compare with MLEvolve ([Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)), ML-Master 2.0 ([Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)),
Arbor ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), and Famou-Agent 2.0 ([Li et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)). Reported competition results are taken from the
official MLE-Bench leaderboard ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib29)) or the
original papers ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18); [Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)).

#### Matched-backbone reruns.

We rerun AIDE and TusoAI on OpenProblems, AIDE and Arbor on NatureBench,
and MLEvolve and Arbor on MLE-Bench Lite using DeepSeek-V4-Pro. We do
not rerun MLEvolve on NatureBench because its harness uses MLE-Bench’s
competition submission and grading format ([Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). AIDE serves as the program-evolution representative
on NatureBench.

### B.3 Evaluation Details

#### Grading procedures.

OpenProblems uses each task’s official evaluator. NatureBench results
are assessed by the benchmark’s validity judge before computing
Match-SOTA and Surpass-SOTA rates. MLE-Bench Lite submissions are graded
by the official grader on the private test split.

#### Metric definitions.

OpenProblems uses the normalized task scores reported by TusoAI ([Turcan
et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), with higher values indicating better
performance. Scores are scaled by 100. Avg is the unweighted mean over
the six tasks, using each task’s benchmark-defined normalization. For
NatureBench, the instance-level direction-adjusted improvement is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{i}=d_{i}\frac{m_{i}-m_{i}^{\mathrm{SOTA}}}{|m_{i}^{\mathrm{SOTA}}|},
``` |  | (12) |

where $`m_{i}`$ is the score, $`m_{i}^{\mathrm{SOTA}}`$ is its reference
and $`d_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}`$ specifies the metric direction. A task’s $`g`$
averages its instance-level improvements. Match-SOTA counts judge-valid
tasks with $`g\geq 0`$. Surpass-SOTA counts those with $`g>0.1`$. Both
rates use all 90 tasks, retaining invalid and missing results in the
denominator. S and M denote Surpass-SOTA and Match-SOTA. For the
ablations, Mean $`g`$ (%) is
$`100\times\frac{1}{90}\sum_{j=1}^{90}g_{j}`$, where $`g_{j}`$ is task
$`j`$’s relative improvement. All ablation tasks have valid results.
MLE-Bench Lite reports Above Median, exclusive Bronze, Silver and Gold
rates, and Any-Medal, the fraction of all 22 tasks earning any medal.
Invalid or missing submissions receive no medal. All rates use the full
task set.

#### Aggregation across runs.

For SwarmRSI and matched-backbone reruns in
Tables [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")–[3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
we compute each benchmark metric separately for each of three seeds,
then average the three values equally. Each seed covers the same six
OpenProblems tasks, 90 NatureBench tasks, or 22 MLE-Bench Lite
competitions. Task scores and domain-specific rates are averaged in the
same way. Results in
Tables [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")–[3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.3 Complementary Swarm Recombination ‣ 3 SwarmRSI ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
are reported to one decimal place after aggregation. Published reference
results retain their original reporting protocols. Medal rates are
rounded independently, so their displayed sum may differ from Any-Medal
by 0.1 pp.

#### Token consumption.

For
[Figure 6](#A2.F6 "In B.6 Performance and Token Cost ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
we sum uncached input and output tokens from all LLM calls throughout
each task’s search. We report the mean over all tasks in each benchmark
and the three seeds, in millions of tokens per task. Arbor’s token
consumption is on the same scale as the 20.19M tokens per task reported
in its original paper ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)).

#### Significance testing.

We compare NatureBench Match-SOTA outcomes for SwarmRSI, Arbor and AIDE
with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, using the 90 tasks as paired units. Each task
contributes its mean binary Match-SOTA outcome across the three runs. As
[Section B.7](#A2.SS7 "B.7 Statistical Reliability ‣ Appendix B Experimental Setup ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
explains, each method’s task-level Match-SOTA outcomes are identical
across its three seeds. We resample the 90 paired task means with
replacement 10,000 times and recompute the rate difference in each
replicate ([Koehn, 2004](#bib.bib44)). The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles
give the 95% paired bootstrap confidence interval. The Match-SOTA
improvement over Arbor is 7.8 pp (95% CI \[2.2, 13.3\]). The improvement
over AIDE is 16.7 pp (95% CI \[8.9, 24.4\]). Exact McNemar tests use the
discordant task pairs: SwarmRSI-only/baseline-only successes are 7/0
against Arbor and 15/0 against AIDE. The resulting $`p`$-values are
$`0.015625`$ and $`0.0000610`$, respectively. Differences are computed
before rounding the displayed rates. Other benchmark and leaderboard
comparisons are reported descriptively.

### B.4 Implementation Details

#### Backbones.

SwarmRSI and the rerun baselines use DeepSeek-V4-Pro ([DeepSeek,
2026](#bib.bib39)). Additional SwarmRSI runs use Gemini 3.1 Pro
Preview ([Google DeepMind, 2026](#bib.bib40)).

#### Time budgets.

We set per-task time limits to 8 hours on OpenProblems ([Turcan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), 4 hours on NatureBench ([Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6)), and 12 hours on MLE-Bench Lite ([Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). Within each benchmark, SwarmRSI and rerun baselines
share the evaluation protocol and time limit.

#### Hardware configurations.

SwarmRSI and rerun baselines use the same hardware within each
benchmark. OpenProblems runs use 4 CPU threads and 50 GB of memory, each
of the 87 NatureBench tasks that require a GPU uses one GPU, and
MLE-Bench Lite runs use 21 vCPUs, 234 GB of RAM and one GPU. These
follow the published protocols for OpenProblems ([Turcan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), NatureBench ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)), and
MLE-Bench Lite ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)).

### B.5 Ablation Settings and Aggregation

The variants in
[Table 4](#S4.T4 "In 4.1 Research Questions ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")
use the same planner, coder, evaluator and total search budget. The w/o
shared graph variant preserves hypotheses, executable programs, and
experimental feedback while disabling their graph-based organization and
reuse. The w/o H-mutation variant only repairs programs under existing
hypotheses ($`\tilde{h}_{t}=h_{t}`$), while w/o P-mutation only revises
hypotheses. The w/o recombination variant disables both rebase and
crossover in Complementary Swarm Recombination, retaining
within-trajectory evolution. The w/o H/P selection variant, described in
[Section A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Recursive Swarm Evolution ‣ Appendix A Method Details ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm"),
retains both frontiers and replaces adaptive H/P selection with fixed
H/P alternation.

For seed $`s`$, let $`n_{s}^{M}`$ and $`n_{s}^{S}`$ count judge-valid
tasks with $`g\geq 0`$ and $`g>0.1`$, respectively. The reported rates
are $`\frac{100}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{3}n_{s}^{M}/90`$ and
$`\frac{100}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{3}n_{s}^{S}/90`$. Mean $`g`$ is
$`\frac{100}{270}\sum_{s=1}^{3}\sum_{t=1}^{90}g_{s,t}`$. All ablation
tasks have valid results. Thus, each seed contributes equally, and
values are rounded to one decimal place only after averaging.

### C.2 Cross-Task Transfer

#### Cross-task research state inheritance.

After each generation, the accumulated research state is preserved for
subsequent tasks. This state includes hypotheses, program components,
experimental evidence, and unresolved implementation needs. On a new
task, prior records are retrieved by matching their mechanism labels to
the task description and initialize the agent’s research state, while
candidate programs are adapted and evaluated under the new task’s
constraints.

#### Experimental setup and results.

We run SwarmRSI with DeepSeek-V4-Pro on six NatureBench source tasks in
three generations of two tasks each, and each generation starts from the
cross-task state exported by the previous one. We then search 12
held-out NatureBench tasks, fixed in advance and disjoint from the
source tasks, from an empty state and from the state after each
generation, with three seeds and the same budget per task. As shown in

after one, two and three generations, and Gen 3 improves on cold start
by 9.88 points. Match-SOTA rises from 41.7% to 86.1% and Surpass-SOTA
from 33.3% to 58.3%, while tokens per task fall from 12.17M to 10.94M.
Gains are largest over the first two generations, and Gen 3 matches

## Appendix D Human Failure Analysis

#### Sample and unit of analysis.

We reviewed 35 failed SwarmRSI task trajectories across NatureBench and
MLE-Bench Lite. The annotation unit was an individual attempt, assessed
against the best preceding valid result using each benchmark’s metric
direction. The final failure set comprised 644 attempts across these
trajectories, after applying the exclusions below. OpenProblems reports
continuous normalized scores without a binary success criterion, so no
OpenProblems trajectories enter the failure set. The 35 trajectories
comprise 32 NatureBench tasks below Match-SOTA and 3 MLE-Bench Lite
competitions without a medal.

#### Evaluation criteria.

Annotators examined the proposed intervention, implementation and
execution records, evaluator outputs, and preceding scores.
*Implementation or execution failure* denotes an intervention that could
not be reliably tested because of code or interface errors, execution
interruptions, or proposal–implementation mismatches. *No performance
gain* denotes a faithfully implemented and executed intervention with a
valid evaluation that did not improve the preceding best valid result.
Equal scores were treated as no gain. Recovered intermediate errors were
judged by the final evaluated outcome. Successful verification or
restoration of an existing result, and repetition of an attained metric
ceiling, were excluded from the failure counts. Attempts with
insufficient evidence to distinguish the two failure classes were also
excluded.

#### Human review and agreement.

Two annotators with PhDs in bioinformatics engineering reviewed the
evidence using these criteria, with inter-rater agreement of Cohen’s
$`\kappa=0.92`$.

#### Representative cases.

On task 00838, a proposed amino-acid substitution was overwritten by
downstream rules, leaving the evaluated output unchanged This was an
implementation failure despite a valid score. In contrast, on task
02316, a negative-binomial mixture with a Potts spatial prior ran
successfully but collapsed all locations into one cluster, yielding ARI
zero. This was a no-gain outcome.

### E.1 Enterocyte: Infection-Associated Expression Variation

#### Task, data and selected program.

NatureBench task s41592-023-01955-3 is associated with
contrastiveVI ([Weinberger et al., 2023](#bib.bib41)) and returns a
low-dimensional target-cell representation. The program selected by
SwarmRSI comes from step 4 of five recorded steps. Its recorded
task-level $`g=1.838461`$ averages the Haber, McFarland and Norman
instances. The analysis here concerns only the Haber intestinal
instance ([Haber et al., 2017](#bib.bib42)), GEO GSE92332. The public
matrix contains 9,842 cells and 15,215 genes: 3,240 healthy background
cells, 2,711 helminth day-10 and 1,770 Salmonella target cells, and
2,121 helminth day-3 cells outside the target submission. The published
Enterocyte annotation selects 1,458 cells, including 833 targets (128
helminth day-10 and 705 Salmonella).

#### Evolved program and expert reproduction.

For Haber, the program normalizes total counts to 10,000 and applies
log1p, selects 2,000 genes by variance across target and background
cells, and standardizes using background means and standard deviations,
clipping at $`\pm 10`$. It identifies 20 healthy neighbours per target
in a 50-dimensional background PCA space and subtracts their mean
expression. A singular-value decomposition yields a 20-dimensional
residual space. A 15-neighbour graph then smooths the first ten residual
coordinates for two iterations, including a self weight. Experts
reproduced the program. Local-coherence analysis uses its
ten-dimensional outputs. The embedding is constructed from RNA, and
infection labels parsed from public cell identifiers are used for
diagnostic silhouette reporting.

#### Measured programs and source units.

Each program score is the arithmetic mean of its genes’
$`\log(1+10^{4}x/\sum x)`$ expression. The MHC-II-associated set is
Cd74, H2-Aa, H2-Ab1 and H2-Eb1 (the input matrix uses dots in the H2
gene names). The interferon set is Stat1, Isg15, Ifit1, Ifit3, Oas1a and
Irf7. All six genes are present in the matrix. The antimicrobial set
contains Reg3g, Reg3b, Lcn2 and Saa1. Its signal is dominated by
Reg3g/Reg3b, with Lcn2 undetected and Saa1 low in these enterocytes.
B1–B4 are control sources, B5/B6 helminth day 3, B7/B8 helminth day 10,
and B9/B10 Salmonella. Source labels denote sequencing batches.

#### Dataset and evolved program.

NatureBench task s41592-023-02124-2 uses three healthy mouse spleen
CODEX images associated with CytoCommunity ([Hu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib43)): 82,382, 81,925 and 80,973 cells, respectively, with
27 supplied cell phenotypes. SwarmRSI receives coordinates and phenotype
labels. The expert follow-up separately examines measured protein
intensities. The program selected by SwarmRSI comes from step 12 of a
15-step search and raises $`g`$ from 0.04507 to 0.10215. It uses
phenotype-derived anatomical priors, 50-neighbour composition and
interface features, Gaussian EM, an anatomically constrained spatial MRF
and cleanup of components smaller than 150 cells. Recorded macro-F1 and
AMI are 0.639247 and 0.583341. Macro-F1 includes the unannotated
reference category, whose F1 is zero. Marginal-zone F1 is 0.600, 0.593
and 0.635.

#### Post-hoc neighbourhood descriptions.

The analysis includes 35,168, 33,544 and 32,451 conventionally annotated
B cells (101,163 total). Experts fit three exploratory
neighbourhood-composition prototypes in the first image and then hold
them fixed for the other two, giving B/FDC-rich (E1),
myeloid/erythroid-rich (E2) and T-cell-rich (E3) groups. E1–E3 describe
local cell composition around B cells in this post-hoc analysis. They
are distinct from the program’s anatomical partitions. The primary
comparison uses E2 versus E1 within the *manual* marginal zone,
independently of whether the SwarmRSI program assigned that cell to the
marginal zone. Coordinates are analyzed in dataset units.

#### Expert protein and geometry checks.

The analysis uses processed protein measurements for all 245,280 cells.
Within each image and manual marginal zone, matching is exact on
original phenotype cluster and imaging tile, then uses five geometry
variables with a 0.5-SD per-variable caliper, joint distance at most 1
and no replacement. There are 815, 995 and 1,025 matched E2–E1 pairs,
retaining 42.5%, 46.2% and 41.1% of E2 cells, respectively. Maximum
absolute geometry standardized mean differences are 0.080, 0.063 and
0.083. Protein contrasts use this matched overlap subset. All six
preselected proteins (CD79b, B220, IgD, IgM, MHCII, CD44) are shown in
[Figure 9](#A5.F9 "In E.2 CytoCommunity: Spatial Context and Protein Follow-up ‣ Appendix E Biological Case Studies ‣ SwarmRSI: Recursive Self-Improvement through Evolving Scientific Agent Swarm")b.
Measurements are transformed as $`\operatorname{arcsinh}(x/150)`$,
retaining negative values. Effects are differences of transformed means
divided by the within-image contrast-population standard deviation.
Intervals use 1,000 bootstrap resamples of 1,000-coordinate-unit spatial
blocks within each image. B220 effects are $`+0.082`$, $`-0.130`$ and
$`-0.190`$. Stricter 0.25-SD matching also yields inconsistent
directions. In spatially blocked validation, adding neighbourhood labels
to a geometry/phenotype protein predictor produces $`\Delta R^{2}`$ from
$`-0.00098`$ to $`+0.00967`$ across 18 image–protein combinations.
Neighbourhood labels add little predictive information for these six
protein markers in spatially blocked validation.

## Appendix F Limitations

SwarmRSI requires an evaluator that can execute candidate programs, so
research without executable feedback is outside its scope. Execution
feedback does not always reveal whether the intended intervention was
faithfully implemented, and performance gains alone do not establish its
scientific mechanism. Rebase selects program bases by matching recorded
implementation needs to program mechanism labels, a heuristic that does
not measure what a program can do. Crossover applies only when a
benchmark reports scores per instance. H/P selection adapts discounted
UCB without inheriting its guarantees, and its benefit is established
only empirically.

Evaluation covers comparisons under matched budgets on three benchmark
families. The human failure analysis characterizes 644 annotated failed
attempts from 35 task trajectories. Cross-task evidence comes from six
source tasks, 12 held-out tasks and one backbone, and generations are
confounded with the number of source tasks seen.

The evaluation does not separately assess literature synthesis ([Shi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib60)), idea quality ([Sauter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib63)), or peer-review quality ([Guo et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib65)). Generalization to complete research workflows ([Lu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Lyu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib21)) and information-access agents ([Kanoulas et al.,
2025](#bib.bib61); [Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib66)) requires additional
evaluation.

Other untested settings include legal reasoning ([Lyu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib50); [Lyu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib53); [Wu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib64)), zero-shot entity extraction ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib52)), conversational recommendation ([Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib51)), and shopping research ([Lyu et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib59)). These settings require task-specific objectives and
evaluation protocols.

## Appendix G Ethics Statement

Benchmark gains do not establish factual reliability or alignment with
human preferences, which require separate evaluation and
optimization ([Lin et al., 2022](#bib.bib67); [Lyu et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib56); [Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib69); [Lyu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib55)). We do not assess robustness to dataset
shortcuts ([Lyu et al., 2023b](#bib.bib54)), social
stereotypes ([Parrish et al., 2022](#bib.bib68)), or cognitive
biases ([Lyu et al., 2024b](#bib.bib58); [Lyu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib57)), nor do we test harmful-content generation.
Applications that affect people should preserve user agency and provide
domain-appropriate human oversight ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib62)).

Generated programs and scientific interpretations require independent
validation before use beyond the benchmark setting. The execution and
scientific audits check task validity and implementation evidence; they
do not certify security or scientific correctness. The biological case
studies analyse existing datasets, and their associations do not
establish causal mechanisms or clinical validity. Any deployment should
restrict code execution, external-tool access and credentials to the
approved scope, and review proposed uses for potential harm.

Data reuse should respect source licences, access restrictions and
privacy requirements, with provenance and intended uses
documented ([Gebru et al., 2021](#bib.bib70)). We report time budgets,
hardware configurations and token consumption to support resource-aware
comparisons ([Schwartz et al., 2020](#bib.bib71)). Token consumption is
not a direct measurement of energy use or carbon emissions.
````
