###### Abstract

We present E3, an automated review assistant that augments reviewers and
engineering teams by identifying decision-relevant technical concerns in
research papers. For each concern, E3 reports its nature, its location,
its bearing on the contribution, and the analysis or evidence that would
resolve it, covering unsupported claims, missing ablations, weak
baselines, hidden assumptions, threats to validity, and leakage risks.
To evaluate E3 without contamination confounds we adopt an issue-level
backtesting protocol: the corpus is restricted to papers postdating the
training cutoff of every automated source, and for each paper a
meta-judge that observes only anonymised reviews labels every (issue,
source) pair as Caught, Partial, or Missed. Applied to 100 ICLR 2026
papers (4,598 judged issue rows), comparing E3 against the ICLR human
reviews and two prompt-matched LLM baselines built on gpt-5.4 (OpenAI)
and claude-opus-4-6 (Anthropic), with meta-judge gpt-5.5: E3 attains the
highest recall on every aggregate metric. Partial-inclusive recall
reaches 90.2% ($`\uparrow`$ 15.5 pp over GPT, $`\uparrow`$ 17.1 pp over
Claude, $`\uparrow`$ 29.2 pp over the human reviews) and strict recall
preserves the ordering at 65.8%. On concerns raised by the human
reviewers E3 recovers 89.6%; on concerns the human reviewers missed it
surfaces 1,635 additional rows admitted into the judged union, 406 above
the next-best source. Corpus, baseline prompts, judge prompt template,
and evaluation code are released.

## 1 Introduction

Peer review is the principal quality-control mechanism for
machine-learning publishing, and its capacity is bounded by the supply
of qualified reviewers. The NeurIPS consistency experiments establish
that even careful reviewing is statistically noisy: when two independent
committees reviewed the same submissions in 2014 and again in 2021, they
disagreed on roughly a quarter of accept/reject decisions and would have
reversed about half of the accept list on a rerun ([Cortes and Lawrence,
2021](#bib.bib1); [Beygelzimer et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)). The major
venues now receive on the order of $`10^{4}`$ submissions per cycle
while the reviewer pool grows at a much slower rate, compressing the
per-paper time budget. As a consequence, concerns that ought to be
surfaced by a careful read (a missing ablation, an unsupported claim, a
leakage risk, an unfair baseline) are increasingly missed not for
subtlety but for lack of reviewer bandwidth, and corpus-level analyses
suggest that 6.5–16.9% of text in recent ICLR, NeurIPS, CoRL, and EMNLP
reviews is already being substantially modified by LLMs in practice
([Liang et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)).

We present E3, an automated review assistant that addresses this
bottleneck. For a given paper, E3 produces a structured technical
critique in which each decision-relevant concern is articulated as a
discrete entry: its nature, its location, its bearing on the
contribution, and the analysis or evidence that would resolve it. The
concerns targeted include unsupported claims, missing ablations, weak
baselines, hidden assumptions, threats to validity, and leakage risks
([Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023](#bib.bib3)). E3 is positioned as
augmentation rather than substitution: it accelerates the location and
articulation of hidden technical faults, so that reviewer attention can
concentrate on the judgements only humans can make. The same workflow
applies to engineering teams running internal audits on in-progress
research.

Evaluating assistants of this kind requires care: as preprints and
public OpenReview content are absorbed into LLM pretraining corpora,
apparently high recall on a familiar paper may reflect prior exposure
rather than analysis ([White et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)). We therefore
grade E3 with an _issue-level backtesting_ protocol: (i) the corpus is
restricted to papers postdating the training cutoff of every model used
in the protocol; (ii) for each paper we construct a union of distinct
concerns surfaced by any reviewer (human or automated) and score each
(issue, source) pair separately as Caught, Partial, or Missed; (iii)
labels are produced by a meta-judge that observes the four review
streams in anonymised form ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib5)).

Applied to 100 ICLR 2026 papers (4,598-row judged union, mean 46.0
concerns per paper), the protocol compares E3 against the ICLR human
reviews and two LLM baselines that run a released flaw-finding prompt on
gpt-5.4 (OpenAI) and claude-opus-4-6 (Anthropic); the meta-judge is
gpt-5.5. E3 attains the highest recall on every aggregate metric, with
partial-inclusive recall 90.2% ($`\uparrow`$ 15.5 pp over GPT,
$`\uparrow`$ 17.1 pp over Claude, $`\uparrow`$ 29.2 pp over the human
reviews) and strict recall preserving the ordering. The remainder of the
paper formalises the protocol
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Evaluation Design ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
defines the metrics
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Metrics ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
reports per-source coverage
(Sections [5](#S5 "5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")–[6](#S6 "6 Human-Review Recall and Alignment ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
characterises residual errors by taxonomy
(Sections [7](#S7 "7 Taxonomy and Error Analysis ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")–[8](#S8 "8 Complementarity ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
and states limitations
(Section [10](#S10 "10 Limitations ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")).

#### Contributions.

1.  1.  E3, an automated review assistant that produces a structured
        technical critique of a research paper in which each
        decision-relevant concern is articulated as a discrete, reasoned
        entry (nature, location, bearing on the contribution, and path to
        resolution); intended to augment human reviewers and engineering
        teams by accelerating the search for hidden technical faults at
        scale.
2.  2.  An issue-level backtesting protocol for unbiased evaluation of
        critique systems, combining a post-cutoff corpus, a per-paper
        concern union, and a blinded meta-judge that separates critique
        generation from critique evaluation.
3.  3.  A like-for-like comparison of E3 against the ICLR human reviews and
        prompt-matched GPT and Claude baselines on 4,598 judged issue rows
        from 100 ICLR 2026 papers, with per-source measurements of strict
        and partial-inclusive recall, weighted coverage, best-rigour share,
        recall on the human-salient subset, and the volume of human-missed
        concerns recovered, stratified by issue severity, decision tier, and
        taxonomy.
4.  4.  Public release of the baseline flaw-finder prompt and the meta-judge
        prompt template
        (Appendices [A](#A1 "Appendix A Exact LLM Baseline Prompt ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")–[B](#A2 "Appendix B Exact Blinded Judge Prompt Template ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")).

## 2 Background and Motivation

Three established facts motivate the system and the protocol. Unaided
peer review at ML venues is statistically noisy: the two NeurIPS
consistency experiments measured a 23–26% disagreement rate between
independent committees on accept/reject recommendations, with roughly
half of the accept list flipping on a rerun ([Cortes and Lawrence,
2021](#bib.bib1); [Beygelzimer et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)). The issue
categories E3 targets (leakage, weak baselines, hidden assumptions) are
the same ones independent audits of ML-based science identify as
systematically under-caught at publication time ([Kapoor and Narayanan,
2023](#bib.bib3)). LLM participation in reviewing is already underway,
with corpus-level analyses estimating that 6.5–16.9% of text in recent
ICLR, NeurIPS, CoRL, and EMNLP reviews has been substantially modified
by an LLM ([Liang et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). The protocol below answers
how to measure such a system’s contribution rigorously; it inherits
blinded LLM-as-judge scoring from MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena ([Zheng et
al., 2023](#bib.bib5)) and a post-training-cutoff corpus from
contamination-aware benchmarking ([White et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)), both
adapted to the review-critique setting.

## 3 Evaluation Design

### 3.1 Corpus

We use 100 ICLR 2026 papers with publicly available reviews, stratified
by decision folder: 28 oral, 28 accepted/poster, 15 conditional, and 29
rejected. The judged union holds 4,598 issue rows, averaging 46.0 per
paper (median 45.5).

Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.1 Corpus ‣ 3 Evaluation Design ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
fixes two corpus facts before any analysis. No single decision tier
dominates the sample (left). Issue-row counts vary widely per paper (a
paper with 55 rows has more places to miss than one with 35), so this
distribution sets the difficulty floor for recall.

Figure 1: Corpus composition and review-surface size. Left: paper count
per ICLR 2026 decision stratum (28 oral, 28 accepted/poster, 15
conditional, 29 rejected). Right: violin of judged issue rows per paper;
each black dot is one paper and the dashed line is the corpus median
(45.5 issues). Rejected papers carry the widest issue surface and
therefore the toughest recall test, which motivates the
decision-stratified analysis in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique").

### 3.2 Sources Compared

Four review streams feed the protocol per paper. _Human_ bundles the
publicly available ICLR review material: individual reviews, the
meta-review, the decision record, and associated metadata. _E3_ is the
automated critique output. _GPT_ and _Claude_ are flaw-finder baselines
running gpt-5.4 (OpenAI) and claude-opus-4-6 (Anthropic) on the paper
text. Both use the same flaw-finder prompt
(Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Exact LLM Baseline Prompt ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
which targets unsupported claims, hidden assumptions, weak baselines,
confounds, missing validations, and domain-specific risks.

### 3.3 Blinded Issue Judge

A _meta-judge_ is a separate model call that scores the four reviews. We
use gpt-5.5 (OpenAI), distinct from every review source. The judge sees
four anonymised streams (M1–M4) and never learns which is which. For
every paper it (i) extracts each stream’s concerns, (ii) merges them
into one union (only genuinely identical concerns merged), and (iii)
labels each row with a severity, a per-source status
(Caught/Partial/Missed), and a _best-rigour_ tag for the source that
addressed the issue most thoroughly. The exact prompt is in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Exact Blinded Judge Prompt Template ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique").

Separating critique from judgment stops any source from grading itself.
Blinding stops the judge from leaning on provider names. We remap M1–M4
to source names only after the JSON validates.

### 3.4 Backtesting Protocol

_Backtesting_ here means evaluating critique sources on papers that
postdate the LLM training cutoffs. ICLR 2026 submissions opened in late
2025 and the public reviews were released after that, which is later
than the cutoffs of gpt-5.4, claude-opus-4-6, and gpt-5.5. The LLM
baselines and judge therefore cannot earn a Caught from memorisation;
every catch comes from reading the paper.

The protocol itself is one pass per paper: collect the four reviews
(Human, E3, GPT, Claude), pass them anonymised through the blinded
judge, and obtain one issue union with per-source statuses. All sources
are scored on the same rows, so recall numbers are directly comparable.
The grading unit (an issue row) is also the unit of review utility: a
review helps when it names a concrete concern. Every input is public, so
the evaluation can be rerun end-to-end.

## 4 Metrics

For every (issue, source) pair the judge writes one of three labels:
Caught, Partial, or Missed. It also tags one source per issue as having
the most thorough treatment: the _best-rigour_ tag. Six numbers
summarise each source $`M`$ over the union of $`N`$ judged issues.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Notation. ‣ 4 Metrics ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
gives the definitions; we use the same symbols throughout the paper.

#### Notation.

We write $`\#\textsc{Caught}{}`$ for the number of issues labelled
Caught for source $`M`$, and likewise $`\#\textsc{Partial}{}`$ and
$`\#\textsc{Missed}{}`$. More generally, $`\#\{i:\,\text{condition}\}`$
denotes the number of issues $`i`$ satisfying that condition.
$`\mathcal{H}`$ denotes the _human-salient_ set: issues the Human source
caught or partially caught. $`|\mathcal{H}|`$ is its size. “$`M`$ hit
$`i`$” is shorthand for status Caught or Partial.

| Metric                                            | Formula                                                     | Plain reading                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Strict recall, $`R_{\mathrm{strict}}(M)`$         | $`\dfrac{\#\textsc{Caught}}{N}`$                            | Share of issues $`M`$ caught outright.                                |
| Partial-inclusive recall, $`R_{\mathrm{hit}}(M)`$ | $`\dfrac{\#\textsc{Caught}+\#\textsc{Partial}}{N}`$         | Share of issues $`M`$ raised at all (full or partial).                |
| Weighted coverage, $`C_{w}(M)`$                   | $`\dfrac{\#\textsc{Caught}+0.5\cdot\#\textsc{Partial}}{N}`$ | Partial mentions count half.                                          |
| Best-rigour share, $`B(M)`$                       | $`\dfrac{\#\{i:\,b_{i}=M\}}{N}`$                            | Share of rows where the judge ranked $`M`$’s treatment most thorough. |
| Agreement with humans, $`R_{M\mid H}`$            | $`\dfrac{\#\{i\in\mathcal{H}:\,M\text{ hit }i\}}{           | \mathcal{H}                                                           | }`$ | Recall on the slice humans themselves raised. |
| Value beyond humans, $`D_{M,\bar{H}}`$            | $`\#\{i\notin\mathcal{H}:\,M\text{ hit }i\}`$               | Count of human-missed issues $`M`$ still catches.                     |

Table 1: Metric definitions. Counts are over the union of $`N`$ judged
issue rows for source $`M`$. $`B(M)`$ closes a loophole in
$`R_{\mathrm{hit}}`$: a source cannot inflate it by mentioning issues
only in passing, because the best-rigour tag requires the most detailed,
evidence-backed treatment on a row. The six metrics answer four
questions: how often $`M`$ raises a real concern ($`R_{\mathrm{hit}}`$,
$`R_{\mathrm{strict}}`$, $`C_{w}`$), how often its treatment is the most
thorough ($`B`$), how well it tracks humans ($`R_{M\mid H}`$), and how
much it adds beyond them ($`D_{M,\bar{H}}`$).

### 5.2 Severity-Stratified Recall

An average can hide a weak spot, so we re-run the comparison inside each
severity stratum. The judge labels every issue as one of three
severities. _Core_ issues threaten the paper’s main contribution;
_important_ issues materially affect a claim; _secondary_ issues are
stylistic, presentational, or minor.
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.2 Severity-Stratified Recall ‣ 5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
shows strict (left) and partial-inclusive (right) recall at each
stratum;
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Severity-Stratified Recall ‣ 5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
prints the exact numbers.

On the 1,313 core rows (the slice that drives accept/reject decisions),
E3 leads by $`\uparrow`$ 3.7 pp over the next-best source: E3 97.9%, GPT
94.2%, Claude 90.9%, Human 68.4%. On the 2,713 important rows the same
ordering holds (E3 92.4% $`\uparrow`$ 16.8 pp over the next-best source;
GPT 75.5%, Claude 72.9%, Human 57.5%). Only the 572 secondary rows
behave differently: automated recall drops (E3 62.1%, GPT 25.7%, Claude
33.2%) while humans hold at 60.8%. Secondary issues are typos, wording,
and presentation, a category humans police well and that a “find
decision-relevant flaws” prompt actively de-prioritises.

## 6 Human-Review Recall and Alignment

Downstream users ultimately judge a critique by how well it tracks what
a human reviewer would have said.
Section [4](#S4 "4 Metrics ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
defined the relevant quantities. The _human-salient_ slice is the 2,805
of 4,598 union rows that the human reviewers caught or partially caught.
The _human-missed_ slice is the 1,793 rows they did not. Two numbers
follow:

- •
  $`R_{M|H}`$: the share of human-salient issues source $`M`$ also
  catches; the operational definition of _agreement with humans_.
- •
  $`D_{M,\bar{H}}`$: the count of human-missed issues source $`M`$
  catches anyway; the operational definition of _value beyond humans_.

On the human-salient slice
(Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6 Human-Review Recall and Alignment ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")),
E3 reaches 89.6% partial-inclusive and 63.7% strict recall, a
$`\uparrow`$ -10.4 pp margin over the next-best source. GPT reaches
78.6% / 49.1% and Claude reaches 76.8% / 44.9%, so the ranking from
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.1 Per-Source Coverage Across the Full Issue Union ‣ 5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
holds on the human-salient subset. On the human-missed slice the Human
source is $`0`$% by definition and the automated sources still hit a
clear majority of admitted concerns.

## 8 Complementarity

Two questions remain. Which source caught issues no other source caught?
Which source covered the largest share of the blind spots left by the
human reviewers?
Figure [8](#S8.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 8 Complementarity ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
answers both in a two-panel layout: the left panel counts _exclusive
discoveries_ (issues only one source caught), the right panel counts
_human-missed coverage_ for the three automated sources (Human is
omitted by construction). E3 contributes the largest counts in both
panels.

- •
  A _unique hit_ is an issue where exactly one source was Caught or
  Partial; remove that source from the protocol and the row vanishes
  from the recovered union.
- •
  A _human-missed hit_ is an issue the source caught while the human
  reviewers missed it, i.e., the source’s contribution beyond what the
  human reviews already covered.

Figure 8: Complementarity by source. Left: Exclusive discoveries: issues
caught by exactly one source and missed by all three others (raw
counts). Right: Human-missed coverage: issues each automated source
caught while the human reviewers missed them; Human is omitted because a
reviewer cannot catch what they missed. Bars are coloured by source for
direct comparison with earlier figures. Raw counts are used because the
operational quantity is the absolute volume of additional concerns each
source brings to a review.

## 9 Discussion

Two conclusions follow from the measurements above.

First, E3 leads on every aggregate metric reported in this study:
partial-inclusive recall ($`\uparrow`$ 15.5 pp over the next-best
source), strict recall, weighted coverage, and best-rigour share
($`\uparrow`$ 29.9 pp). The leading position holds inside every severity
bin and every decision stratum
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Severity-Stratified Recall ‣ 5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique"),
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3 Recall by Severity and Decision Stratum, All Four Sources ‣ 5 Results ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique"))
and inside both the human-salient and the human-missed slices
(Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6 Human-Review Recall and Alignment ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")).
Differences to the next-best non-E3 source range from
$`\uparrow`$ 15.5 pp to $`\uparrow`$ 29.2 pp on $`R_{{\mathrm{{hit}}}}`$
within this corpus.

Second, the GPT and Claude baselines contribute non-trivial coverage.
Figure [6](#S7.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 7 Taxonomy and Error Analysis ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
shows both receiving the best-rigour tag in categories where they did
not lead on overall recall, and
Figure [8](#S8.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 8 Complementarity ‣ E3: Issue-Level Backtesting for Automated Research Critique")
shows both adding unique-hit and human-missed-hit counts. Ensembling at
the issue level could therefore recover a wider union.

## 10 Limitations

Four limitations are worth naming explicitly. (i) The union depends on
the judge model; best-rigour attribution in particular is more
subjective than Caught/Missed assignment. (ii) The meta-judge is itself
a language model: M1–M4 blinding removes name-based bias, but residual
stylistic or terminological affinity between the judge and any
individual LLM-based source cannot be ruled out ([Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib5)). (iii) The taxonomy is keyword-based, so it is
auditable but coarse. (iv) The corpus is ICLR 2026 papers; results may
not transfer to other venues or fields.

## 11 Conclusion

Across 4,598 judged issue rows from 100 ICLR 2026 papers, E3 is the
highest-recall source on every aggregate metric we report: 90.2%
partial-inclusive recall ($`\uparrow`$ 15.5 pp over the next-best
source), 65.8% strict recall, 48.5% best-rigour share
($`\uparrow`$ 29.9 pp), 89.6% agreement with humans, and 1,635
human-missed concerns recovered (406 above the next-best source). The
wider contribution is the protocol itself: an issue-level backtest that
is transparent, reproducible, and applicable to any review system whose
only observable is the critique text.
