###### Abstract

Recently, there is an increasing interest in using artificial
intelligence (AI) to automate aspects of the research process, or even
autonomously conduct the full research cycle from idea generation, over
data analysis, to composing and evaluation of scientific manuscripts.
Examples of working “AI scientist” systems have been demonstrated for
computer science tasks and running molecular biology labs. While some
approaches aim for full autonomy of the scientific AI, others rather aim
for leveraging human-AI teaming. Here, we address how to adapt such
approaches for boosting Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) development, as
well as brain research resp. neuroscience at large. We argue that at
this time, a strong emphasis on human-AI teaming, in contrast to fully
autonomous “AI BCI researcher,” will be the most promising way forward.
We introduce the collaborative workspaces concept for human-AI teaming
based on a set of Janusian design principles, looking both ways, to the
human as well as to the AI side. Based on these principles, we present
ChatBCI, a Python-based toolbox for enabling human-AI collaboration
based on interaction with Large Language Models (LLMs), designed for BCI
research and development projects. We show how ChatBCI was successfully
used in a concrete BCI project on advancing motor imagery decoding from
EEG signals. Our approach can be straightforwardly extended to broad
neurotechnological and neuroscientific topics, and may by design
facilitate human expert knowledge transfer to scientific AI systems in
general.

## 1 Introduction

Human–Artificial Intelligence (AI) collaborations may play an important
role in promoting scientific discovery [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib36).
Efforts to automate research and development tasks have recently led to
the creation of “AI scientists”—AI agents specialized to autonomously
carry out various roles in the scientific process. AI scientists may run
virtual or even physical labs (self-driving labs, SDLs [Rapp et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib29)) and thus help to “super-charge” fields including
biomedical research, material science, or computer science.

For example, in an effort to leverage AI for biomedical research,
Swanson and colleagues have recently created a virtual laboratory that
brings together multiple AI scientists—implemented as large language
models (LLMs) with specialized roles—to collaborate autonomously on
objectives defined by human investigators. This system rapidly proposed
nearly 100 designs for antibody fragments, known as nanobodies, capable
of binding to the virus responsible for COVID-19—accomplishing in a
short span what might otherwise take a purely human team much longer
[Swanson et al. (2024)](#bib.bib34). In the field of computer science,
Lu and colleagues introduced an AI scientist system that aims to
automate the complete research cycle. They also use a LLM to survey
scientific literature, formulate hypotheses, test them by running
experiments, and then compile the results into a written paper. The
system goes a step further by performing its own ‘peer review,’
evaluating and refining the outputs it generates [Lu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib23). However, despite these impressive first
demonstrations, substantial challenges remain for AI-driven scientific
discovery in general, as well as for the adaptation of AI-scientists to
the unique requirements that the scientific domains of research and
development may impose on such a system.

For AI-scientist systems (AISS) in general, for example, one major
challenge is how to integrate the vast amount of implicit, unstructured,
and often context-dependent knowledge and expertise that human
scientists rely on for experimental design, interpretation, and
innovation—which may not be sufficiently represented in the available
training data. For example, Rihm and colleagues argue that current
AI-driven labs are only able to incorporate explicit knowledge such as
recorded data, scientific documentation, and publications and hence only
see the “tip of the iceberg” of the relevant knowledge [Rihm et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib30).

However, even explicit knowledge such as that expressed in scientific
publications may hold problems of its own when used as training data for
scientific AI systems. Geoffrey Boulton and Moumita Koley observed in a
contribution for the International Science Council: “Many scientific
journals and papers maintain high standards, too many lack proper
editorial oversight, many lack rigour and integrity, some engage in
fraudulent practices, \[…\], and agreed standards for overall governance
of the process are lacking.” [International Science Council
(2023)](#bib.bib14). All of these point might compromise training data
quality. This situation, together with the recent “publishing explosion”
(47% growth between 2016 and 2022 in the global number of published
papers [Hanson et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13); nearly 250% increase in the
number of Google Scholar hits for the term “Brain-Computer Interface” in
the same time interval) which may reflect a overemphasis of quantity
compared to quality, may result in quality issues with respect to the
available training data for scientific AI systems. Such problems may be
more or less serious in some research fields compared to others [Bakker
et al. (2012)](#bib.bib2).

Thus, in addition to general challenges such as those related to
training data availability and quality, adaptation of scientific AI to a
specific research field may hold its own challenges—not only due to
differences in research standards. AI systems may face vastly different
requirements depending, for example, on the role of experimental work,
the complexity of the relevant mathematical tools and concepts, or the
degree of adoption of open science practices with resulting differences
in public data and scientific software access. For example, adoption of
scientific AI agents in subfields of machine learning such as diffusion
modeling, transformer-based language modeling, and learning dynamics [Lu
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23) benefits from publicly accessible and
well-documented datasets of, for many relevant questions, adequate size
and quality, as well as from well-established benchmarks. In contrast,
fields such as EEG-based BCI research face much larger problems of small
and diverse datasets of variable data quality. Additional
domain-specific challenges arise due to the complexities of the
foundation of EEG signals in multiscale brain dynamics and their
relation to human cognition, emotion, and behavior [Friston
(2005)](#bib.bib11).

Against this background, our objective in the present work is to adopt
AI scientific agents to the field of human brain research and
Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI)
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research").
To address the general as well as the domain-specific challenges of this
task, we introduce a concept for human-AI workspaces designed to foster
scientific human-AI co-learning and collaboration, summarized in our
Janusian Design Principles. As an instance of this design concept, we
present the LLM-based ChatBCI toolbox.

We show how ChatBCI enabled a human-AI team to perform a prototypical
scientific BCI project based on a public EEG dataset in a very
time-efficient and productive manner, addressing the full cycle of
project phases from data import, exploration, and validation, over
machine learning model selection and implementation (a deep
convolutional neural network as the LLM-generated brain signal decoder),
setting up a training loop with integrated data augmentation, to the
joint interpretation of the obtained results. We illustrate how our
structured interaction framework flexibly supports different levels of
agent autonomy and demonstrate how it allows transfer of expert
knowledge about non-obvious, non-trivial aspects of the EEG dataset
used, from the human expert to the ChatBCI AI agent. This, in turn,
proved a valuable teaching and training partner for the human side.
Thus, we achieved meaningful human-AI co-learning together with
substantially faster collaborative completion of our BCI research task
than would have been possible with a purely human approach. Our approach
holds great potential for teaching and training in the field of human
brain research and BCI development. LLM-generated brain signal decoders
also present a novel class of AutoML for brain signal analysis. The “AI
brain researcher” or “AI BCI researcher” presented here is an instance
of an “AI worker” in general. It will be interesting to study how
abilities learned to handle the complexities of the human brain, and of
the signals that can be measured from it, may also be helpful for AI
workers in other domains. We anticipate that collaborative human-AI
research and development in the field of brain research and BCI, as
enabled by our design concept and ChatBCI toolbox, will pave the way
toward AI systems with a deep and holistic understanding of human brain
function - a "brain-grokking AI" - with a far-reaching application
potential.

## 2 ChatBCI toolbox overview and Janusian Design Principles

The ChatBCI toolbox¹¹ 1 The toolbox and corresponding detailed
documentation will be made publicly available. is fully implemented in
Python, deep learning functionality is relying on PyTorch; in the
present work we use GPT-4o as underlying LLM [OpenAI
(2024)](#bib.bib25). It comprises the following main components:

- •
  A collection of public EEG data sets in ChatBCI compatible format,
  including documented data validation
- •
  EEG and BCI knowledge base
- •
  Core functionality: EEG preprocessing, analysis, decoding models,
  training, interpretability, and visualization
- •
  Auxiliary components and supporting infrastructure, such as for
  generating summaries of data and code directory structures and
  contents at adjustable levels of granularity.
- •
  LLM communication tools.

ChatBCI is by-design build to fostering human-AI collaboration
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research")).
To this aim, we have defined a set of design principles which are the
foundation of our general framework for human-AI teams. In the following
we give a summary of our current working version of these principles. ²²
2 Named after Janus, two-faced Roman God of duality, to express the
basic aspect of our design concept of looking both ways - to the human
side, and to the AI side. We see this duality not as a contradiction but
as a synthesis.

### Working version of the seven Principles of Janusian Design

1.  1.  Speaking the Same Language: Janusian workspaces foster the
        development of a joint collaboration language, minimizing
        misunderstanding and confusion. This enables AI systems to act as
        intuitive partners, aligning with human intentions and promoting
        seamless interactions. To achieve this, the workspace leverages
        intuitive interfaces, contextual feedback, and iterative refinement,
        ensuring that communication between humans and AI becomes
        progressively more natural and effective.
2.  2.  Transparency and Trust: Trust requires mutual clarity and
        predictability. Explainability is a cornerstone, allowing users to
        progressively build confidence in AI systems by understanding their
        decision-making processes. The framework incorporates bidirectional
        transparency, encouraging not only explainable AI (XAI) methods, but
        also mechanisms for AI systems to ask clarifying questions when
        encountering ambiguous inputs or tasks. This fosters a collaborative
        dynamic where both human and AI agents seek to represent/understand
        each other’s intentions, enhancing mutual trust and reducing the
        likelihood of errors.
3.  3.  Building a Shared Knowledge Base: The workspace amplifies human and
        AI collaboration by creating and maintaining a shared knowledge
        base. This repository encodes specialized expert knowledge and
        evolves iteratively. The Janusian Wiki (Jiki) serves as the
        foundation for this knowledge base, providing a structured,
        centralized system for documenting workflows, best practices, and
        domain-specific knowledge. By linking tasks, tools, and insights,
        the Jiki ensures that both human collaborators and AI systems have
        access to the collective intelligence of the workspace, facilitating
        informed decision-making and continuous improvement.
4.  4.  Joint Integration of Priorities: The Janusian framework acknowledges
        that human-AI teams must balance and integrate priorities jointly,
        addressing trade-offs such as the need for immediate actions versus
        long-term objectives, or reconciling priorities driven by strategic
        considerations or overarching research questions ("top-down") with
        reactive, task-specific responses ("bottom-up").
5.  5.  Adaptive Autonomy: AI systems adjust their level of autonomy based
        on task demands, enabling granular human supervision where needed
        while offering higher degrees of independence for routine or
        low-stakes tasks. For example, in the ChatBCI toolbox, the adaptive
        autonomy principle is exemplified through Collaborative Test-Driven
        Development (TDD), where the AI autonomously generates test cases
        and mock data for routine validations, while human oversight is
        applied to critical or ambiguous scenarios. This flexibility ensures
        that the level of autonomy dynamically aligns with the complexity
        and importance of the task.
6.  6.  Accessibility from Novice to Expert: The workspace aims to balance
        accessibility for novice users, such as those in teaching and
        training scenarios, with maintaining advanced functionality tailored
        for expert users. It also differentiates how information from these
        scenarios is processed and utilized on the AI side, ensuring that
        insights from both novice interactions and expert workflows are
        effectively integrated. This inclusive design promotes efficiency
        and reduces the supervision workload, enabling seamless
        collaboration across varying levels of expertise.
7.  7.  Continuous Evolution: A shared Janusian workspace is not static, but
        designed to foster mutual co-learning between humans and AI. This
        principle ensures that the ChatBCI toolbox or other workspaces based
        by-design on this concept not only supports effective and scalable
        data analysis but also fosters a dynamic workspace where both human
        and AI partners can co-evolve to achieve aligned goals.

In summary, the Janusian framework establishes a synergistic foundation
for human-AI collaboration by aligning intuitive interfaces, transparent
processes, and adaptive autonomy with evolving user needs. In the
following, we illustrate how the ChatBCI toolbox based on these design
ideas can be used in a concrete project example.

## 3 Analyzing the BCI competition IV2a dataset using ChatBCI

As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research"),

processes tailored to Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and brain
research. This concept builds upon the approach introduced by [Lu et al.

executing all phases of the research cycle. In contrast, our approach

for flexible levels of shared autonomy, enabling human-AI collaboration

### 3.1 Idea generation

The research ideas presented in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 Idea generation ‣ 3 Analyzing the BCI competition IV2a dataset using ChatBCI ‣ Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research")
were generated using GPT-4o, as our underlying LLM. The model was
provided with a detailed prompt to explore advancements in EEG-based
motor imagery classification within the context of Brain-Computer
Interfaces (BCIs) using the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset [Brunner
et al. (2008)](#bib.bib4). We decided to use this specific dataset
because it has been widely used, maybe the closest to a "MNIST of EEG",
providing a rich background of published results.

The prompt included instructions to identify key challenges, research
gaps, motivations, and potential approaches across a range of topics,
including domain adaptation, lightweight architectures, temporal
dynamics, and explainability. GPT-4o synthesized the list of ideas by
leveraging its training on a wide range of scientific and technical
texts; notably here we did not fine-tune on more specific literature.
Overall, this demonstrates the utility of LLMs in assisting researchers
by rapidly generating diverse, structured, and actionable ideas that may
have merit in guiding future investigations. Notably, the generated
research ideas included indeed notoriously under-researched yet
important topics such as the role of data preprocessing (c.f. example 10
in
 [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 Idea generation ‣ 3 Analyzing the BCI competition IV2a dataset using ChatBCI ‣ Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research")).
On the other hand, an expert in the field in a single glance can find
that all generated research questions have already been addressed by the
research community ([Peterson et al. (2019)](#bib.bib28); [Meng et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib24); [Li et al. (2022)](#bib.bib22); [Jin et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib15); [Kostas et al. (2021)](#bib.bib20); [Kasahara
et al. (2015)](#bib.bib18); [Echtioui et al. (2021)](#bib.bib7); [Saha
and Baumert (2020)](#bib.bib32); [Ju et al. (2020)](#bib.bib16); [Feng
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib9); [Kessler et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19); [Duan
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib5); [Ferrez and Millán (2008)](#bib.bib10)).
Therefore, the introduction of iterative refinement in the process of
idea generation (e.g. like chain of thoughts [Lu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib23)) and tools for novelty verification (through
Semantic Scholar API) and quantification are necessary. Clearly, the
scientific ideas generation stage itself could also benefit from more
sophisticated approaches. For instance, leveraging creative templates
grounded in evolutionary computation and open-endedness principles
offers significant potential. These approaches, as exemplified in works
by [Lu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23) and [Faldor et al. (2024)](#bib.bib8),
use iterative, mutation-driven frameworks to refine and evolve novel
ideas, mimicking the exploratory processes of natural evolution.
Furthermore, combining knowledge graphs with LLM, as demonstrated by [Gu
and Krenn (2024)](#bib.bib12), allows for the synthesis of diverse
interdisciplinary ideas. Another promising approach is curiosity-driven
learning, as proposed by [Pathak et al. (2017)](#bib.bib27), which
emphasizes exploring the unknown and prioritizing novelty over immediate
utility.

However, it was also observable that many of the proposed topics were
under-specified and lacked exact knowledge of what has already been done
and reported in the relevant literature. For example, the suggestion was
to explore lightweight neural networks that achieve state-of-the-art
accuracy while being optimized for low-power devices, which is a
promising topic, but one that is already well-trodden in the field.
Studies like the widely known EEGNet paper [Lawhern et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib21) have specifically addressed this challenge by
designing compact, efficient neural networks tailored to EEG data.
Therefore, while this idea aligns with key goals in the field, it lacks
specificity in identifying novel directions. For instance, future
advancements might focus on extending lightweight models to handle more
diverse datasets, integrating adaptive techniques that dynamically scale
complexity based on resource availability, or innovating in model
interpretability for constrained devices. Without domain-specific
fine-tuning, the model had also difficulties in retrieving the published
SoA in decoding accuracies on the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset. While a
re-implementation of the FBCSP winning model of the original BCI
Competition IV yielded 67.8% accuracy across the 4 motor imagery
classes, and convnets allowed for a gain of approx. 4% [Schirrmeister
et al. (2017)](#bib.bib33). In the meanwhile, accuracies close to 100%
have been reported (e.g., 97.61% in [Xie and Oniga (2023)](#bib.bib38)).

On this background, we decided for the following, human-specified topic
for the first ChatBCI project: What do the close to 100% decoding
accuracies reported on the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset mean? To which
extent do they reflect sophistication of the brain signal decoding
methodology, or possibly also over-fitting to this (relatively small)
dataset, and/or contributions from non-brain signals (artifacts which
may contain class-specific information and might be easier to decode
than brain signals)? The BCI Competition IV 2a data set is particularly
interesting in this context, as it was part of the original competition
instructions that since three EOG channels were provided, it was
"required to remove EOG artifacts before the subsequent data processing
using artifact removal techniques such as highpass filtering or linear
regression" [Brunner et al. (2008)](#bib.bib4). The winning entry of the
competition and based on their approach, also subsequent works on the
same data set used a 4-Hz high-pass filter to address this requirement
[Ang et al. (2012)](#bib.bib1) [Tangermann et al. (2012)](#bib.bib35)
[Schirrmeister et al. (2017)](#bib.bib33).

Thus, for our first ChatBCI project, we set two objectives:

- •
  Perform an exploratory data analysis of the BCI Competition IV 2a data
  set
- •
  Design, implement and test a convolutional neural network (CNN)
  decoder with a good tradeoff of simplicity and hence interpretability
  on the one side, and meaningful decoding results in terms of decoding
  accuracies on the other, including a corresponding within-subject
  training loop.

We addressed these objectives using the ChatBCI toolbox in a human-AI
collaborative framework (Stage 2 "Experiment Iteration" according to our
generic research cycle plan

#### 3.2.1 Exploratory data analysis and data validation

Analysis for data exploration and validation let to several interesting
observations. ChatBCI suggested initially three starting steps for data
validation and exploration. First, examine the basic signal statistics
to understand the distribution and variability of the EEG data across
classes. This involves calculating the mean, standard deviation, and
variance for each channel and class, identifying systematic differences
in signal variance, and detecting outliers, which may indicate
artefacts. Second, analyze event-related potentials (ERPs) to
investigate time-locked neural activity by segmenting the data around
key events, calculating average ERP waveforms for each class, and
identifying distinctive features such as P300 peaks while ensuring the
observed differences are neural rather than artefact-related. Third,
explore class-specific differences in the frequency domain by computing
power spectral density (PSD) for each class and channel, identifying
frequency bands where power varies, and detecting potential artefacts
such as line noise or muscle activity.

#### 3.2.2 LLM-generated deep neural network decoder and training loop

Not surprisingly, given the well-documented abilities of LLMs in code
generation ([Rozière et al. (2024)](#bib.bib31)), ChatBCI was able to
design and implement both deep networks for EEG decoding and working
training loops, including plausibly motivated data-augmentation
strategies. Figure

shows results obtained with a simple network consisting of a sequence of
spatial and temporal convolutions, using batch normalization, dropout,
and a SWISH activation function, with within-subject training using the

Results showed clear learning on the validation set across all subjects,
reaching accuracies, on the one hand, clearly above chance, and on the
other hand, also substantially below previous reports [Zhang et al.

point for, in a next step, implementing interpretability tools, and
gradually moving to more complex decoding models. Setting up the network
as well as the training loop used in this example, in total required
less than 10 prompts.

Another next logical step would be the implementation of hyperparameter
optimization and neural architecture search functionality in ChatBCI,
for advancing to models with SoA accuracy or beyond. Note that this
would potentially enable novel approaches for AutoML leveraging LLMs,
without the necessity for expert knowledge of underlying AutoML
libraries and toolboxes. We foresee that ChatBCI would also be
particularly useful in BCI decoding benchmarking tasks, which require
structured large-scale approaches which may especially align with the
specific strengths of artificial compared to human intelligence.

## 4 Conclusions

This paper introduced the ChatBCI toolbox as a practical and innovative
solution to foster human-AI collaboration in the fields of
Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) research and brain signal analysis. By
adopting our Janusian Design Principles, we demonstrated how human-AI
teaming can address challenges in BCI development, such as small
datasets, complex signal dynamics, and knowledge transfer. Specifically:

1.  1.  Collaborative Human-AI Workspaces: The ChatBCI framework was built
        to integrate the strengths of both human expertise and AI
        capabilities, providing an adaptable, interactive workspace that
        supports all phases of scientific research, from idea generation to
        experimental analysis and result interpretation.
2.  2.  Application to Motor Imagery Decoding: Using the ChatBCI toolbox, we
        explored the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset and implemented a deep
        learning decoder in a collaborative, time-efficient manner. This
        process highlighted the utility of ChatBCI for accelerating
        workflows, identifying domain-specific challenges, and fostering
        human-AI co-learning.
3.  3.  Transfer of Expertise and Knowledge: The structured interaction
        framework enabled effective transfer of non-obvious, domain-specific
        knowledge from human experts to the AI, ensuring meaningful
        co-learning. The AI, in turn, proved to be a valuable partner,
        suggesting analyses, generating code, and adapting to user inputs.
4.  4.  Scalable Design for Broader Neurotechnological Applications: While
        demonstrated for a specific EEG dataset, the principles and methods
        behind ChatBCI are extensible to a wide range of neuroscience and
        neurotechnology challenges, laying a foundation for future
        advancements in brain research and AI-driven discovery.

By merging the adaptability of AI systems with the nuanced understanding
of human researchers, ChatBCI represents a step forward in achieving
efficient and collaborative research processes. Our findings indicate
that human-AI teaming, as enabled by ChatBCI, holds significant promise
for accelerating scientific discovery while maintaining the
interpretability and trust essential for impactful advancements in
neuroscience and BCI.

We envision a future where in some scenarios brain-computer interfaces
(BCIs) transcend traditional keyboard inputs, replacing them with direct
interaction through physiological signals—a transformative goal of BCI
research and development. For instance, advances like Meta’s silent
speech decoding demonstrate the potential of such technologies to enable
seamless communication between humans and machines [Défossez et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib6). This innovation aligns perfectly with the evolving
paradigm of human-AI collaboration, where neural interfacing could
ensure seamless interaction and integration of human-AI teams. By
integrating robust privacy and security measures ([Kapitonova et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib17)), such systems could enable richer societal
interactions and more immersive experiences in scientific discovery,
healthcare, and beyond. The convergence of neurotechnology and AI
promises not only to amplify human cognitive capabilities but also to
unlock new paradigms for how humans and machines collaborate to solve
complex problems.

Through the type of collaborative teamwork on brain data enabled by
ChatBCI, AI systems may progressively develop a deep and holistic
understanding of human brain function. By engaging with the complexities
of brain signals, their multiscale dynamics, and their connections to
cognition, behavior, and emotion, such AI systems may transcend
conventional analytical approaches. This emerging paradigm has potential
not only in neuroscience and neurotechnology but also fields such as
medicine, psychology, and education. Furthermore, a "brain-grokking AI"
could unlock significant performance advantages in areas that rely on
human brain function, including optimized mental health interventions
and tailored cognitive training, underscoring the importance of human-AI
collaboration as a cornerstone for advancing knowledge and unlocking new
possibilities in science and technology.
