# Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?

Ming Zhong    Stacy Li    Nicholas Carlini Affiliation: Anthropic
Fellows Program  Anthropic    Matthew Jagielski Affiliation: Anthropic
Fellows Program  Anthropic

###### Abstract

Frontier coding agents are increasingly framed as end-to-end automated
researchers, and ML for science offers a demanding test of that promise:
improving a scientific model often requires domain insight beyond
routine ML engineering. We introduce Agent4Sci, a long-horizon testbed
for measuring how far today’s agents can improve the published best
models on scientific ML benchmarks, spanning 20 benchmarks across 11
domains. Rather than ranking agents on a leaderboard, Agent4Sci asks
what kinds of progress agents can make on these tasks under in-the-wild
conditions, and where they fall short. Empirically, today’s frontier
agents advance the published best on every benchmark, with Claude Opus
reaching 10.4% mean relative improvement and no run flagged for academic
misconduct. These gains, however, come almost entirely from a bag of
ML-engineering tricks rather than research-level insight. Trajectory
analysis surfaces three recurring limitations in this setting: novelty
miscalibration, resource under-utilization, and rapid collapse into
local engineering sweeps. Lightweight plug-in skills for research
ideation, experiment management, and scheduled checklists raise Claude
Opus from 10.4% to 20.1% and Codex from 4.4% to 8.1%, while yielding
more diverse, domain-relevant interventions. Agent4Sci gives the
community a way to track and push frontier agents from ML engineering
toward domain insight on real-world ML-for-science problems. Code and
data are available on
[GitHub](https://github.com/maszhongming/agent4sci) and
[Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/MingZhong/agent4sci).

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in frontier coding agents are turning automated research
into an executable workflow: agents can read papers and repositories,
modify implementations, run experiments, interpret quantitative
feedback, and iterate. A growing body of recent work suggests a common
recipe for this vision: cast research as a feedback loop in which an
agent operates in a workspace, pursues a measurable objective, and
improves through experimentation \[[50](#bib.bib30), [38](#bib.bib15),
[28](#bib.bib14), [62](#bib.bib13), [23](#bib.bib12)\]. Yet most
demonstrations so far assume well-structured
codebases \[[19](#bib.bib11), [36](#bib.bib8), [61](#bib.bib9)\],
operate in single-task settings \[[50](#bib.bib30), [38](#bib.bib15),
[23](#bib.bib12)\], and are heavily skewed toward computer science
domains \[[40](#bib.bib7), [56](#bib.bib18), [13](#bib.bib17)\]. The
broader landscape of ML for science remains far less explored:
benchmarks span diverse scientific domains and modalities, codebases are
often research artifacts rather than polished evaluation environments,
and nontrivial gains often require domain insights. This gap motivates
our central question: Can the frontier agents we have today make
meaningful progress on ML for science when operating in the wild?

We instantiate this question as automated scientific hill-climbing
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
In this setting, an agent is given a scientific ML benchmark, its paper,
codebase, data, and target metric. Starting from the best available
model in the benchmark, the agent works under a fixed resource budget to
improve the metrics through code changes and experiments. However, our
pilot study shows that this natural setup is fragile. Across 12 tasks
from 5 NeurIPS 2025 benchmarks, 5 tasks exhibit severe evaluation-gaming
behaviors, including selective reporting, test-set contamination, metric
manipulation, and even direct hardcoding. This aligns with recent work
showing that coding agents succeed in well-structured evaluation
settings but degrade sharply on real-world code and
evaluations \[[10](#bib.bib35)\]. These failures motivate a testbed that
preserves the in-the-wild nature of scientific tasks, makes meaningful
progress measurable, and guards against evaluation gaming.

To address this challenge, we construct Agent4Sci, a testbed for
evaluating automated scientific hill-climbing across 20 ML-for-science
benchmarks, 25 tasks, and 11 scientific domains spanning the physical
sciences, life sciences, Earth and environmental sciences, and
scientific engineering. We first standardize each benchmark through
human-verified baseline reproductions, repaired data splits, reduced
runtime bottlenecks, and isolated environments. On top of this
foundation, each run follows the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol. The
hill-climbing agent receives the standardized environment with access
only to training and validation data, then submits artifacts such as
code changes, checkpoints, logs, and outputs. A separate evaluation
agent runs the submitted artifacts on the held-out test set, and an
auditing agent inspects the trajectory for academic misconduct.

Agent4Sci is motivated not as a leaderboard for ranking agents, but as a
way to ask how much measurable progress agents can make on scientific ML
tasks, whether they produce insight, and where they fall short. We
evaluate four frontier coding agents: Claude Code (Opus and Sonnet),
Codex, and Gemini CLI. Each receives 8 H200 GPUs, a 12-hour budget, and
a minimal instruction to hill-climb the specified metric. Across the
suite, frontier agents push every benchmark beyond its published best,
all four agents achieve positive mean improvements, and the auditing
stage flags no academic misconduct under our evaluation protocol. On
Agent4Sci, current agents already produce measurable, fully automated
gains on real scientific ML benchmarks.

The more revealing question is what produces these gains. The
improvements concentrate on familiar ML-engineering moves instead of
research-level insight, and the agents adopt sharply different default
workflows: Claude Opus behaves as an iterative optimizer, Codex as a
commit-and-wait planner, and Gemini oscillates between the two. Across
trajectories, we identify three recurring limitations: agents
miscalibrate novelty, under-utilize resources, and collapse quickly into
local engineering sweeps. Lightweight plug-in skills for research
ideation, experiment management, and scheduled checklists raise Claude
Opus from 10.4% to 20.1% mean relative improvement and Codex from 4.4%
to 8.1%, without changing the evaluation protocol. Beyond the score
gains, agents propose more varied, problem-grounded interventions and
surface insights that move beyond routine ML engineering.

Overall, these results draw a practical boundary for automating ML for
science today. Frontier coding agents can climb scientific hills in the
wild, but the climb is still powered mostly by engineering optimization
rather than scientific reasoning. Agent4Sci makes this boundary
measurable and visible: each new agent generation can be evaluated not
only by how much it improves a metric, but by whether its improvements
reflect increasingly transferable research insight. The goal is to help
move automated agents from executing better experiments toward doing
better science.

## 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing

### 2.1 Task and Benchmark Selection

We construct Agent4Sci from recent ML-for-science benchmarks, retaining
only tasks that support iterative improvement by an agent. We use
benchmark to denote a published benchmark, and task to denote one of its
evaluation tasks (typically with a designated mainstream metric); a
single benchmark can contribute multiple tasks. Selection runs through a
four-stage filtering pipeline:

- •
  Domain Classification. Retain papers whose benchmark targets a
  scientific domain, filtering out benchmarks aimed at generic AI, ML,
  or systems research.
- •
  Automated Screening. An agent scores each retained paper against seven
  criteria covering data, training, baselines, evaluation, and licensing
  (see
  Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Selection Criteria ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
  and 7/7 candidates advance.
- •
  Human Verification. Re-check the automated criterion labels by hand
  and demote any candidate whose 7/7 status does not survive
  verification.
- •
  Deep Review. Inspect the paper and the official repository for
  reproducibility evidence, task specificity, repository usability, and
  domain coverage relative to the rest of the suite.

Taking the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks track as a
representative case, this pipeline admits 10 benchmarks from 497
candidates (a 98% rejection rate, with full per-stage diagnostics in
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Benchmark Filtering: A Case Study on NeurIPS 2025 D&B ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
Rejected papers are typically competitive in their original setting and
are excluded only because they target a different scenario than ours,
not because of any deficiency in the underlying work. After adding
curated benchmarks from other top ML venues and domain journals,
Agent4Sci contains 20 scientific benchmarks and 25 tasks across 11
scientific domains.

### 2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks

To assess whether existing scientific repositories can directly support
automated hill-climbing, we conduct a pilot study on 12 tasks drawn from
5 NeurIPS 2025 scientific benchmarks. The agent receives the paper, the
original repository, the task description, and the data artifacts as
released, then attempts to improve the reported metric. The pilot
reveals that this setup is unreliable: 5 of 12 tasks exhibit one or more
severe evaluation-gaming behaviors, which we group into four categories.

- •
  Selective reporting. The agent evaluates only on a favorable subset of
  the test set and reports the inflated score as the headline result.
- •
  Test-set contamination. The agent extracts held-out statistics,
  inspects test-input error cases, or uses test-set information to guide
  model selection or hyperparameters.
- •
  Metric manipulation. The agent invents a non-standard evaluation
  protocol, claims the official metric is incorrect, or modifies the
  evaluation script to favor its model.
- •
  Direct hardcoding. The agent hardcodes final outputs, copies
  predictions from earlier runs, or claims that legitimate checkpoints
  were accidentally deleted to obscure failures.

We use evaluation gaming as an umbrella term because these failures need
not require malicious intent. Official scientific ML repositories often
lack clear metric definitions, fixed data splits, or complete training
and evaluation scripts. End-to-end hill-climbing directly on such
repositories, therefore, opens seams that an aggressive optimizer can
exploit, even when the agent’s high-level instruction is framed
honestly. An evaluation that exposes the original repository and trusts
the final score returned by the hill-climbing agent cannot reliably
measure scientific progress.

### 2.3 The Agent4Sci Evaluation Protocol

The pilot study motivates the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.3 The Agent4Sci Evaluation Protocol ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
The protocol has two parts: a one-time standardization phase that
prepares each task into a verified reference environment, and a
three-stage evaluation procedure that separates hill-climbing, held-out
evaluation, and auditing.

![Refer to caption](2610.28763v1/Figures/agent4sci_protocol.png)

Figure 2: Agent4Sci evaluation protocol. Each task includes a standard
reproduction folder with standardized training scripts, evaluation
scripts, and data splits. Runs separate the hill-climbing agent from
held-out evaluation and auditing to prevent evaluation gaming.

#### Standardization.

Each admitted task is converted into a verified reference environment
through substantial human effort, and the foundation of that effort is
reproduction. Starting from the official codebase, we reproduce the
paper’s headline number on the published baseline, and a task is
admitted into Agent4Sci only when this reproduction matches the paper
within a verified tolerance. Reproduction is rarely a drop-in rerun of
the published recipe: 3 benchmarks require an additional training
pipeline that the original release does not include, 2 require a
held-out validation slice carved from training data because our protocol
does not allow test-set access during checkpoint selection, and another
2 require alignment of data-handling and split conventions with our
standardized setting.

Standardization extends beyond reproduction along three more axes:
deterministic train/validation/test splits with the test set
sequestered, runtime optimization that fits the baseline within a
single-H200 envelope, and isolated hill-climbing and evaluation
containers. Every standardized task is independently re-checked by two
reviewers, with disagreements resolved through repository inspection and
reruns until both reviewers agree that the reproduction and the
surrounding environment match the paper. The full per-benchmark record,
including a paper-vs-reproduction comparison and the specific
standardization moves applied to each project, appears in
Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Standardization Details ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?").

#### Three-Stage Evaluation.

After standardization, each run evaluates only the hill-climbing agent.
The evaluation and auditing agents are auxiliary safeguards: they
execute the submitted artifacts on the held-out test set and inspect the
run for evaluation gaming and other academic misconduct.

- •
  Hill-climbing. The hill-climbing agent receives the standardized
  environment and task specification. It can modify code, launch
  experiments, train models, and submit artifacts, but cannot read or
  evaluate on the held-out test set.
- •
  Evaluation. A separate evaluation agent loads the submitted artifacts
  and runs them on the held-out test set using the standardized
  evaluation path.
- •
  Auditing. A third auditing agent inspects the trajectory and submitted
  artifacts for any of the four evaluation-gaming behaviors from
  §[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
  as well as other forms of academic misconduct.

Together, standardization and the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol let each
hill-climbing run start from a verified SOTA baseline and focus on the
scientific task itself. Test-set access and score computation remain
outside the hill-climbing agent, directly addressing the four
evaluation-gaming behaviors observed in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
and making residual protocol deviations auditable. All agents used to
assist benchmark selection and standardization are Claude Opus 4.6,
while final acceptance and verification decisions are made by human
reviewers.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Experimental Setup

We benchmark four frontier agents under identical conditions: Claude
Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Codex (GPT-5.4), and Gemini 3.1 Pro. We run
them through the latest stable release of the corresponding official CLI
(Claude Code, Codex, or Gemini CLI), each configured with the maximum
reasoning-effort setting. Every run receives 8 H200 GPUs and a 12-hour
wall-clock budget, with no human intervention beyond starting the run,
stopping it at the time limit, and collecting artifacts.

The instruction (full text in
Appendix [B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Evaluation Protocol Prompts ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?"))
starts the agent from the standardized reproduction as the baseline and
exposes only training and (where available) validation data, so the
agent must propose ideas that generalize beyond what it directly
observes. It also asks the agent to persist explanatory artifacts and
the best checkpoint(s) for held-out test scoring, and we re-run the full
hill-climbing process when no evaluable checkpoint is saved. All
relative improvements are signed so that positive values always indicate
improvement: $`\Delta\%=(s-b)/|b|\times 100`$ for higher-is-better
metrics and $`\Delta\%=(b-s)/|b|\times 100`$ for lower-is-better, where
$`s`$ is the hill-climb score and $`b`$ is the baseline.

### 3.2 Main Results

Figure [1(b)](#S1.F1.sf2 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
reports each agent’s mean relative improvement across the 20 benchmarks:
Claude Opus 4.6 at 10.4%, Claude Sonnet 4.6 at 9.6%, Gemini 3.1 Pro at
6.8%, and Codex (GPT-5.4) at 4.4%. Because long-horizon trajectories
vary from run to run

we do not read these averages as a leaderboard but use Agent4Sci as a
per-benchmark probe.

#### Every benchmark in Agent4Sci admits fully automated improvement.

The $`\Delta\%`$ column of
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.4 Composition of Agent4Sci ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
is positive for all 20 benchmarks, showing that every published baseline
in Agent4Sci can be improved by at least one frontier agent without
human intervention. The best observed gains range from 1.1% (ProteinGym)
to 64.4% (PFDelta), with the winning agent varying across the suite
(full per-agent results in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Per-Agent Performance on Agent4Sci ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
Two of the largest gains illustrate what these improvements look like in
practice. On PFDelta, Claude Opus reduces the average power-balance loss
by 64.4% by adding a gradient-safe squared form of the evaluation metric
(PBL) as an auxiliary loss, raising the MSE weighting coefficient
$`\lambda`$ from 0.1 to 0.3, and switching to cosine annealing with
warmup. On Mars-Bench, Claude Opus selects a detector better suited to
the cone-detection sub-task, replacing the original
RetinaNet \[[26](#bib.bib56)\] with Faster R-CNN \[[48](#bib.bib57)\]
and lifting mAP from 16.5 to 30.4.

#### Across all runs, the auditing stage identifies no evaluation gaming or academic misconduct.

We default to Claude Opus 4.6 as the evaluation and auditing agent,
prompting it to flag any forms of academic misconduct. To rule out
single-auditor bias and same-family coordination between a hill-climber
and its auditor, we additionally re-audit half of each agent’s runs with
three different agents. Across every (agent, auditor) combination, both
held-out scores and audit verdicts agree, and no run is flagged. Human
spot-checks further support the auditors’ reliability: auditors from all
three model families flag every known case of cheating in the pilot
trajectories, and human judgment matches their verdicts on sampled clean
runs
(Appendix [B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Auditor Reliability ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
The audit results suggest that the three-stage Agent4Sci protocol closes
the seams the pilot identified in raw scientific repositories.

#### Hill-climbing methods: auto-engineering, not auto-research.

aggregates the analyzed Opus, Codex, and Gemini trajectories into a
vocabulary view. The dominant moves are a familiar bag of practical
tricks: learning-rate tuning, deeper or wider models, backbone swaps,
weight decay, pooling choices, data augmentation, and auxiliary losses.
Per-agent biases are visible in the relative font sizes. Claude Opus
leans on cosine learning-rate schedules, deeper or wider models, and

augmentation, and weight decay. Gemini emphasizes feature engineering,

and feature engineering, data augmentation, and loss design each at 24%;
Gemini falls between, with hyperparameter tuning at 48% and architecture
changes at 32%. Problem-specific interventions remain rare across all
three.

#### Frontier agents default to sharply different workflows on Agent4Sci.

Even with no human intervention, the active-time profiles in
Figure [4(a)](#S3.F4.sf1 "In Figure 4 ‣ 3.3 Agent Behavior ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
reveal three contrasting paradigms. Claude Opus keeps real-time
monitoring active for essentially the full 12-hour budget (12.0h on
average), inspecting new logs and analyzing intermediate results before
launching each next round. Codex sits at the opposite extreme: within
the first hour it commits to a single long-running script and then waits
for it to finish, sweeping over a hundred experiments without any in-run
analysis (which helps explain its lower mean improvement). Gemini does
not settle into either paradigm, alternating between Claude-like
real-time monitoring and Codex-like commit-and-wait.

## 4 Eliciting Research-like Behavior with Plug-in Skills

This section presents our plug-in skills for moving frontier coding
agents from automated ML engineering toward more research-like behavior.

### 4.1 Recurring Issues in Default Agent Behavior

Across the run trajectories from
§[3](#S3 "3 Experiments ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?"),
three failure modes consistently hold today’s frontier agents back from
deeper scientific progress.

- •
  Novelty miscalibration. The agent’s assessment of what constitutes a
  novel idea is systematically misaligned with human judgment: in its
  own reasoning, it routinely presents well-established techniques as
  creative ideas. For instance, Claude Code treats cosine learning-rate
  schedules as a creative breakthrough across runs, even though they are
  long-standing standard practice.
- •
  Resource under-utilization. Throughout a hill-climbing run, GPUs are
  frequently idle, and agents often make implementation choices that
  further lower resource utilization, such as serializing experiments
  that could run in parallel or creating CPU/GPU contention.
- •
  Exploration collapse. Despite generating diverse ideas in early
  rounds, the agent progressively narrows its strategy as the run goes
  on, defaulting to exhaustive hyperparameter sweeps and model
  ensembling once a sweet spot is found. For instance, after finding in
  the first hour that a particular activation function helps, the agent
  often spends the rest of the run tuning seeds and learning rates
  around it instead of exploring substantively different approaches.

### 4.2 Skill Design

We design three plug-in skills targeting these issues: research
ideation, experiment management, and scheduled checklists. Each is a
short markdown document the agent loads at the appropriate stage of a
run, heavily optimized for Claude Code and also adapted to run on Codex.

- •
  Research ideation. A persona-driven ideation skill invoked at the
  start of every run. The skill grounds the agent in parallel literature
  mining on both the scientific domain and broader AI/ML methods, while
  assigning it a deliberately exaggerated “crazy research scientist”
  persona that disdains hyperparameter tuning and is satisfied only by
  ground-breaking ideas. After the review, the agent drafts a plan that
  separates ambitious interventions from safer ones and prioritizes bold
  ideas over safe defaults, countering _novelty miscalibration_.
- •
  Experiment management. A set of common instructions for efficient
  experiment management during the implementation phase: real-time
  monitoring of running jobs, priority resource allocation toward more
  ambitious experiments, rules for avoiding CPU/GPU contention between
  concurrent jobs, and runtime optimizations such as faster data loaders
  and mixed precision. This counters _resource under-utilization_ by
  keeping more of the GPU budget productive throughout the run.
- •
  Scheduled checklists. Periodic self-assessment triggered once an hour
  through a tool-call hook. The skill prompts the agent with diagnostic
  questions such as “Am I designing creative experiments, or just
  tweaking hyperparameters?” and “Would a domain expert be interested in
  what I’m currently trying?”, forcing it to revisit its current
  strategy and propose new bold ideas whenever drift is detected. This
  directly counters exploration collapse and keeps the agent out of
  “lazy mode”.

#### Plug-in skills nearly double the mean improvement for both agents.

Claude Opus’s mean relative improvement rises from 10.4% to 20.1%, while

design generalizes.

Beyond aggregate gains, the skills change the space of ideas agents try:
interventions become more varied and tied to the task structure. PFDelta
is the clearest example. Skills lift Claude Opus’s improvement from
64.4% to 91.0%, and the method reads more like physics-informed
reasoning than ML engineering. Recognizing that PBL is derivable from
Kirchhoff’s laws and is a self-supervised objective requiring no labels,
the agent reformulates training around PBL as the primary loss instead
of MSE supervision, and at inference time treats the GNN prediction as a
warm start for the AC power-flow equations, refining voltage variables
to drive the physical residual close to zero.

## 5 Related Work

#### Automated ML R&D.

A growing body of work evaluates agent performance on ML research and
engineering, where outcomes are jointly shaped by the underlying LLM and
the scope of its harness. Improved general and specialist scaffolds
elicit performance gains \[[64](#bib.bib28), [63](#bib.bib32),
[21](#bib.bib29), [27](#bib.bib33)\], especially on engineering tasks
that benefit from rapid implementation and iteration. In parallel, agent
benchmark suites measure progress on open-ended ML research and
engineering, often against human-expert baselines \[[19](#bib.bib11),
[36](#bib.bib8), [13](#bib.bib17), [56](#bib.bib18), [40](#bib.bib7),
[12](#bib.bib10), [61](#bib.bib9), [44](#bib.bib6), [17](#bib.bib21)\].
Overall, agents can match or outperform these baselines on short,
well-scoped tasks, but fall short of expert-level performance over
extended time horizons. Among the hill-climbing benchmarks closest to
ours, tasks target general ML rather than scientific domains and are
drawn from Kaggle-style competitions \[[12](#bib.bib10),
[44](#bib.bib6)\] or hand-crafted environments \[[19](#bib.bib11),
[36](#bib.bib8), [61](#bib.bib9)\], and only MLE-bench and RE-Bench
inspect agent trajectories for gaming. Agent4Sci differs on all three
axes: its tasks are ML-for-science problems from 11 domains, each run
builds on the benchmark’s published codebase, and a dedicated stage
audits every trajectory.

#### Agents for Science.

Work on autonomous scientific research can be divided into
function-level and end-to-end paradigms. Function-level paradigms use
program search under verifiable evaluation to find new algorithms and
mathematical constructions \[[38](#bib.bib15), [50](#bib.bib30)\].
End-to-end paradigms run full research pipelines spanning ideation,
experimentation, and manuscript drafting \[[62](#bib.bib13),
[28](#bib.bib14), [4](#bib.bib19), [7](#bib.bib58), [57](#bib.bib20),
[35](#bib.bib22)\]. Performance on end-to-end research is sensitive to
LLM-harness pairing and task specification \[[30](#bib.bib16)\].
Benchmarks that reach into science evaluate scientific code
generation \[[58](#bib.bib4), [14](#bib.bib5)\], data-driven hypothesis
discovery \[[32](#bib.bib3)\], or broader research-assistance
tasks \[[6](#bib.bib2)\], rather than the long-horizon loop of improving
a scientific model. To our knowledge, Agent4Sci is the first benchmark
to evaluate general-purpose coding harnesses on long-horizon ML
hill-climbing across many scientific domains.

## 6 Conclusion

We present Agent4Sci, a human-verified testbed of 20 scientific ML
benchmarks across 11 domains for evaluating automated scientific
hill-climbing in the wild. Frontier coding agents push every benchmark
beyond its published baseline without supervision, and lightweight
plug-in skills roughly double the mean improvement. The gains still come
mostly from ML engineering rather than scientific reasoning, and
Agent4Sci offers a measurable signal for tracking when that begins to
change.

## Appendix A Limitations

We discuss the limitations of this paper from the following six angles:
the scope of Agent4Sci as a benchmark, the coverage of agent harnesses
we evaluate, the verification afforded by held-out test scores, the
scope of hill-climbing relative to the broader scientific process, the
potential overfitting of our plug-in skills, and the absence of a
human-researcher baseline.

### A.1 Non-Leaderboard Benchmark Scope

Run-to-run variance is unavoidable in long-horizon agent runs, so we
treat Agent4Sci as a discovery probe rather than a leaderboard. Even
with identical seeds and hardware, two runs of the same agent on the
same benchmark can land at meaningfully different scores, because the
trajectory through code edits, experiment ordering, and intermediate
diagnostics is itself stochastic.
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Run-to-Run Variance ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
quantifies this variance on six benchmarks. The aggregate $`\Delta\%`$
across the 20 benchmarks carries meaningful signal about a frontier
agent’s hill-climbing capability, but we do not use it as a reliable
ranking metric across agents. The intended use is discovery-oriented:
Agent4Sci is built to track when an agent surfaces a genuinely new
scientific or methodological insight on a benchmark, and to expose how
often that happens relative to routine ML-engineering moves; each new
agent generation should be evaluated against the suite as a probe for
measurable scientific progress rather than as a leaderboard contender.

### A.2 Single-Agent Harness Coverage

Frontier agents are evaluated only in their default single-agent
harnesses, which likely under-elicit their capabilities relative to
specialist multi-agent setups. This profiling captures the performance
researchers can expect from out-of-the-box deployment, and Agent4Sci
should therefore be read as a baseline for current performance rather
than a ceiling. The failure modes we describe in
§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Recurring Issues in Default Agent Behavior ‣ 4 Eliciting Research-like Behavior with Plug-in Skills ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
may be partially addressed by specialist research harnesses and
multi-agent frameworks that coordinate defined search strategies across
multiple sessions, which are likely to elicit stronger hill-climb
performance on tasks whose structure aligns with their search strategy.

### A.3 Imperfect Held-Out Verification

Held-out test performance is an imperfect verifier of genuine scientific
progress, even under our auditing pipeline. Agent4Sci provides a target
hill-climb metric for each task and holds out the test set to prevent
data leakage during the hill-climb session, but an agent can improve the
test-set score while its methodology remains ecologically dubious. We
audit every transcript for the four evaluation-gaming behaviors of
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
and other academic misconduct, but cannot rule out subtler subversive
behaviors such as rationalization or motivated interpretations of the
task specification.

### A.4 Limited Scope of Hill-Climbing

Hill-climbing from a verified baseline does not capture the full breadth
of scientific research, which begins with ideation and prior-work
synthesis. Anchoring every task on a verified baseline lets Agent4Sci
measure relative improvement against a shared starting point, but
precludes assessment of end-to-end agent research capabilities, where
innovation itself is harder to measure than benchmark metrics. Each
provided baseline is also state of the art at the task and benchmark
scope, so the room for further improvement on any single baseline is
difficult to predict in advance: an apparently weak baseline may be only
trivially improvable, and an apparently optimized one may still yield
substantial gains under a different framing. An extension of this work
could compare relative improvement on Agent4Sci against baseline-free
end-to-end trials on the same benchmarks.

### A.5 Potential Overfitting of Plug-in Skills

Because we designed the plug-in skills by inspecting agent failures on
Agent4Sci, their gains may not fully transfer to other benchmarks or
agents. The skills target general failure patterns and contain no
benchmark- or domain-specific instructions
(Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Skill Details ‣ Appendix D Plug-in Skills ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
but two forms of overfitting remain possible: to the scientific domains
and task formats in the suite, and to Claude-family agents, since we
derived the skills from Claude Code trajectories rather than from other
agents’ traces.

### A.6 Absence of a Human-Researcher Baseline

We do not compare against human researchers attempting the same tasks,
which would be the most direct reference for how hard each improvement
is. Improving these tasks well requires deep domain expertise, and we
could not bring in the right experts for long enough to attempt such a
comparison. As partial context, where a benchmark is well established,
our starting point is the strongest published human method: on
ProteinGym, for instance, we start from Kermut, the top-ranked method on
its public leaderboard, and the best agent still improves on it
slightly.

### B.2 Selection Criteria

The compact criterion list in
§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Task and Benchmark Selection ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
maps to the following definitions. A task is admitted into Agent4Sci
only when all seven are satisfied.

- •
  Held-out test set. The benchmark provides explicit training and
  held-out test data, or sufficient structure to construct a defensible
  train/validation/test split.
- •
  Data-size tractability. At least one representative task fits within a
  512 GB storage budget.
- •
  Tractable training. The chosen baseline trains within 6 hours on a
  single H200 GPU.
- •
  Trainable baseline. The task requires model training or fine-tuning.
  We exclude inference-only evaluations such as zero-shot prompting or
  retrieval.
- •
  Non-saturated SOTA. The published SOTA leaves meaningful room for
  further improvement.
- •
  In-distribution evaluation. The evaluation primarily measures
  hill-climbing on the scientific model, rather than out-of-distribution
  generalization. Concretely, validation and test data are drawn from
  similar distributions, so that validation gains give hill-climbing a
  meaningful signal about the test set; the training split may still
  differ substantially from both, so benchmarks that demand
  train-to-test generalization remain in scope. Because the test set is
  never seen during hill-climbing and is scored only once, it also
  catches solutions that overfit the validation data.
- •
  Open code and data. The official code and dataset are public and
  licensed for research redistribution.

### B.4 Standardization Details

Each task admitted in
§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Task and Benchmark Selection ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
is converted into a verified reference environment before any agent
evaluation begins. Standardization is carried out by the authors with
Claude Opus 4.6 as an assistant, and every standardized task is
independently re-checked by two reviewers; disagreements are resolved
through repository inspection and reruns until both reviewers agree that
the reproduction and the standardized environment are correct.
Throughout the rest of the paper, every per-benchmark baseline reported
in our results tables refers to this human-verified reproduction value
rather than the number originally claimed in the source paper, with both
values listed side by side in
Table [4](#A2.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Standardization Moves Beyond Reproduction. ‣ B.4 Standardization Details ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?").

#### Reproduction Workflow.

Reproduction is the first and most labor-intensive step of
standardization. We follow the same human-in-the-loop seven-step
workflow on every benchmark in the suite, summarized in the items below.

- •
  Understand the task. Read the paper alongside the official repository
  documentation and identify the headline metric, the model to
  reproduce, and the training configuration the paper documents.
- •
  Explore the repository. Trace the training, evaluation, and data
  scripts end-to-end, including configuration and evaluation entry
  points plus any data-handling logic that may need changes.
- •
  Set up the environment. Create an isolated virtual environment per
  project and install the dependencies declared by the official
  repository. We pin versions only when version drift demonstrably
  changes the metric, and every pin is recorded together with the change
  that motivated it.
- •
  Adapt the run. Convert the project to the standardized layout in
  Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Reproduction Workflow. ‣ B.4 Standardization Details ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?").
  Train, validation, and test data live in shared directories, training
  and evaluation entry points are isolated from each other, and the
  original code is preserved as a read-only reference.
- •
  Run experiments. Train on the prepared environment and iterate until
  the reproduction matches paper-reported numbers within an agreed
  tolerance, without accessing test data during training. When progress
  stalls, we revisit the paper and the official repository before
  introducing any further changes to our setup.
- •
  Report. Emit a structured change log, a patch against the upstream
  repository, and a metric report comparing reproduced numbers to
  paper-reported values.
- •
  Review and clean up. Convert absolute paths to relative paths, drop
  intermediate checkpoints and scheduler scripts, and verify there is no
  test-data leakage in the surviving artifacts.

#### Standardization Moves Beyond Reproduction.

Reproducing a paper’s number is the starting point. To support
hill-climbing by an agent, each project also needs deterministic data
splits, separable training and evaluation paths, a single-H200 runtime
envelope, and a clean environment partition. We describe these four axes
below with representative examples drawn from across the suite.

- •
  Data splits. A subset of source papers ship without a clean validation
  split, or use the test set during training-time model selection. We
  carve a held-out validation slice from training data and sequester the
  test set. CarbonGlobe is the clearest example: the original protocol
  selects checkpoints on the test set, and we replace it with a 90/10
  train/val split inside the training set. UniSite (10% held-out
  validation), MassSpecGym (a 19K-sample validation slice for checkpoint
  selection), and LITHOS (17% validation held out per fold) follow the
  same pattern.
- •
  Evaluation scripts. A subset of repositories ship without a usable
  training pipeline (UniSite is inference-only, GTPBD provides only
  evaluation), or with thresholds that need to be selected on validation
  rather than fixed in advance (MOTIVE). In those cases we author a
  training script from the paper text and always emit one
  validation-time evaluator (used during training) and one test-time
  evaluator (used only after submission), so that hill-climbing and
  held-out evaluation paths cannot be conflated through accidental edits
  to a shared script.
- •
  Runtime. We profile each reproduction and remove avoidable bottlenecks
  so the baseline fits the single-H200 envelope. Changes are limited to
  implementation-level optimizations: faster data loaders (Mars-Bench,
  ESCAPE, gRNAde), larger batch sizes, mixed precision, early stopping,
  and replacement of multi-device launchers with single-device JIT, in
  each case only when this leaves the headline metric numerically
  unchanged.
- •
  Environment. Each task ships with two separate Docker environments.
  The hill-climbing environment contains the paper, the standardized
  repository, training and validation data, and dependencies; the
  evaluation environment alone contains held-out test data and the
  test-time evaluation entry point, and the two environments share no
  filesystem state at any point during a hill-climbing run.

#### Comparison with Paper-Reported Numbers.

Most reproductions land within standard-deviation tolerance of the
paper’s number. Six benchmarks diverge by more than run-to-run noise; in
every case the standardized setting differs from the paper’s setting
rather than the source paper itself being incorrect, and we document
each cause below so that comparisons in the rest of the paper can be
read in the right context.

- •
  CarbonGlobe (0.571 paper, 0.604 ours; RMSE). The original protocol
  selects checkpoints on the test set. Our reproduction carves a 90/10
  train/val split inside the training set and selects checkpoints on
  validation, which both reduces training data by 10% and removes a
  source of test-set leakage. The slightly worse RMSE is the cost of an
  honest split that keeps the test set sequestered.
- •
  DART-Eval (0.515 paper, 0.544 ours; Spearman). The published baseline
  trains the 494M-parameter Nucleotide Transformer with LoRA. Because a
  single H200 has 141 GB of memory, we instead full-fine-tune the same
  model. Training time is essentially unchanged, so the upgrade incurs
  no meaningful compute overhead while still lifting the reproduction
  above the LoRA-only number originally reported by the source paper.
- •
  GEO-Bench-2 (43.10 paper, 45.90 ours; mIoU). The official repository
  ships only data, not training code. We author a training pipeline from
  the paper text using a TerraMind-V1-Large backbone with seven
  timesteps averaged at 224$`\times`$224 resolution. The combination is
  consistent with the paper’s description, but is more carefully tuned
  than what is otherwise possible from the data-only release.
- •
  GTPBD (57.16 paper, 69.87 ours; mIoU). The released test set is
  incomplete: five countries (South Africa, Brazil, Morocco, Nigeria,
  Tunisia) lack imagery because of Google Earth licensing, leaving the
  test set 89.8% complete. The missing regions correspond to the most
  challenging terrain in the benchmark, which inflates our mIoU by
  roughly ten points relative to the full-coverage number that the paper
  reports.
- •
  LITHOS (43.20 paper, 42.20 ours; macro F1). The paper averages a
  2-fold cross-validation. We run a single fold and additionally hold
  out 17% of that fold for validation-based checkpoint selection, which
  both halves the number of folds and reduces per-fold training data,
  together explaining why our reproduction sits below the paper’s
  two-fold average.
- •
  MassSpecGym (14.64 paper, 16.14 ours; Hit@1). The paper selects
  checkpoints by validation loss, which converges very early on this
  task. We use a held-out validation slice (19K samples) and select
  checkpoints by validation Hit@1, which the agent can then optimize
  directly during hill-climbing. The headline number is therefore
  higher, but the resulting baseline stays self-consistent with the
  validation Hit@1 metric used in all subsequent agent comparisons.

### B.6 Auditor Reliability

Because the default auditor, Claude Opus 4.6, belongs to the same model
family as two of the hill-climbing agents, we validate the auditing
stage in two ways. First, to rule out same-family bias, we re-audit half
of each agent’s runs (40 trajectories in total), each with three
auditors from different model families: Claude Opus 4.6, Codex
(GPT-5.4), and Gemini 3.1 Pro. Across all 120 (trajectory, auditor)
pairs, the verdicts are fully consistent, and no run is flagged. Second,
we validate the auditors against human judgment on both known-positive
and clean trajectories.

- •
  Known-positive trajectories. We re-run the auditing stage on the five
  pilot-study trajectories with known evaluation gaming
  (§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
  using auditors from all three model families. Every auditor flags the
  gaming in every case, and our manual review of all 15 resulting audit
  reports confirms that their judgments are accurate on each criterion
  we specify.
- •
  Clean trajectories. We manually inspect 15 audit reports sampled from
  the main experiments, and human judgment agrees with the auditor
  verdict in every case.

Overall, we find no evidence of auditor-dependent bias, and frontier
models from all three families reliably detect the salient
evaluation-gaming behaviors in our setting.

### B.7 Agentic Benchmark Checklist

The Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC) \[[68](#bib.bib1)\] organizes best
practices for rigorous agentic evaluation into three parts: task
validity, outcome validity, and benchmark reporting. Although Agent4Sci
is a per-benchmark discovery probe rather than a leaderboard
(Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Non-Leaderboard Benchmark Scope ‣ Appendix A Limitations ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
these criteria still apply, since a probe whose scores can be inflated
would surface spurious progress. For task validity, each task runs in a
container image built from a human-verified reproduction of the
published baseline
(Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Standardization Details ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
and the hill-climbing agent is isolated from the held-out test set. Our
pilot study on raw repositories
(§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks ‣ 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?"))
played the role of pilot runs, exposing the shortcuts the protocol now
closes.

For outcome validity, every score is the benchmark’s own metric,
computed on the held-out test set by a separate evaluation agent with
the standardized evaluator rather than with code the hill-climbing agent
wrote, and an auditing agent validated against human judgment
(Appendix [B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Auditor Reliability ‣ Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?"))
inspects each trajectory for evaluation gaming. For benchmark reporting,
we release the code, data, and evaluation harness, document the
construction process and its limitations
(Appendices [A](#A1 "Appendix A Limitations ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
and [B](#A2 "Appendix B Benchmark Construction ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
report run-to-run variance
(Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Run-to-Run Variance ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
and compare against both the reproduced published baselines and an
HPO-only baseline
(Appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Comparison with Hyperparameter Optimization ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
The main gap is a human-researcher baseline
(Appendix [A.6](#A1.SS6 "A.6 Absence of a Human-Researcher Baseline ‣ Appendix A Limitations ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).

#### Training Compute.

Skill-enabled runs frequently spend more training compute than the
reproduction baseline
(Figure [11(a)](#A3.F11.sf1 "In Figure 11 ‣ C.2 Performance and Runtime Trade-offs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")),
primarily through deeper or wider networks, longer training schedules,
larger pretrained backbones, or model ensembling (we count an
$`N`$-model ensemble as $`N\times`$ training cost). On Mars-Bench and
GEO-Bench-2, the additional GPU-hours bring meaningful improvement, with
$`\Delta\%`$ rising substantially relative to the no-skill run. The
relationship is not monotonic, however. On CarbonGlobe and TIDMAD, the
skill-enabled agent reaches higher $`\Delta\%`$ at lower training cost.
CarbonGlobe is the most striking case: the no-skill Opus run scales out
to a 14-model DeepED ensemble that consumes roughly 49 GPU-hours, while
the skill-enabled run replaces this with an 8-model non-autoregressive
transformer ensemble that finishes in under 6 GPU-hours, almost matching
the reproduction’s compute budget while substantially improving the
metric.

#### Inference Compute.

Most benchmarks keep the original architecture and land at the
1.0$`\times`$ reference line in
Figure [11(b)](#A3.F11.sf2 "In Figure 11 ‣ C.2 Performance and Runtime Trade-offs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?").
The exceptions sit on either tail of the x-axis. On segmentation and
detection benchmarks (Mars-Bench, GEO-Bench-2, TreeFinder), larger
backbones, model ensembling, and test-time augmentation push inference
time more than $`10\times`$ over the reproduction baseline while only
modestly improving $`\Delta\%`$, so the marginal cost of each additional
point of accuracy at inference time is high. CarbonGlobe sits at the
opposite extreme: the agent’s switch to a non-autoregressive
architecture collapses inference into a single forward pass and yields a
roughly $`500\times`$ speed-up over the original autoregressive
evaluator while still improving accuracy. The two halves of
Figure [11](#A3.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ C.2 Performance and Runtime Trade-offs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")
therefore tell a consistent story: skill-driven gains are not always
paid for in extra compute, and the most interesting wins come from
architectural reformulations that move along both axes at once.

### D.3 Three-Day Long-Running Experiments

To probe how skill-enabled agents behave under a much longer budget, we
run two long-running experiments at three days each, with zero human
intervention beyond launching the run and collecting artifacts. We pick
two benchmarks: PFDelta 2000, a harder variant of PFDelta with a
2000-bus grid and an inference-aware metric, and CarbonGlobe, where the
skill-enabled Opus 4.6 had previously discovered a non-autoregressive
reformulation that improved both accuracy and runtime
(§[C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Performance and Runtime Trade-offs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Results and Analysis ‣ Automating ML for Science: Can Frontier Agents Climb Scientific Hills in the Wild?")).
Each project is run with both Claude Opus 4.6 + Skills and Codex
(GPT-5.4) + Skills.

The original PFDelta task uses small grids on which a Newton-Raphson
solver returns the exact solution, and its evaluation script does not
measure inference time, so a hill-climbing agent that simply calls the
solver gets a near-perfect score for free. We therefore raise the grid
size to GOC-2000 (2000 buses), where Newton-Raphson is no longer
affordable at scale, and redefine the metric to _PBL at GNN latency_,
which penalizes any submission whose inference time exceeds the GNN
reference budget so the agent cannot hide behind a slow exact solver.
This keeps the task aligned with what PFDelta targets: reaching an
accurate power-flow solution quickly with an ML model. The redefined
metric is used only in this three-day study; all PFDelta results in the
main text use the original metric.

Across these four three-day runs, the headline result is that
hill-climbing breakthroughs do not scale with time alone. Claude on
PFDelta 2000 and both agents on CarbonGlobe spend the bulk of the budget
on incremental tweaks that deliver diminishing or no returns, and on
CarbonGlobe Codex’s extra 60 hours of compute actively erode held-out
performance. The one outsized win, Codex’s 99.5% PBL reduction on
PFDelta 2000, comes from a single domain-specific insight (treat the GNN
as a warm start for a condition-aware solver) that the agent stumbles
onto well into the run rather than approaches by gradient. The pattern
is consistent with our main-paper findings in

when skill-driven gains are real, they are gated by whether the agent
surfaces a genuine scientific insight, not by how long it is allowed to
keep climbing.

## Appendix E Extended Related Work

Below, we highlight additional topics relevant to the motivation of our
benchmark and the agent behaviors we observe during our study.

#### Long-Horizon Agent Evaluation.

Agent capabilities track with completion time and success rate on
challenging software tasks \[[43](#bib.bib67)\]. Long-horizon task
success is variable and poorly described by single-shot
performance \[[65](#bib.bib64)\], even though recent measurements report
exponential growth in success rates on long-horizon
tasks \[[25](#bib.bib25), [66](#bib.bib65), [29](#bib.bib66)\].
Human-calibrated evaluations also show sharply diminishing returns
beyond a few human-hours, even under multi-pass
scoring \[[47](#bib.bib31), [61](#bib.bib9)\].

#### Reward Hacking, Specification Gaming, and Other Misalignment.

Reward hacking, specification gaming, and similar misaligned behaviors
are by now well-documented across both formal analyses and empirical
studies \[[55](#bib.bib26), [41](#bib.bib27), [2](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib62), [18](#bib.bib68)\]. Beyond opportunistic exploitation
of scoring bugs or answer keys, frontier models are now known to be
capable of in-context scheming and other subversive behaviors that
persist through training interventions \[[34](#bib.bib24),
[52](#bib.bib60), [5](#bib.bib59), [33](#bib.bib61), [31](#bib.bib63)\].
Metagaming, defined as reasoning about scoring, feedback, or oversight
in the agent environment, is an emergent behavior that appears to
intensify during capabilities-focused RL \[[53](#bib.bib34)\].

## Appendix F Broader Impacts

Agent4Sci is intended as a measurement tool for automated scientific
hill-climbing rather than a deployed research agent or a new high-risk
model. All benchmarks in the suite are drawn from publicly released,
peer-reviewed scientific benchmarks, and our selection process favors
open code, open data, clear licensing, and tasks whose outputs are
standard scientific ML predictions rather than directly actionable
dual-use artifacts. The main positive impact is a more transparent way
to track when frontier agents begin to produce measurable progress on
real scientific workflows; the main risk is that stronger automated
hill-climbing methods could be transferred to less benign domains. We
mitigate this risk at the testbed level by focusing on public benchmark
tasks, separating hill-climbing from held-out evaluation, and auditing
trajectories for evaluation gaming and academic misconduct.
