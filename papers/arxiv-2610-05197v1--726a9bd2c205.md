# From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists

###### Abstract

Data-driven mechanistic hypotheses are essential to scientific discovery
because they explain how underlying processes produce observed
phenomena. AI agents and AI scientists increasingly support scientific
data analysis. However, their ability to turn empirical findings into
mechanistic hypotheses remains insufficiently examined. To address this
gap, we introduce MechHypoBench, the first benchmark for evaluating
whether AI agents and AI scientists can generate such hypotheses from
empirical data. It combines paper-derived mechanisms from 14 scientific
fields with real-world datasets containing 17.98 million records. The
construction retains the observational complexity of empirical data
while providing a specified underlying mechanism. Agents analyze the
observations and propose open-form hypotheses. We develop an evaluation
framework that assesses open-form mechanistic hypotheses through their
consequences under withheld conditions. Experiments with general agents
and AI scientists reveal a substantial gap between generated hypotheses
and the underlying mechanisms.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: We randomly select papers published in 2021-2025 from
high-impact journals. Overall, 70.2% of the papers treated mechanisms as
explanatory outputs, motivating our research on mechanism-driven
hypothesis generation.

Recent advances in AI agents and AI scientists have largely accelerated
scientific discoveries across domains ([Wang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib18);
[Boiko et al., 2023](#bib.bib19); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib11);
[Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)), including physics, biology,
chemistry, etc. One of the central tasks is to use observed data to
investigate the underlying mechanism of how scientific phenomena arise.
For example, when a catalytic reaction slows over time, researchers may
hypothesize that molecules progressively block the catalyst’s active
sites, leaving fewer sites available for reaction. A mechanistic
hypothesis is an open-form account of how processes and their
interactions produce an observed phenomenon, including the conditions
under which they operate. In our review of papers from natural-science
journals, 70.2% treated a mechanism as an explanatory output
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")).
Assessing AI agents’ ability to generate mechanistic hypotheses from
data is therefore important for determining whether they can contribute
to scientific discovery.

![Refer to caption](2610.05197v1/benchmark_overview_v3.png)

Figure 2: Overview of our constructed benchmark.

Existing benchmarks have expanded the evaluation of LLMs in scientific
discovery beyond question answering ([Jin et al., 2019](#bib.bib16);
[Wang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib15)). Several benchmarks study
literature-grounded hypothesis and idea generation using scientific
papers and background knowledge ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Liu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Lew et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)). Others evaluate
hypothesis discovery from specified entities or scientific data,
including the recovery of entity relations, equations, data
relationships, explanatory patterns, and admissible hypothesis
sets ([Xiong et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Majumder et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1); [Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). A separate group
evaluates scientific research and discovery workflows, ranging from
executable data-analysis to broader task- and project-level
evaluations ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Bragg et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5); [Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)).

However, existing benchmarks pose unique challenges for evaluating
data-driven mechanistic hypotheses. (1) Finding a pattern does not
explain how a phenomenon arises. Most data-driven benchmarks ask models
to recover relationships or equations ([Majumder et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1); [Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)). Such tasks show
whether a model can detect what happens in the data, but not whether it
can explain how underlying processes produce the phenomenon under
specific conditions. The Mechanistic column of
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")
shows that direct evaluation of this ability remains limited, even among
benchmarks that accept explanatory hypotheses. (2) Known mechanisms
rarely come with realistic observations. Real datasets preserve the
complexity of scientific observations, but they rarely identify a unique
and fully specified mechanism ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). Treating
a source-paper explanation as ground truth may privilege one
interpretation and reward familiarity with the paper rather than
reasoning from the data. Synthetic data provide exact references but
simplify the observations ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). Accordingly,
the Exp. Data and Source columns of
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")
show that no prior benchmark fully combines realistic empirical data
with a controllable underlying mechanism. (3) Open-form hypotheses
cannot be scored by wording alone. Equivalent mechanisms may use
different wording, while a hypothesis may have clear implications only
under some conditions. Text matching or a single classification decision
cannot capture these differences. Evaluation should instead test the
outcomes implied under unseen conditions to assess how well a hypothesis
captures the behavior of the underlying mechanism. The pipeline should
also support agents that iteratively inspect data and execute analyses.
The Open-form and Agent columns of
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")
show that existing benchmarks rarely provide both.

In response, we introduce MechHypoBench, a benchmark for data-driven
mechanistic hypothesis generation with AI agents
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")).
We instantiate mechanisms derived from papers spanning 14 scientific
fields ([Priem et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)) on independently collected
real-world scientific datasets. The datasets vary in structure and
collectively contain 17.98 million observation records. This
construction combines a scientifically grounded generating process with
empirical observational complexity by aligning mechanisms and datasets
through their experimental roles. Agents analyze the resulting
observations, then propose open-form mechanistic hypotheses to explain
how the observed phenomena arise. We assess these hypotheses through
their implied consequences under withheld changes in conditions, using
ground truth outcomes determined by the same generating mechanism. Our
experiments reveal substantial room for improvement in agents’ ability
to formulate mechanistic hypotheses from scientific data. We summarize
our contributions as follows:

- •
  We propose MechHypoBench, the first benchmark for data-driven
  mechanistic hypothesis generation. It combines paper-derived
  mechanisms from 14 scientific fields with independently collected
  empirical datasets.
- •
  We develop an evaluation framework for open-form mechanistic
  hypotheses. It assesses how well a hypothesis captures the behavior of
  the underlying mechanism.
- •
  Through experiments with AI scientists, we identify limitations in
  data-driven mechanistic hypothesis generation. A substantial gap
  remains between the generated hypotheses and the underlying
  mechanisms.

## 2 Related Work

We position MechHypoBench among existing benchmarks for scientific
reasoning and discovery in
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists").
These benchmarks extend beyond question answering ([Jin et al.,
2019](#bib.bib16); [Wang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib15)). Literature- and
knowledge-driven benchmarks evaluate idea or hypothesis generation from
published research and background knowledge ([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Lew et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Xiong et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). But discovery also
requires direct analysis of observational data to establish empirical
support for these proposals. Data-driven benchmarks can be broadly
divided into two directions. One line focuses on recovering structured
scientific targets, ranging from observable relationships and
equations ([Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)), to enumerable hypothesis
spaces ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), and structured mechanistic
models in MechBench ([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). Another line
explores open-form hypothesis generation from scientific datasets or
analysis contexts ([Majumder et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)).

However, existing empirical settings generally lack explicit grounding
in fully specified underlying mechanisms. Controlled synthetic settings,
in turn, simplify the complexity of real scientific observations. The
growing use of AI agents also motivates evaluating how they conduct
analyses and interact with tools in discovery. Research-workflow-based
benchmarks focus on evaluating executable analyses, tool usage, and
end-to-end scientific tasks ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Bragg et
al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). More recent
efforts, such as EurekaBench ([Geng et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), further
explore autonomous scientific research through iterative experimentation
and mechanism discovery. These works either emphasize workflow execution
or rely on interactive environments with predefined discovery processes,
leaving open the question of whether AI agents can directly formulate
open-form mechanistic explanations from complex empirical observations.
MechHypoBench addresses this gap by evaluating open-form mechanistic
hypothesis generation from empirical scientific datasets while
preserving their observational complexity.

## 3 MechHypoBench

### 3.1 Concept Formalization

Mechanisms, conditions, and observations. Scientific inquiry draws on
observed data collected under particular conditions to investigate how
phenomena arise ([Woodward, 2011](#bib.bib26)). An underlying mechanism
$`\mathcal{M}`$ generates the phenomenon, but the available observations
may reveal only part of how it operates. Its observable response may
depend not only on the factor being examined, but also on the
surrounding context, prior exposure, and the stage of the
process ([Machamer et al., 2000](#bib.bib23)). To represent these
dependencies, we distinguish four experimental semantic roles:

- •
  Driver ($`\mathrm{d}`$): the factor whose effect is examined.
- •
  Context ($`\mathrm{x}`$): a contextual factor that may modify the
  driver’s effect.
- •
  History ($`\mathrm{h}`$): prior exposure or state.
- •
  Phase ($`\mathrm{p}`$): the stage of the process.

A condition
$`c=(c_{\mathrm{d}},c_{\mathrm{x}},c_{\mathrm{h}},c_{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathcal{C}`$
specifies the joint values or states associated with these roles, where
$`\mathcal{C}`$ denotes the relevant condition space. Let
$`R_{\mathcal{M}}(c)`$ denote the response, the observable outcome
generated by mechanism $`\mathcal{M}`$ under condition $`c`$. For
instance, in the chemistry example of
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists"),
$`c_{\mathrm{d}}`$ specifies the _reactant concentration_,
$`c_{\mathrm{x}}`$ the _catalyst surface state_, $`c_{\mathrm{h}}`$ the
_catalyst pretreatment_, and $`c_{\mathrm{p}}`$ the _reaction stage_.
Together, these specify a condition $`c`$, with _product selectivity_ as
the response $`R_{\mathcal{M}}(c)`$. Changing the _reactant
concentration_ $`c_{\mathrm{d}}`$ while keeping the other three
components fixed yields a different condition. Let
$`\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}`$ denote the conditions
under which observations are available. These observations can be
represented as condition-response pairs:
$`\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}})=\{(c,R_{\mathcal{M}}(c))\mid c\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}}\}.`$
In the chemistry example, $`\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}}`$ contains the
combinations of _concentration, catalyst state, pretreatment_, and
_reaction stage_ under which measurements were collected;
$`\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}})`$ pairs those
combinations with their corresponding _product selectivity_.

Mechanistic hypothesis. Since the available observations do not fully
reveal how the underlying mechanism operates, researchers formulate
mechanistic hypotheses to explain how the observed phenomenon
arises ([Darden, 2002](#bib.bib29); [Craver and Darden,
2013](#bib.bib24)). A mechanistic hypothesis $`h`$ is an open-form
account of how relevant processes and their interactions produce the
observations $`\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}})`$,
including the conditions under which they operate. The available
observations may support multiple mechanistic hypotheses to differing
degrees.

### 3.2 Scientific Source Collection

We collect scientific papers to derive mechanisms and independently
gather real-world empirical datasets on which these mechanisms are
instantiated.

Paper collection. We collected candidate papers through Europe PMC and
OpenAlex([Priem et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)) across a range of scientific
journals, including PLOS journals and eLife. (1) Candidate filtering. We
retained empirical studies that proposed, tested, or revised mechanisms,
excluding reviews, protocols, retracted papers, and studies focused
solely on prediction or optimization. (2) Mechanistic claim review.
Human review assessed whether the reported claims described how a
phenomenon arises and provided sufficient information to identify the
relevant experimental semantic roles and conditions. (3) Claim
extraction and verification. We used LLMs to assist in extracting
mechanistic claims and supporting passages, then manually checked the
extracted content against the source papers, preserving the claims and
their conditions of applicability. The resulting collection contains 306
papers spanning 14 scientific fields.

Dataset collection. We independently collected candidate datasets from
scientific repositories, including PANGAEA ([Felden et al.,
2023](#bib.bib21)), ScienceDB ([Long et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)),
Zenodo ([Zenodo, 2026](#bib.bib39)), Dryad ([Dryad, 2026](#bib.bib40)),
and the Open Science Framework ([Foster and Deardorff,
2017](#bib.bib28)). (1) Candidate filtering. We selected datasets with
traceable provenance, accessible data files, suitable reuse licenses,
and documentation describing the recorded variables. (2) Scientific and
structural review. Human review assessed whether each dataset contained
sufficient variation, ordered observations, auxiliary measurements, and
grouping structure to support the experimental semantic roles and the
intended task construction. (3) Dataset preparation and role annotation.
Dataset variables were manually annotated with their corresponding
experimental semantic roles. Dataset-specific preprocessing adapters
accommodated differences in file formats and table layouts. The
resulting collection contains 200 datasets with manual role annotations
and diverse structures, including cross-sectional, longitudinal,
time-series, grouped, and spatiotemporal formats, among others.

### 3.3 Scientific Skills Collection

To examine whether scientific skills affects mechanistic hypothesis
generation, we collected over 500,000 science-related skills from GitHub
and filtered them for relevance to scientific data analysis, discovery,
and hypothesis generation. (1) Initial filtering. An LLM used for
screening skill descriptions to shortlist over 20,000 candidates.
Filtering excluded academic writing and formatting tools,
literature-retrieval and web-dependent tools, and generic agent
frameworks or LLM API wrappers. (2) Relevance review. Further LLM-based
review examined both descriptions and skill contents to identify
candidates relevant to scientific data analysis, discovery, or
hypothesis generation. (3) Pool consolidation. After removing duplicates
and entries with insufficient content, the final pool contained 4,043
skills.

### 3.4 Task Construction

Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.4 Task Construction ‣ 3 MechHypoBench ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")
illustrates how we simulate the scientific inference setting using a
specified paper-derived mechanism $`\mathcal{M}^{*}`$, which serves as
the underlying mechanism within each task. It is known to the benchmark
designer but hidden from the agent. We use LLM-assisted conversion to
organize reviewed paper claims into a directed graph of processes,
interactions, and responses associated with the experimental semantic
roles. Numerical functions and parameters specify how these components
operate. The resulting mechanism is checked against the reviewed claims
and fixed before dataset pairing, providing a controlled realization of
the reported processes rather than a numerical reconstruction of the
original experiment. From the same mechanism graph, we derive two
open-form textual mechanism references (MRs): the qualitative mechanism
reference (QMR) describes its dependencies, interactions, and
conditions, while the parameterized mechanism reference (PMR)
additionally provides the corresponding equations and numerical
parameters.

Figure 3: Task construction pipeline. Paper-derived claims are compiled
into a fixed mechanism $`\mathcal{M}^{*}`$ and aligned with independent
scientific datasets through experimental roles. The mechanism generates
responses on the aligned conditions and yields two open-form references.
Agents receive public observation and are evaluated on hidden matched
comparisons.

We align an independently collected dataset with $`\mathcal{M}^{*}`$
through its manually annotated experimental semantic roles. Dataset
variables specify conditions $`c`$, under which the mechanism generates
responses $`R_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(c)`$. This preserves empirical sampling
patterns, grouping, and auxiliary measurements, with added noise
representing measurement variability. Dataset labels and task
descriptions retain the experimental roles and their interpretation
while replacing source-specific terminology to avoid misleading domain
associations and cues to memorized scientific accounts. The generated
observations are divided into public observations
$`\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{obs}})`$, available
to the agent, and hidden observations, withheld for assessment. Each
task includes a set $`\mathcal{Q}`$ of matched comparisons
$`q=(c^{(1)},c^{(0)},R)`$, each comparing response $`R`$ under two
conditions that differ in exactly one component. These comparisons have
stable ground-truth direction of response changes determined by
$`\mathcal{M}^{*}`$ and sufficient observational support. Observations
under both compared conditions belong to the hidden portion, although
individual component values may still occur in the public observations.
Task difficulty is determined by a marginal baseline fitted to the
public observations: easy tasks yield one to three errors across ten
directional comparisons, whereas hard tasks yield six to ten,
distinguishing how poorly marginal relationships capture the conditional
response changes.

### 3.5 Human Evaluation

To assess the quality of the constructed tasks, we conducted a human
evaluation across both difficulty levels. Ten reviewers with scientific
backgrounds assessed each task on four dimensions using a 0-5 scale: (1)
Scientific Coherence (SC) assesses whether the mechanism, experimental
roles, and comparisons form a scientifically interpretable scenario; (2)
Mechanism-task Consistency (MC) measures agreement among the mechanism,
role mapping, data-generation process, and comparisons; (3) Probe
Clarity (PC) assesses whether the condition change, response variable,
and requested judgment are unambiguous; and (4) Reference-Answer
Validity (RV) assesses how clearly the ground-truth direction of
response changes are supported by the specified mechanism.

### 3.6 Evaluation Framework

Evaluation Framework. Mechanistic hypotheses support reasoning about
interventions and outcomes under conditions not yet observed ([Woodward
and Woodward, 2005](#bib.bib25); [Craver, 2007](#bib.bib27)). Motivated
by this perspective, we assess hypotheses through the response changes
they imply for the hidden matched comparisons. For each
$`q=(c^{(1)},c^{(0)},R)`$, let $`\Delta_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(q)`$ denote
the ground-truth direction of change in response $`R`$ from $`c^{(0)}`$
to $`c^{(1)}`$ determined by the paper-derived mechanism, and let
$`\Delta_{h}(q)`$ denote the corresponding direction implied by
hypothesis $`h`$. Agreement between these directions provides a
criterion for assessing the hypothesis. Open-form mechanistic hypotheses
do not necessarily specify an executable response function, so their
implications must be interpreted before scoring. Asking the generating
agent to answer the comparisons could draw on observations or
intermediate analyses not expressed in its hypothesis. We therefore use
a separate, fixed LLM-based hypotheses interpreter that receives only
$`h`$ and the comparison descriptions, without access to the public
observations or ground-truth directions. For each comparison $`q`$, it
returns $`p_{h}(t\mid q)`$ over
$`\mathcal{T}=\{\mathrm{decrease},\mathrm{no\_change},\mathrm{increase}\}`$,
representing its interpretation of $`\Delta_{h}(q)`$. We request
probabilities rather than a single direction because a hypothesis may
leave some consequences ambiguous or incompletely specified. A
probability distribution allows the interpreter to express this
uncertainty instead of forcing an equally definite answer for every
hypothesis. A deterministic evaluator then computes scores against
$`\Delta_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(q)`$. The same interpreter processes both
MRs, providing a comparison with specified underlying mechanism content
in each task. Empty and shuffled controls provide baselines for absent
and mismatched content, respectively.

Metrics. We assess each mechanistic hypothesis using two complementary
metrics:

| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\;}l@{}}\operatorname{MCA}(h)&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\arg\max_{t\in\mathcal{T}}p_{h}(t\mid q)=\Delta_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(q)\right],\\
\operatorname{MCNLL}(h)&\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\log p_{h}\!\left(\Delta_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(q)\mid q\right).\end{array}
``` |  |

Mechanism Consequence Agreement (MCA) evaluates how consistently the
hypothesis-implied direction of response change agree with those
determined by the generating mechanism. Mechanism Consequence Negative
Log-Likelihood (MCNLL) further evaluates the probability assigned to the
ground-truth direction, penalizing confident errors more strongly.
Together, higher MCA and lower MCNLL provide evidence that a mechanistic
hypothesis captures the behavior of the underlying mechanism under the
tested conditions.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Tasks. Our evaluation includes 300 tasks, drawing on 244 papers and 150
datasets from source collections of 306 papers and 200 datasets
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Scientific Source Collection ‣ 3 MechHypoBench ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists")).
It comprises 120 easy and 180 hard tasks.

Evaluated AI scientists and Agents. (1) General Agents. General AI
agents are increasingly being used to assist and accelerate scientific
discovery. We evaluate Codex ([OpenAI, 2026a](#bib.bib31)) with GPT-5.6
Sol ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib33)) and Claude Code ([Anthropic,
2026a](#bib.bib32)) with Claude Sonnet 5 ([Anthropic,
2026b](#bib.bib35)). To compare the two harnesses with shared model
backends, we also pair each with DeepSeek-V4-Flash ([DeepSeek-AI,
2026](#bib.bib37)) and GLM-5.2 ([GLM-5-Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)).
Each of the six harness-model configurations is evaluated with and
without scientific skills. (2) AI Scientists. To test whether workflows
designed for scientific discovery help with mechanistic hypothesis
generation from observational data, we evaluate the ideation component
of AI-Scientist-v2 ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) and the
Bayesian-surprise-driven search procedure of AutoDiscovery ([Agarwal et
al., 2026](#bib.bib30)).

Implementation details. For skill-enabled runs,
SkillRouter-Reranker-0.6B ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib38)) selects 32
skills from the collected pool
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Scientific Skills Collection ‣ 3 MechHypoBench ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists"))
using the task description and public dataset information, including its
overview, structure, and format. General-agent runs use a 30-minute
terminal budget and high reasoning settings. Across the twelve
general-agent configurations, 214-289 of the 300 tasks yield valid
terminal outputs. Invalid or missing outputs are scored using the result
from the empty-control condition. Both AI-scientist systems use GPT-5.6
Sol. AutoDiscovery is evaluated on a fixed, stratified subset of 40
tasks, comprising 20 easy and 20 hard, since its search used 4,464
accepted LLM calls, averaging 111.6 per task. GPT-5.6 Terra ([OpenAI,
2026c](#bib.bib34)), with high reasoning and temperature zero, serves as
the fixed hypothesis interpreter. Details of interpretation and scoring
are given in
Section [3.6](#S3.SS6 "3.6 Evaluation Framework ‣ 3 MechHypoBench ‣ From Scientific Observations to Mechanisms: Benchmarking Hypothesis Generation by AI Scientists").

### 4.4 Evaluating Mechanistic Hypothesis Generation in AI Scientists

AI-scientist workflows do not consistently improve mechanistic
hypothesis generation. AI-Scientist-v2 achieves 59.2% and 43.8% MCA on
easy and hard tasks, respectively, below Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol.

### 4.5 Evaluating Effects of Evidence Access and Retention

We examine two settings that change how agents access and retain
evidence from the public observations. In the evidence summaries
setting, agents receive precomputed summaries of how responses vary
across conditions in the public data. These summaries make relevant
empirical relationships explicit without providing new observations. In
the structured records setting, agents receive no precomputed evidence.
Instead, they are instructed to record their own findings, the
conditions under which each finding holds, its supporting evidence, and
potential falsifiers, and to revisit these records before producing
their final hypotheses. We evaluate both settings on fixed 40 tasks
using Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Code with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the
highest-MCA model configurations within their respective harnesses in
the main experiments.

These results suggest that making empirical relationships explicit can

imply increases or decreases. These results suggest that the difficulty
of mechanistic hypothesis generation for AI agents lies partly in
discovering and selecting relevant evidence from the available
observations. Structured records provide a way to retain findings during
extended analysis, but cannot compensate for evidence that agents have
overlooked. Their limited gains further suggest that preserving findings
and integrating them into a mechanistic hypothesis are distinct demands;

## 5 Conclusion

This paper introduces MechHypoBench, a benchmark for data-driven
mechanistic hypothesis generation that combines paper-derived mechanisms
across 14 fields with independently collected empirical datasets. We
then develop an evaluation framework that assesses open-form mechanistic
hypotheses through their consequences under withheld conditions, with
ground truth outcomes determined by the same paper-derived mechanism
that generates the observations. Through systematic experiments with AI
agents, we identify limitations in data-driven mechanistic hypothesis
generation and examine how evidence availability and the retention of
findings affect the hypotheses they produce.
````
