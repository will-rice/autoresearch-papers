# Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks

Zihan Dong Affiliation: Lenovo Group E-mail <puma122707@gmail.com>   
Qishi Zhan Affiliation: Marquette University    Minxuan Hu
Affiliation: Cornell University

###### Abstract

Data-science and ML-engineering agents are evaluated on benchmarks such
as MLE-bench, ALE-Bench, SWE-fficiency and AlgoTune, whose tasks yield a
quantity (a Kaggle metric, contest score or speedup). When one stops
separating the best models, the usual remedy is a harder version.
Re-scoring released runs of eight benchmarks, including SWE-bench
Verified and Terminal-Bench for breadth, we find much of this saturation
lies in the measurement, not the tasks. Most agent benchmarks reduce a
run to one pass/fail bit, which fails in two ways. It is too coarse:
neighbouring top-ten agents on SWE-bench Verified differ by less than
its tests’ errors, and correcting them reorders neighbours no more than
deleting a random sixth of each agent’s passes. It is capped at the
reference: ALE-Bench’s top ten, separated by contest score, tie when
graded against the median human, although nearly half the runs beat that
human. On fixed tasks, a higher bar only moves the problem; graded
credit fixes it. We derive a clipped log-ratio score with dated anchors
and cost as a separate axis. In a Terminal-Bench re-run, pass/fail-tied
models differ over tenfold on the quantity the tests discard.

## 1 Introduction

Data-science and ML-engineering agents are benchmarked on Kaggle
competitions (MLE-bench \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]), optimization contests
(ALE-Bench \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]) and code speedups
(SWE-fficiency \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], AlgoTune \[[30](#bib.bib30)\]).
Consider ALE-Bench, 40 contests scored by contest performance: its top
ten separate into three tiers. Scored pass/fail against the median
human, as most agent benchmarks score runs, five models pass 39 of the
40 problems and the top ten become one tier, although nearly half the
runs beat that human. On the same tasks, a higher bar does not fix this:
one that separates the newest models scores 89% of the earlier ones
zero. A single pass/fail bar per task sees one band of ability; graded
credit widens it.

The symptom is general: the last flagship model card to report SWE-bench
Verified appeared in April 2026
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")b),
when its top ten solved 76–80% of the tasks: it ran out of _ranking_,
not tasks. Across 57 flagship cards from seven labs, only a third of a
card’s agentic results are comparable with the same lab’s previous
flagship (same benchmark, version, subset and harness;
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")a).

Figure 1: Benchmark turnover in 57 flagship model cards (2024–2026). (a)
Agentic benchmark families reported per half-year, and the share of a
card’s agentic results comparable with the lab’s previous flagship. (b)
Which SWE-style benchmark each card reports.

Turnover has many causes, contamination among them; we use it as
motivation only. The usual reading of saturation is that the tasks
became too easy, and the usual remedy is a harder version. We find that
much of it lies in the _measurement_. Almost every agent benchmark
grades a run against an _answer key_ written for each task (a reference
solution, an expected end state, or tests derived from the author’s
solution), which returns one bit. The key is also the costliest and
least verifiable part of a benchmark: a checker that reliably separates
good answers from bad ones is case-specific, slow to write and still
often wrong \[[4](#bib.bib4), [40](#bib.bib40), [10](#bib.bib10)\], and
OSWorld reports twice as much effort per task on the evaluator as on the
task \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. Where no key can be written, the fallback is
an LLM judge, which a single token can fool \[[39](#bib.bib39)\].

The answer key thus fails in two separate ways. Failure A, the bit is
too coarse
(§[3](#S3 "3 Failure A: The Bit Is Too Coarse to Rank the Frontier ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")):
on three leaderboards, neighbouring agents in the top ten differ by less
than a few hundred bits can detect, and on SWE-bench Verified by less
than the tests’ errors. Failure B, the bit is capped
(§[4](#S4 "4 Failure B: The Bit Is Capped at the Reference ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")–[6](#S6 "6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")):
a run that beats the reference, or passes at a fifth of the cost, scores
the same as one that just passes.

Both failures come from one design choice: the key encodes an _answer_
rather than a _preference_. An answer must be written for every task and
stops informing once agents reach it. A preference (“faster is better”)
can be scored once per domain, with room above any reference.
Reinforcement learning made this switch when agents passed
humans \[[28](#bib.bib28)\], as did optimization
benchmarking \[[5](#bib.bib5), [14](#bib.bib14)\].

### Contributions.

(1) On released runs of eight benchmarks and a Terminal-Bench re-run, we
measure both failures, locate the lost information above the reference,
and report where it does not help
(§[3](#S3 "3 Failure A: The Bit Is Too Coarse to Rank the Frontier ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")–[5](#S5 "5 Failure B on Terminal-Bench, and the Ignored Cost ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
(2) We show why, on a fixed task set, a higher pass/fail bar cannot fix
a saturated key while graded credit can, and derive the form of the
graded score
(§[6](#S6 "6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
(3) We derive an evaluation methodology with a hardened, auditable gate,
an anchored objective, cost as a separate reported axis, and frozen
anchors that keep versions reproducible and comparable, instantiated in
a small re-run; LLM judges help write evaluators but do not score
(§[7](#S7 "7 A Benchmark Design That Scores Acceptance, Quality and Cost Separately ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
We release code and derived statistics.

## 2 Related Work

### Saturation.

Saturation of 60 LM benchmarks has been tested against answer format,
access, size and age \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], not against how the score is
built. Two 2026 analyses argue that hard answer keys become unaffordable
or impossible once models exceed their authors \[[12](#bib.bib12),
[25](#bib.bib25)\]. Evaluation results need error
bars \[[27](#bib.bib27), [24](#bib.bib24)\], and top SWE-bench
submissions are statistically tied \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]; we find the
same for neighbouring agents on two more leaderboards and relate it to
verifier error. Item response theory selects tasks of informative
difficulty \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], and test equating links
versions \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]; we test calibrated per-task bars and link
versions through anchors. Surveys of agent evaluation report fragile
evaluators, contamination and fast-moving versions \[[9](#bib.bib9)\],
and rankings are fragile to the wording of the
task \[[38](#bib.bib38)\].

### Verifiers and judges.

Weak tests let wrong patches pass on SWE-bench \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[37](#bib.bib37), [36](#bib.bib36)\], false fails are
common \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], and seven of ten audited agent benchmarks
have flawed outcome checks \[[40](#bib.bib40)\]. This work makes the bit
valid and, in our design, hardens the _gate_; we ask what the score
should measure once it is.

### Beyond pass/fail.

Some leaderboards report the dollar cost of each agent beside its
accuracy \[[18](#bib.bib18), [17](#bib.bib17), [11](#bib.bib11)\]; time
horizons express success in human time \[[20](#bib.bib20)\].
Optimization benchmarks score an objective in single
domains \[[15](#bib.bib15), [30](#bib.bib30), [23](#bib.bib23),
[6](#bib.bib6)\], and NPPC pairs one checker with generated
instances \[[35](#bib.bib35)\]. These rules are not new; we show when
they matter and why a new version cannot replace them.

## 3 Failure A: The Bit Is Too Coarse to Rank the Frontier

A leaderboard ranks agents only if the gaps between neighbours exceed
two errors: _statistical_ error from a finite task set, and _verifier_
error from a key that accepts wrong solutions or rejects right ones. For
SWE-bench Verified \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] we use per-instance verdicts for
the official top 30 (1 Sep 2026), whose passing patches
SWE-ABS \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] re-checked with strengthened tests. For
Terminal-Bench 2.0 \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] we use per-trial results of 61
submissions, after removing three flagged for cheating. For
OSWorld-Verified \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] the top ten, seven with per-task
trajectories.

We group agents into _tiers_, sets of agents that the benchmark cannot
tell apart, by walking down the ranking: an agent starts a new tier if
it is significantly worse than the current tier’s leader. As that leader
is the best observed agent, we test all pairs at once, by a step-down
max-$`T`$ test on paired $`t`$-statistics with a null from permuting
agent labels within tasks \[[33](#bib.bib33), [31](#bib.bib31)\]. This
bounds the chance of any false split at 5% (3–6% for ten equal agents in
simulation, against 11–24% for Holm over the walk’s own comparisons).

Figure 2: The top ten agents on three leaderboards, with 95% confidence
intervals. Colours mark tiers; tier 1 cannot be separated from the
leader. OSWorld-Verified tiers use the seven agents with per-task data.

### Neighbours at the top are statistically tied.

In September 2024, the SWE-bench Verified top ten spanned 19.2 points
and three tiers; since March 2025 they have formed one or two (today 3.6
points, one tier; two in 59% of task resamples). Today
Terminal-Bench 2.0’s top ten form one tier and OSWorld-Verified’s two
(Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Failure A: The Bit Is Too Coarse to Rank the Frontier ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
On SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.0, none of the 45 pairs
within the top ten differs significantly (Holm over all 45), and on all
three leaderboards no neighbouring pair does; on OSWorld-Verified a
leading pair stands apart from a tied pack. The test is not weak: it
detects about 6.3 points with 80% power and splits ten real entries
spaced 5.7 points apart into ten tiers, but the largest gap between
SWE-bench neighbours is 1.4 points. Nor is it a ceiling: together the
top ten solve 91% of the tasks.

### The tests’ errors are larger than the gaps.

On SWE-bench Verified, strengthened tests reject 17.7% of the median
agent’s passing patches (range 10.8–23.2%). The rate differs between
agents beyond sampling noise ($`\chi^{2}_{29}=77.3`$, permutation
$`p<10^{-4}`$), with a spread of about 2 points, while neighbours are
typically 0.2–0.4 points apart. Correcting the verdicts shrinks the top
tier of the top 30 from 23 agents to a leading group of five, so a
better key changes who is in it. The 18 agents that leave it stay nearly
tied among themselves (1 of 153 pairs separates), and no neighbouring
pair separates. The correction reverses 8 of the 18 neighbouring orders
with a non-zero gap (Kendall $`\tau=0.51`$ between the two
leaderboards), although in 11 of these pairs the two agents’ inflation
differs significantly and by more than the gap. Removing the same share
of passes _at random_ reverses about as many (8; 95% band 4–11). So the
order of neighbours is noise, which any perturbation of this size
reorders. False fails, about one in six failed runs in an OSWorld
audit \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], add to this error; none are released per
agent, and simulated, they separate no neighbouring pair at a common
rate (5–25%) and at most 9% of runs at agent-specific rates.

This failure has nothing to do with the cap. Its remedies are a stronger
gate, more tasks or trials, more information per task, and tiers instead
of ranks.

## 4 Failure B: The Bit Is Capped at the Reference

If a key stops resolving the frontier because it is capped, the same
runs scored with room above the cap should resolve it again, where the
cap binds. We use every benchmark we found that releases per-run,
per-task continuous results; 4% to 71% of their runs beat the reference
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Failure B: The Bit Is Capped at the Reference ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).

Two of these benchmarks officially grade pass/fail per task:
MLE-bench \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] by medals, and METR \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] by
binarized success. The other three officially score a continuous
quantity; they let us ask what they would report had they been built as
answer keys, as most agent benchmarks are. We score the same released
runs three ways. The _answer key_ is the official binary verdict where
one exists, and otherwise whether the run reaches the reference: the
median human contestant on ALE-Bench \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], the expert
patch’s speedup on SWE-fficiency \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], and a 1.1-fold
speedup over the reference solver on AlgoTune \[[30](#bib.bib30)\]. The
_capped_ score gives partial credit up to the reference and none beyond
it. The _objective_ is the score of
§[6](#S6 "6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks"):
$`0`$ for runs that fail the benchmark’s own validity check or do no
better than a baseline, $`1`$ at the same reference, and above $`1`$ for
runs that beat it, up to a clip at $`3`$. The baseline is the
bottom-quartile human on ALE-Bench, no speedup on SWE-fficiency, a zero
speedup on AlgoTune, and the median submission on MLE-bench; on
AlgoTune, whose baseline is zero, $`\log_{2}(1+\text{speedup})`$
replaces the log-ratio.

We also report a paired variant of the saturation index
of \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], $`S=\exp(-R^{2})`$, where $`R`$ is the mean gap
between the first and fifth model over the standard deviation of their
per-task differences, times $`n^{1/4}`$ for $`n`$ tasks: near $`1`$, the
top five are within noise; near $`0`$, clearly separated.

### Binarize a benchmark that resolves, and it saturates.

ALE-Bench’s official score, mean contest performance, separates its top
ten into three tiers. Scored as an answer key against the median human,
the same runs give one tier ($`S=1.00`$), and the objective restores
three ($`S=0.03`$; with 40 tasks, robustly at least two). Across task
resamples, the key gives one tier every time and the objective at least
two 99.6% of the time. Partial credit below the reference does not help:
the capped score still gives one tier. Keeping the bit below the
reference and crediting only the margin above it gives all three.

### Where it is not saturated, the margin does not help.

On SWE-fficiency, partial credit below the reference helps most, and the
official score, a harmonic mean in which failed tasks dominate, resolves
worst of all (one tier): how a continuous score is aggregated matters as
much as whether it is continuous. On METR, few runs beat the reference.
On MLE-bench the key already separates the top ($`S=0.10`$), and the
heavy tail of Kaggle metrics blurs it ($`S=0.73`$). The margin above the
reference carries information mainly where the key has run out.

## 5 Failure B on Terminal-Bench, and the Ignored Cost

Figure 3: (a) What each task’s test checks. (b) Re-run: all runs of the
three models that pass/fail ties, on four tasks where all pass, as the
quantity relative to the reference solution (higher is better: speedup,
inverse MSE or keystrokes). Right: best over worst model median. (c)

### What the tests compute.

Consider the Terminal-Bench 2.0 task query-optimize: the agent must
speed up a slow SQL query. The test runs the agent’s query, measures its
runtime, and passes it if the runtime is below a threshold. The runtime
is computed and then thrown away. We coded the tests of all 89
Terminal-Bench 2.0 tasks and samples of SWE-bench Verified (30),
OSWorld-Verified (30) and $`\tau^{2}`$-bench (20) as T (a quantity
compared with a threshold), S (a unique correct end state) or F (other).
On Terminal-Bench 2.0, 18 tasks (20%) are T. The other samples contain
no T tasks
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5 Failure B on Terminal-Bench, and the Ignored Cost ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")a;
with samples this small, true rates up to 10–15% cannot be excluded).
For them our design offers a hardened gate and a cost meter, but no
quality objective unless the task is restated around a quantity, as
SWE-fficiency does \[[23](#bib.bib23)\].

### A controlled re-run.

Released logs record the quantity for only four T tasks, on differing
machines, so we re-ran 13 T tasks with tests changed only to print it.
The re-run is a small instance of the design of
§[7](#S7 "7 A Benchmark Design That Scores Acceptance, Quality and Cost Separately ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks"):
the unchanged test is the gate, the printed quantity the objective, and
per-trial dollars and tokens the meter. We ran four models
(gpt-5.3-codex, claude-opus-5, gpt-6-astra, gemini-3.1-pro; codex, opus,
astra, gemini below), reached through a third-party OpenAI-compatible
gateway, three trials per task in the Terminus-2
agent \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] (156 runs, about \$115 at list prices). Each
run had its own CPU core. Pass/fail ties opus, astra and gemini and
separates codex (within-task permutation tests, Holm).

### Same pass, an order of magnitude apart.

The tied three differ widely on the quantity
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5 Failure B on Terminal-Bench, and the Ignored Cost ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")b):
on portfolio-optimization all their runs pass, yet opus’s median speedup
is 10.9$`\times`$ and the others’ 1.3$`\times`$, the reference’s level;
on pytorch-model-recovery the median MSE spans 0.016 to 0.60. Of all
passing runs, 69% beat the reference solution. Ranking models is harder:
an anchor-free permutation test (failures at the threshold, standardized
log quantity, Holm) separates four of six pairs, pass/fail three, and
the one tied pair it separates (opus and gemini, Holm $`p=0.031`$) is
not separated on passing runs alone. With three trials per cell, the
robust evidence is per task. Two codex runs passed by reward hacking,
more verifier error
(§[3](#S3 "3 Failure A: The Bit Is Too Coarse to Rank the Frontier ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")),
and both stood out in the printed quantity; counted as failures, they
leave codex below its leaderboard entry on these tasks (stratified
$`p=0.025`$).

### What the key ignores about cost.

Over all 89 tasks, the six tied top-ten agents that log tokens differ
$`7.3\times`$ in median tokens per run
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5 Failure B on Terminal-Bench, and the Ignored Cost ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")c)
and, for the four priced at list rates, $`5.5\times`$ in median cost per
run (\$0.44 to \$2.47; every pair significant); the cheapest per run
uses the priciest model per token. Where per-run cost is released, cost
per accepted run separates pass/fail ties (4 of 8 adjacent
mini-SWE-agent pairs on SWE-bench Verified and 4 of 5 priced
Terminal-Bench 2.0 pairs in the top pass-rate tier differ at
$`p<0.05`$), but under one scaffold this mostly reflects price per token
and verbosity, not ability.

## 6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It

On ALE-Bench a run’s contest score can be compared with any bar, so we
can test the usual remedy for a saturated key, a higher bar, without a
new version.

### Candidates.

Let $`J`$ be the quantity a task measures (runtime, contest score),
$`J_{\text{base}}`$ a baseline, $`J_{\text{ref}}`$ the human or expert
reference, and $`s`$ the run’s position between them ($`0`$ at the
baseline, $`1`$ at the reference). Pass/fail _bars_ put the target at
the median human (the key), at the contest winner, or where it best
separates the models of the time. Continuous scores from prior work:
_capped_ $`\min(s,1)`$; _linear_
$`(J-J_{\text{base}})/(J_{\text{ref}}-J_{\text{base}})`$, the
human-normalized score of reinforcement learning \[[28](#bib.bib28)\];
_log-ratio_
$`\log(J/J_{\text{base}})/\log(J_{\text{ref}}/J_{\text{base}})`$, as in
systems benchmarking \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]; the log-ratio _clipped_
at $`3`$; and the _percentile_ among a pool of runs on the
task \[[7](#bib.bib7), [2](#bib.bib2)\]. All are floored at $`0`$ and
averaged over tasks.

### A pass/fail bar sees one band of ability.

We split the 141 ALE-Bench models with known or estimated release dates
into 46 released by the end of 2025 and 95 released later
(Table [2](#S6.T2 "Table 2 ‣ A pass/fail bar sees one band of ability. ‣ 6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
The median-human bar ties the later top ten. The contest-winner bar
separates them into two tiers, but scores 89% of the earlier models zero
on every task (agreement with the human contest rating: $`0.43`$). A bar
tuned to separate the earlier models puts the later top ten in one tier.
Each bar resolves one band of ability; raising it moves the band instead
of widening it. This is what a harder bar on the same tasks does; a real
new version also adds tasks, which we do not simulate. Nor does varying
the bar by task: setting each task’s bar where half the earlier models
pass still ties the later top ten. Several bars per task, a graded score
in the spirit of polytomous item response models, do resolve them; a
continuous score is the limit of that. Bounded scores with room above
the reference rank both groups (clipped log-ratio, percentiles), while
the linear score and the unclipped log-ratio tie the earlier top ten,
which supports the clip. The clipped log-ratio agrees with humans about
as well as the key (difference $`+0.02`$, 95% CI $`-0.002`$ to
$`+0.05`$) and is more stable across task halves ($`+0.03`$, CI excludes
0). On the 32 later models with verifiable dates, the tiers are
unchanged (3 vs 1; $`S=0.41`$).

### Choosing among continuous scores.

Capped scores saturate with the bar. Percentiles depend on the pool:
adding or removing models flips up to a third of pairs. Linear scores
are dominated by a few tasks: one ALE-Bench problem’s contest score
spans four orders of magnitude, and 79% of the linear score comes from
the top 5% of tasks (log-ratio: 11%).

### Why the clipped log-ratio.

The log form is derived rather than chosen on ALE-Bench. A score of a
positive quantity should not depend on its unit, so it is a function
$`f`$ of $`x=J/J_{\text{base}}`$, and credit should add under
composition, so $`f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)`$. The only continuous solutions of
this Cauchy equation are $`f(x)=k\log x`$; averaging over tasks then
summarizes ratio-scale results meaningfully \[[13](#bib.bib13),
[1](#bib.bib1)\], and dividing by
$`\log(J_{\text{ref}}/J_{\text{base}})`$ sets $`k`$ per task so that the
reference scores $`1`$. The clip at three reference gaps is a stated
convention: it bounds any single task’s share of the score to $`3/n`$
for $`n`$ tasks. Without it, a task whose reference barely beats the
baseline dominates: on SWE-fficiency, one with an expert speedup of
1.001 would hold a third of the score. Its value matters little: it
binds on 4% of ALE-Bench runs, and from clip 2 to none, agreement with
humans stays within 0.90–0.94 in both cohorts and the tiers do not
change. A clip that binds often signals a weak reference, to be replaced
rather than the clip tuned. We also ablated the form: unclipped log-fold
scores, raw or scaled by each task’s anchor-set spread, agree less with
humans (later cohort: at most 0.89 against 0.935) and are less stable; a
quality–cost composite ranks cost
(§[7](#S7 "7 A Benchmark Design That Scores Acceptance, Quality and Cost Separately ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).

We therefore propose the log-ratio, floored at the baseline and clipped
at three reference gaps, averaged over tasks, for positive quantities
such as runtime or contest score, and the linear score with the same
floor and clip for quantities with no natural zero, such as Kaggle
metrics. It gives at least the key’s top-ten tiers on SWE-fficiency and
AlgoTune, one fewer on MLE-bench.

## 7 A Benchmark Design That Scores Acceptance, Quality and Cost Separately

We separate the roles that an answer key merges
(Fig. [4](#S7.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 7 A Benchmark Design That Scores Acceptance, Quality and Cost Separately ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks"));
$`x`$ is an agent’s final artifact and $`h`$ its trajectory.

Figure 4: Left: an answer key merges acceptance, quality and the
author’s solution into one per-task checker that emits one bit, with an
LLM judge as the fallback. Right: the layered design. Quality is scored
once per domain with room above the reference, and cost is metered
separately.

### Intent

states what the human wants: hard constraints and a direction of
preference (“the bracket must carry the load; lighter is better”), never
a procedure or a target shape, which would force per-task
keys \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]; the bracket intent needs one physics check and
one mass objective per domain.

### Gate

$`G(x)\in\{0,1\}`$ is a deterministic, hardened acceptance check
(behavioural, physics or safety, anti-tamper), where verifier
hardening \[[40](#bib.bib40), [37](#bib.bib37), [36](#bib.bib36)\]
belongs.

### Objective

$`s(x)`$ normalizes a raw quantity $`J(x)`$ (runtime, mass, cost,
contest score) to dated anchors, mapping $`J_{\text{base}}`$ to $`0`$
and $`J_{\text{ref}}`$ to $`1`$:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s(x)=G(x)\cdot\min\!\Big(3,\ \max\!\Big(0,\ \tfrac{\log(J(x)/J_{\text{base}})}{\log(J_{\text{ref}}/J_{\text{base}})}\Big)\Big)
``` |  |

for positive quantities, averaged over tasks
(§[6](#S6 "6 Why Graded Credit, Not a Higher Bar, Fixes It ‣ Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks")).
The design requires a strong reference, near the best known solution,
and excludes tasks whose reference barely beats the baseline by a margin
fixed in advance, as SWE-fficiency does. A clip that binds often flags a
weak reference, to be replaced, as on four re-run tasks. The objective
is written once per domain; exposing it in the re-run took one or two
lines per task.

### Meter

$`c(h)`$ records dollars at a dated price table, tokens, steps and time,
reported as cost-to-target \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] and never blended into
$`s`$.

### Anchors

$`J_{\text{base}}`$ and $`J_{\text{ref}}`$ are frozen with a date, so
two versions of a benchmark can share one scale. Runtimes drift with
hardware, so the baseline and reference artifacts are re-measured on the
machine of the runs and enter only as ratios, as the re-run did.
Re-running three reference agents linked an old and a harder new half of
ALE-Bench within 1–3 points of the accuracy of re-running every old
model, which a moving bar cannot do.

### Judges and reporting.

LLMs may draft gate and objective code at authoring time, with human
audit \[[32](#bib.bib32)\], but do not score at run time, so no judge
can be persuaded; this is a design position \[[39](#bib.bib39)\], not a
finding. Results are reported lexicographically: the gate rate with
statistical tiers, then the mean of $`s`$ (a run that fails the gate
scores $`0`$, so the objective never rewards an invalid run). Cost is a
separate axis, reported jointly with quality and its Pareto front. Any
claimed lead states the interval of exchange rates $`\lambda`$ (folds of
quality per fold of cost) over which it holds; holding for every
$`\lambda`$ is Pareto dominance. A composite would rank cost, not
quality: on ALE-Bench its $`\tau`$ with the human rating is $`-0.35`$ in
the later cohort, and 129 of 137 adjacent pairs flip as $`\lambda`$
moves from 0.5 to 2.

## 8 Limitations and Conclusion

### Limitations.

Per-agent verifier error exists only for SWE-bench Verified, where we
take SWE-ABS’s tests as ground truth and can bound false fails only by
simulation. The sliding-window result rests on ALE-Bench, with partly
reconstructed dates; AlgoTune agrees weakly, and MLE-bench, whose key is
not saturated, shows no such effect. The re-run’s aggregate separation
of tied models is borderline (three trials). Rubric-graded deliverables
such as GDPval have neither a gate nor a scalar objective, and
objectives can be gamed \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], so the gate must stay
hardened.

### Conclusion.

Agent benchmarks often saturate in the score, not the tasks: the bit is
too coarse to separate leading agents, and capped at its author.
Benchmarks should build in an anchored objective with room above the
reference and a separate cost meter, so a saturated key ends a version,
not the measurement.
````
