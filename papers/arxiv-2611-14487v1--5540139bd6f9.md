# Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game

Muhan Gao Affiliation: Texas A&M University    Hangxiao Zhu
Affiliation: Texas A&M University    Xinqi Su Affiliation: Texas A&M
University    Yu Zhang Affiliation: Texas A&M University    Daniel
Khashabi Affiliation: Johns Hopkins University    Kuan-Hao Huang

###### Abstract

Research ideation is iterative: researchers refine their ideas step by
step based on feedback. Yet existing evaluations obscure this search
process, making it difficult to understand how LLMs explore the space of
research ideas and what limits their progress. We introduce Re-Search,
which adapts the format of Twenty Questions to research ideation: given
only the research problem of a published paper, a model must rediscover
the paper’s core idea by asking yes-or-no questions. Built from 200 ICML
2026 papers, Re-Search tracks how a model’s idea evolves as it asks
questions and receives binary oracle feedback, isolating ideation from
execution. Across six recent LLMs, nearly all progress happens within
the first 30 of 100 questions, which largely decide how the game ends;
after that, most models plateau. Through controlled interventions on the
candidate questions and the hypothesis space, we find that the
bottleneck is generation, not judgment: models recognize a good idea or
a good question when one is offered, but often cannot produce it on
their own. We further analyze the ideation traces and find that models
fail by either over-exploiting one direction or over-exploring too many.
These findings point to concrete design principles for autoresearch
systems: since models can judge candidates they cannot produce,
enriching the search with externally sourced ideas and questions may be
more effective than improving any single model’s generation ability.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2611.14487v1/fig1_climb.png)

Figure 1: DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GPT-5.4 play Re-Search on the same
paper. Height reflects recovery of the paper’s idea. Green marks

circling in a basin, GPT-5.4 recovers from a dip and reaches the summit.

Research ideation is not a one-shot proposal but an iterative search
interleaved with every stage of a project: researchers form hypotheses,
test them, and refine them based on feedback. This process can be viewed
as adaptive information seeking over a space of plausible yet unverified
ideas ([Klahr and Dunbar, 1988](#bib.bib15)). However, existing
benchmarks provide limited visibility into this iterative search.
Ideation benchmarks evaluate one-shot proposals using LLM judges ([Guo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)), overlooking
how ideas evolve through feedback. End-to-end benchmarks involve
iterative search but entangle it with coding, debugging, and
experimentation ([Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Wu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17); [Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib18); [Lupidi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19); [Garikaparthi et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Chan et
al., 2025](#bib.bib6)), making it difficult to distinguish failures of
ideation from failures of execution.

In this work, we aim to isolate the information-seeking process from the
rest of autoresearch. To this end, we introduce Re-Search, a two-player
questioning game that makes every step of this process observable and
measurable
(Fig [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
The setting is a scientific variant of Twenty Questions ([Mosher and
Hornsby, 1966](#bib.bib21); [Siegler, 1977](#bib.bib22)), a classic
paradigm in cognitive science for studying human reasoning and problem
solving ([Coenen et al., 2019](#bib.bib27)). Given only the research
problem of a published paper, an inquisitor model must rediscover the
paper’s idea by asking up to 100 yes-or-no questions, while an answerer
model reads the full paper and replies to the questions. Every 10
questions, the inquisitor model writes down its current best guess of
the idea, which a panel of judges scores against the paper’s idea
(§[3](#S3 "3 The Re-Search Game ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),
see
Fig [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Information seeking and question asking. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
as an example). Success on Re-Search requires three capabilities. The
model must (1) ask _informative questions_, which means both proposing
good candidate questions and judging which of them to ask, (2) maintain
an _adequate hypothesis space_ that includes the correct idea, and (3)
_hill-climb_ along promising directions while abandoning dead ends.

We evaluate six recent LLMs on 200 ICML 2026 papers. GPT-5.4 leads by a
wide margin, followed by Gemma-4-31B and Claude Sonnet 4.5; the
remaining three models score about two thirds as high as GPT-5.4. Yet
the models share a common pattern: most progress happens within the
first 30 questions, after which gains slow sharply for all but GPT-5.4,
and a model’s score at question 30 largely predicts its final score. So
why do models stall with 70 questions left, and which capabilities are
missing?

We first rule out a simple knowledge deficit: on papers where both a
strong and a weak model can list the core idea beforehand, the strong
model still finishes far ahead. The first clue comes from question
quality. When a weak model can choose from candidate questions proposed
by several models, it scores far above its own solo performance. The
same pattern holds for the hypothesis space. We give models a survey of
candidate directions that includes the paper’s core idea; without it,
many models never reach the idea, but with it, they zero in on it within
a few questions—and the weaker the model, the larger the gain.

In both cases, models _recognize_ a good idea or a good question when
one is offered, but often cannot _generate_ it on their own. The
ideation traces show how hill-climbing fails: weaker models either
over-exploit one direction or over-explore too many, stuck into an
unproductive pattern early.

Our contributions are: (1) Re-Search, a two-player questioning game that
isolates research ideation from execution and makes every step of
ideation observable and measurable; (2) the finding that ideation is
decided early: most progress happens within the first 30 questions, and
weaker models lock into either over-exploiting one direction or
over-exploring too many; and (3) a diagnosis that the bottleneck is
generation, not judgment, pointing to external candidates as a direct
lever for improving autoresearch systems.

## 2 Related Work

#### AI scientists and their evaluation.

LLM-based systems now attempt to automate research from idea to
paper ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2); [Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib1);
[Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Gottweis et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). Most are evaluated end to end: benchmarks run an
agent on a research project for hours and score the outcome ([Chan et
al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11); [Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Rank et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Lupidi et al., 2026](#bib.bib19); [Garikaparthi et
al., 2026](#bib.bib20)), or grade intermediate steps against expert
rubrics ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Kon et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12); [Bragg et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Jansen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14)), and open-world environments let agents pursue long
projects on their own ([Chung and Du, 2025](#bib.bib53)). Some of them
ask agents to rediscover the findings of a hidden paper, from raw
data ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib38)) or by running experiments ([Wang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib48)). In all of these, the search for the idea is
mixed with coding, debugging, and experiments, and can only be
reconstructed from logs afterwards ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib45)).
Re-Search isolates this search, so that each question and its effect on
the idea can be observed directly.

#### Research ideation.

A separate line of work asks whether LLMs can propose research ideas.
[Si et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4) find in a large human study that LLM ideas
are judged more novel but slightly less feasible than those of experts,
and benchmarks such as IdeaBench ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)) and the
ideation stage of MLR-Bench ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) rate
generated ideas with LLM judges. Other systems refine ideas over several
rounds, through literature-grounded review ([Baek et al.,
2025](#bib.bib49)), planned retrieval of external knowledge ([Hu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib50)), or multi-agent and quality-diversity search ([Chen
and Zhang, 2026](#bib.bib52); [Gumma et al., 2026](#bib.bib51)). These
evaluations either treat ideation as a single step or guide the search
with LLM feedback, and none of them has a correct answer to compare
against. Like us, [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43) and [Liu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib44) use recent papers as ground truth, testing whether
LLMs can rediscover their hypotheses by retrieving inspirations and
composing a hypothesis from them. The model receives its evidence up
front, whereas in Re-Search it must find the evidence itself by asking.

Concurrently, [Wen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib60) introduce Priced Guidance
to quantify the cost of steering a model toward a paper’s core idea. The
evaluated model proposes multiple-choice questions with reported answer
probabilities. A guide with access to the target then selects an answer,
priced at $`-\log_{2}p_{\mathrm{selected}}`$ bits. Because this cost is
computed from verbalized probabilities, the resulting score reflects
probability-reporting behavior as well as candidate generation. Their
analysis also highlights the cost of acquiring useful candidate
concepts, consistent with our finding that weaker models can use
supplied candidates they struggle to propose independently. In our work,
the focus is on _how_ ideation unfolds through interaction and _why_
progress stalls. Following the cognitive view of discovery as hypothesis
search guided by experimental feedback ([Langley et al.,
1987](#bib.bib16)), Re-Search tracks idea recovery through
model-generated questions and paper-grounded feedback. Controlled
interventions on candidate questions and hypotheses then examine how
candidate availability and selection affect recovery, without requiring
models to report answer probabilities.

#### Information seeking and question asking.

Twenty Questions and related games are standard tools for studying how
people ask questions: children move from testing single hypotheses to
asking questions that narrow the space ([Mosher and Hornsby,
1966](#bib.bib21); [Ruggeri and Lombrozo, 2015](#bib.bib24); [Ruggeri et
al., 2016](#bib.bib25)), and people adapt their questions to the
structure of the hypothesis space ([Nelson et al., 2014](#bib.bib23);
[Cohen and Lake, 2016](#bib.bib26); [Coenen et al., 2019](#bib.bib27)).
Recent work uses the same games to evaluate and improve
LLMs ([Bertolazzi et al., 2023](#bib.bib28); [Zhang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib29); [Mazzaccara et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)), often by
choosing questions with high expected information gain or value of
information ([Hu et al., 2024](#bib.bib30); [Choudhury et al.,
2026](#bib.bib33); [Grand et al., 2026](#bib.bib35); [Dong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib34)); MediQ ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)) brings the
same setting to clinical diagnosis. In these tasks, the target is an
object, a diagnosis, or a board configuration from a small, known set of
candidates. In Re-Search, the target is a research idea, and the model
must generate the candidates itself. More broadly, LLMs in multi-turn
and long-horizon settings tend to commit early and rarely recover from
early mistakes ([Laban et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Sinha et al.,
2026](#bib.bib40)), although deep-research agents can also switch
direction mid-trajectory ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib46)); we find that
in research ideation, early questions largely decide the outcome
(§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Early Questions Decide the Outcome ‣ 4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).

## 3 The Re-Search Game

We define the game in
§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Task Description ‣ 3 The Re-Search Game ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),
describe how we build it from published papers in
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Data Construction ‣ 3 The Re-Search Game ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),

### 3.1 Task Description

In Twenty Questions, one player holds a hidden target (e.g., a specific
person), and the other must identify it by iteratively asking yes-or-no
questions. Re-Search adapts this format to research ideation. Each game
is played on a pair $`(p,s)`$ built from a published paper in ICML 2026,
where $`p`$ is the research problem and $`s`$ is the solution that the
paper proposes for it.

Three roles are involved: (1) The _inquisitor_
$`{\color[rgb]{0,0.4453,0.6992}M_{\mathrm{i}}}`$ sees only $`p`$. At
each turn $`t`$, it asks a yes-or-no question $`q_{t}`$ based on the
problem and the history of earlier questions and answers,
$`q_{t}={\color[rgb]{0,0.4453,0.6992}M_{\mathrm{i}}}(p,h_{t-1})`$, where
$`h_{t-1}=(q_{1},a_{1},\dots,q_{t-1},a_{t-1})`$ and $`h_{0}`$ is empty.
(2) The _answerer_ $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0.418,0}M_{\mathrm{a}}}`$ reads
the full paper $`d`$ and replies to each question on its own,
$`a_{t}={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0.418,0}M_{\mathrm{a}}}(d,q_{t})\in\{\textsc{Yes},\textsc{No},\textsc{Unknown}\}`$.
At fixed points $`t\in\{0,10,\dots,T\}`$, the inquisitor also writes an
idea card
$`c_{t}={\color[rgb]{0,0.4453,0.6992}M_{\mathrm{i}}}(p,h_{t})`$, a
short, structured statement of its current best guess of $`s`$.

\(3\) After the game, a panel of three LLM judges
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6328,0.375,0.5195}M_{\mathrm{j}}^{(1)}},{\color[rgb]{0.6328,0.375,0.5195}M_{\mathrm{j}}^{(2)}},{\color[rgb]{0.6328,0.375,0.5195}M_{\mathrm{j}}^{(3)}}`$
scores all saved idea cards $`\{c_{0},c_{10},\dots,c_{T}\}`$ against
$`s`$.

#### Information revealed by questioning.

Let $`S`$ be the hidden solution, $`P`$ the research problem, and
$`H_{T}`$ the question–answer history. Suppose answers belong to a
finite alphabet $`\mathcal{A}`$, and each question is conditionally
independent of $`S`$ given $`P`$ and the preceding history. Then

       ``` math
       I(S;H_{T}\mid P)\leq T\log_{2}|\mathcal{A}|.

Binary feedback yields at most $`T\log_{2}2=T`$ bits; our three-answer

gained through questioning, conditional on the problem, rather than
information supplied before the game. Additional questions need not
supply additional information, and this bound does not guarantee a
particular recovery score. See

for the proof.

### 3.2 Data Construction

We build Re-Search from 200 papers accepted at ICML 2026, one of the
most recent top-tier conferences. The papers are selected based on three
criteria: (1) the main contribution is a method, rather than a
benchmark, a dataset, or an analysis, so that each paper has a concrete
solution to recover; (2) its review score is as high as possible,
indicating a reliable solution; and (3) the paper became public as late
as possible. All selected papers first appeared, on arXiv or the
conference, after 31 August 2025, later than every reported knowledge
cutoff
(§[A](#A1 "Appendix A Models and Knowledge Cutoffs ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
minimizing the risk of knowledge contamination ([Cheng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib36); [Roberts et al., 2024](#bib.bib57)).

For each paper, we take its full text $`d`$ and use an LLM (GLM-5.2) to
extract the pair $`(p,s)`$ in a structured form, which has been shown to
be reliable in prior work ([Gao et al., 2025](#bib.bib37); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib44)). The research problem $`p`$ has four fields (the
problem domain, the research goal, the main challenge, and the gap in
prior work), and the solution $`s`$ has four fields (the main
contribution, the approach, the technical components, and the novelty).
Human annotators (three Computer Science PhD students) checked the
extracted pairs $`(p,s)`$ for 100 papers. By majority vote, 93% are
correct, and the errors mostly omit a component that distinguishes the
paper’s method.

### 3.3 Evaluation

We score each idea card $`c_{t}`$ against the hidden solution $`s`$ with
a panel of three LLM judges (Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.6, and GPT-OSS-120B).
Each judge sees the research problem $`p`$, hidden solution $`s`$, and
idea card $`c_{t}`$, and rates on a 1–10 scale how much of $`s`$ the
card recovers. The recovery score is the mean of the three ratings,
$`r_{t}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}{\color[rgb]{0.6328,0.375,0.5195}M_{\mathrm{j}}^{(k)}}(p,s,c_{t})`$.
The judges agree closely: over all the cards in our experiment, their
pairwise Spearman correlations range from 0.85 to 0.88. Since three of
the judges are also inquisitors, we check for self-preference: leaving
out the judge from an inquisitor’s own model family changes the gain
from naming the idea by at most 0.08 points
(§[B](#A2 "Appendix B Judge Validation ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).

Human evaluation. We use human annotators to check the reliability of
the LLM judges, by comparing 498 pairs of idea cards; for each pair,
they choose the card closer to the hidden solution, or mark a tie. Their
labels agree well, with a Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ of 0.83. In 91.2% of pairs,
the LLM judges agree with the annotator majority on which card is
closer, or that the two are tied. Most of the remaining pairs are ones
that the annotators mark as a tie but the judges do not; when both sides
prefer one card, they choose the same card in 97.8% of pairs.

#### Model selection.

We evaluate both open-weight and proprietary LLMs of different
strengths: Gemma-4-31B, GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Preview,
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and GPT-5.4. We do not include newer
models because of the risk of knowledge contamination. In all games,
Gemma-4-31B serves as the answerer, each inquisitor asks up to $`T=100`$
questions, and we play three games per paper. Among three candidate
answerers (GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and Gemma-4-31B), Gemma agrees
most often with human labels on 795 annotated answers (94.0%, against
86.2% for GPT-OSS-120B and 78.4% for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B; see
§ [D](#A4 "Appendix D Choice of Answerer ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
for details), and only 1.1% flip Yes and No, far below the 10% answer
noise assumed in [Grand et al. (2026)](#bib.bib35). Therefore we use
Gemma-4-31B as the answerer model.

### 4.1 Most Models Stall Early

Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 Evaluation ‣ 3 The Re-Search Game ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
shows how recovery develops over a game. Before the first question,
every model’s idea card $`c_{0}`$ scores very low, confirming that the
research problem alone does not reveal the solution. The game then
unfolds in three stages.

_Questions 1–10_ bring the largest gain for every model, with scores
rising by $`+1.3`$ to $`+3.2`$. _Questions 11–30_ add much less, between
$`+0.2`$ and $`+0.8`$. The most interesting part happens in _Questions
31–100_, where the models are separated into different patterns. Three
models stall, gaining almost nothing over the remaining 70 questions;
two keep improving slowly; and only the strongest model keeps climbing
steadily.

A natural implication of
§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Most Models Stall Early ‣ 4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
is that, if most models stall after about 30 questions, the early
questions should largely decide how a game ends. This echoes findings in
long-horizon LLM generation, where early mistakes compound and are
rarely repaired ([Zhang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib41); [Laban et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39); [Sinha et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)). We test this with
a prefix-fork experiment ([Bigelow et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)). Each
model plays three games per paper. First, for each paper, we pick the
games with the highest and the lowest final score, and truncate both at
round $`k`$ ($`k\in\{10,20,30\}`$). Then, the same model continues each
prefix to question 100 three more times, yielding three continuations
from the best start and three from the worst. If the opening matters,
the best-start continuations should stay ahead.

As shown in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Early Questions Decide the Outcome ‣ 4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),
the score at question $`k`$ predicts the score at question 100 well.
Across games, the correlation is $`r=0.76`$–$`0.87`$ at $`k=10`$ and
rises to $`r=0.87`$–$`0.92`$ at $`k=30`$.

## 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck

As
§[4](#S4 "4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
shows, most models plateau early, while GPT-5.4 keeps climbing. What
explains this gap? We first rule out knowledge as the main cause
(§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Knowledge Explains Little of the Gap ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
then show that supplying external candidates, either as questions from
other models
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Weak Models Can Pick Informative Questions ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"))
or as a survey of the hypothesis space
(§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 A Survey Enriches the Hypothesis Space ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
closes much of the gap.

### 5.1 Knowledge Explains Little of the Gap

A strong model may simply have seen more scientific text, know more
candidate ideas before the game, and turn this knowledge into an
advantage in the game. To test this, we use a closed-book probe that
isolates the effect of knowledge. We collect a subset of 100 papers, and
focus on two models, a strong one (GPT-5.4) and a weak one
(GPT-OSS-120B). Each model is provided with the research problem and
asked to list ten candidate solutions, three times, for 30 candidates in
total; two LLM annotators then judge whether the list contains the
paper’s core idea.

Knowledge does not explain the gap between models. On this 100 papers
subset, GPT-5.4 scores 2.54 points higher than GPT-OSS-120B; on the 24
papers where both models list the core idea, it still scores 2.28
higher, and knowledge closes barely 10% of the gap. Therefore, knowing
the idea is not enough.

### 5.2 Weak Models Can Pick Informative Questions

§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Knowledge Explains Little of the Gap ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
suggests that the gap lies in how models ask. Asking an informative
question involves two steps: proposing candidate questions and choosing
which one to ask. To separate the two, we let a weak model choose each
question from candidates proposed by several models, rather than asking
only its own. If it improves, the bottleneck lies in proposing rather
than judging. We test this using GPT-5.4, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and GPT-OSS-120B. Each round, all four models
independently propose a question from the same history, and one model
picks among them.

After 30 rounds with the shared pool, all four models score between 5.19

the gain of weak models does not come from blindly copying GPT-5.4’s

Which of the two steps drives the gain? We vary each while holding the
other fixed. When GPT-OSS-120B proposes all four candidates itself
(best-of-4 sampling, akin to self-consistency ([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib47))), its score rises only from 4.16 to 4.57, well below
the 5.20 where candidates are from four models, so the candidates
matter. When we keep the four-model pool but pick at random, the score
drops to 4.43, not significantly above playing alone, so selection
matters too
(Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.2 Weak Models Can Pick Informative Questions ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
Both steps are necessary, but they are not equally scarce. When we swap
the selector across models, the final scores barely change
(Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.2 Weak Models Can Pick Informative Questions ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"));
the gap between models therefore comes mainly from the candidates they
propose. A weak model’s selection is good enough; what it lacks are
informative questions worth selecting.

### 5.3 A Survey Enriches the Hypothesis Space

Figure 7: Top: score after 100 questions under $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ and
$`C_{\text{full}}`$ for each inquisitor. Bottom: gain in that score from
naming the idea ($`C_{\text{full}}-C_{\text{decoy}}`$, with 95%
confidence intervals), against the score under $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$; the
dashed line is a least-squares fit (Pearson $`r=-0.74`$ across the six
inquisitors). Naming the idea helps most the models that score lowest
without it.
(§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 A Survey Enriches the Hypothesis Space ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"))

In real research, ideas do not start from a purely blank page, as
researchers usually begin with a landscape in mind: a rough sense of
which approaches could solve the problem. We give the inquisitor a
similar starting point with a _survey_
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Model selection. ‣ 4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
an overview of the hypothesis space for the research problem. Each
survey has five to seven dimensions along which solutions to the problem
can differ, and lists four to six candidate approaches in each
dimension, named at the level of known families of techniques. The
survey is placed right after the problem when the model asks questions,
with no instruction on how to use it, and is not shown when the model
writes its idea card
(§ [C](#A3 "Appendix C Survey Construction ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
More importantly, the inquisitor is not told whether the solution is in
the survey.

Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.2 Weak Models Can Pick Informative Questions ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
shows the four context conditions. Naming the core idea
($`C_{\text{full}}`$) lifts the curves of most models clearly above
$`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ and $`C_{\text{dummy}}`$. When the name is replaced
by a decoy ($`C_{\text{decoy}}`$), the survey still helps through the
other listed components, but the curves slightly fall back towards
$`C_{\text{none}}`$; removing those components as well
($`C_{\text{dummy}}`$) brings them closer still.

A survey enlarges the model’s hypothesis space from outside: under
$`C_{\text{full}}`$, the correct idea is already among the candidates.
Naming the core idea in the survey makes every model reach the core idea
during the game more often, and helps weaker models most: the final
score improves by up to 1.25 points, with the largest gains for the
models that score lowest without it ($`r=-0.74`$,
Figure [7](#S5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 5.3 A Survey Enriches the Hypothesis Space ‣ 5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
This is not because models simply go through the survey item by item. A
survey lists 20 to 42 candidates, yet under $`C_{\text{full}}`$ models
ask about the named idea within a median of 3 to 6 questions, and far
more often than they ask about a decoy in the same line under
$`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ (67–89% against 29–52% of games;
§ [C](#A3 "Appendix C Survey Construction ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
Even under $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$, models ask about that line more often
than about nearby lines, so they can find the right family of methods;
what the name adds is the specific idea. Once the correct idea is in the
hypothesis space, weak models recognize it; what they lack is the
ability to put it there themselves.

## 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees

§[5](#S5 "5 Diagnosing the Bottleneck ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
shows why models differ, but not how ideation unfolds question by
question. A game is recorded as a flat sequence of questions, but the
questions are not independent: most of them follow up on an earlier
question, while the rest open new directions. Research proceeds in the
same way: a researcher opens a few lines of inquiry, runs follow-up
experiments on the promising ones, and drops those that stop paying off.
We therefore turn each game into a tree that makes this structure
explicit. Each top-level branch is a line of inquiry, and its depth
shows how far the model follows it; together they reflect the model’s
hypothesis space and its hill-climbing.

As using LLMs to turn reasoning traces into trees or graphs has become a
common way to analyze how LLMs reason ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib54);
[Zeng et al., 2025](#bib.bib55); [Lee et al., 2025](#bib.bib56)), we
follow this line of work and use GPT-6 Astra to construct the trees. For
every question, the model chooses a parent: one of the earlier
questions, or the research problem if the question opens a new
direction.

tree: mean top-level branches per game on 200 papers, as a range over
the models with that pattern (per-model means in
Figure [11](#A7.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix G More Search Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
See more examples in
§[G](#A7 "Appendix G More Search Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game").

### 6.1 The Shape of the Search

We build 1,200 trees from six models on the 200 papers. Although the
three weaker models end with similar scores, their trees reveal opposite
failure modes
(Figure [8](#S6.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
The number of top-level branches
$`B_{\mathrm{top}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[\operatorname{parent}(q_{t})=p]`$,
i.e., questions asked directly under the research problem, cleanly
separates three patterns
(Figure [11](#A7.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix G More Search Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).

Over-exploitation. DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3.6 open only 5.2–5.5
branches per game. Once they confirm a direction, they enumerate
variants within it rather than questioning the direction itself. For
example, after learning a paper uses weight statistics for quantization
scales, DeepSeek-V4-Flash spends dozens of questions guessing which
statistic, never asking whether the approach is something else entirely.
Across all games, 23% of these two models’ questions are such
enumerations.

Over-exploration. GPT-OSS-120B opens 13.7 branches, jumping to new
methods regardless of feedback. It is the only model that switches
branch more often after a Yes than after a No.

Balanced. The three stronger models open 7.2–8.2 branches, following
confirmed directions and stepping back when a line stops paying off.

While the two failure modes are opposite, they both explain why most
models plateau after 30 questions
(§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Most Models Stall Early ‣ 4 Ideation Dynamics in Re-Search ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")).
The stronger models balance the two, which lets them keep climbing.

### 6.2 Search Breadth and Final Recovery

Figure 9: Search breadth and final recovery across 1,200 games. Shading
separates the three patterns in
§[6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The Shape of the Search ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game").
Left. After the inquisitor receives a Yes ($`\blacktriangle`$) or a No
($`\vartriangle`$), how often its next question is a child of the
answered question (_goes to a child_) or moves to another top-level
branch (_leaves the branch_). In particular, over-exploitation models
rarely leave the branch, even after a No, while the over-exploration
model goes to a child more often after a No than after a Yes, and leaves
the branch about 30% of the time after either answer. Right. Each point
groups games by their number of top-level branches. The $`y`$-axis shows
how much higher or lower these games score at question 100 than an
average game, once differences between models and between papers are
taken out. The fitted curve peaks at about eight branches (dotted line):
games with a moderate number of top-level branches score the highest.

§[6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The Shape of the Search ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
reveals contrasting search patterns among the weaker models: DeepSeek
and Qwen tend toward over-exploitation, repeatedly refining a small
number of directions, whereas GPT-OSS tends toward over-exploration,
frequently opening new branches. Meanwhile, stronger models show a more
balanced pattern, opening new directions while pursuing promising ones.
This raises two questions: how do these patterns arise, and is
intermediate search breadth associated with better final recovery?

To see how the patterns arise, we look at each answered question and the
question that follows it
(Figure [9](#S6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 6.2 Search Breadth and Final Recovery ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),
left). After a Yes ($`\blacktriangle`$), the stronger models make the
next question a child of the answered one far more often than after a No
(39% vs. 24% for GPT-5.4), and after a No ($`\vartriangle`$), about a
quarter of their next questions leave the top-level branch. DeepSeek and
Qwen also ask more children after a Yes, but after a No they leave the
branch less often than any other model, so new questions keep
accumulating in the same top-level branch. GPT-OSS shows the opposite
pattern: it asks a child more often after a No than after a Yes, and
about 30% of its next questions leave the branch regardless of the
answer, which gives it the most top-level branches.

We examine this relationship across the 1,200 games by fitting a
quadratic model relating final recovery scores to the number of
top-level branches, with model and paper fixed effects. For the points
in
Figure [9](#S6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 6.2 Search Breadth and Final Recovery ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")
(right), we group games by their number of top-level branches and fit
each game’s final score as the sum of three parts: one for its model,
one for its paper, and one for its group; each point is the group’s
part, shifted so that the average game is at 0. A point at $`+0.5`$ thus
means that these games end half a point higher than expected from their
model and paper alone. The fitted curve shows an inverted-U
relationship: recovery is highest at an intermediate breadth and lower
at both extremes. The estimated peak is 8.3 top-level branches
(Figure [9](#S6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 6.2 Search Breadth and Final Recovery ‣ 6 Turning Ideation Traces into Trees ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game"),
right). This association provides quantitative support for the
connection between the search patterns described above and final
recovery.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduce Re-Search, a two-player questioning game where a model must
rediscover the idea of a published paper by asking yes-or-no questions.
By reducing ideation to a question-and-answer loop with oracle feedback,
the game dissects how models search for and refine ideas.

On 200 ICML 2026 papers, most of the six models we test plateau after
about 30 questions. Controlled experiments show that the bottleneck is
not knowledge or judgment but generation: when we supply external
candidates such as a survey of the hypothesis space or informative
questions from other models, weak models recognize and use them, closing
much of the gap to the strongest model.

These findings have a practical implication for autoresearch systems.
Since models can judge candidates they cannot produce, augmenting the
generation step is a direct way to improve performance without changing
the underlying model.

## Limitations

Our testbed consists of 200 machine learning papers from ICML 2026.
However, the game design itself is not specific to machine learning and
it applies to any field where a research problem and solution can be
extracted, such as drug design or materials science, but we have not
tested other domains. Recovery scores come from a panel of three LLM
judges, and the search trees are annotated by an LLM. Human evaluation
shows high agreement with the judges (91.2%;
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Evaluation ‣ 3 The Re-Search Game ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
but errors remain, particularly on papers whose solution is ambiguous or
has multiple equally valid formulations. Since three of the six
inquisitor models also serve as judges, we check for self-preference
bias and find it negligible (at most 0.08 points;
§[B](#A2 "Appendix B Judge Validation ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")),
though we cannot fully rule it out. Last but not least, due to knowledge
contamination, we choose the six recent models, yet unable to apply our
experiment setting on the newest models.

## Appendix C Survey Construction

All surveys are generated by GPT-5.6 Sol in three steps and then checked
by human annotators.

#### Blind survey.

For each paper, the model first writes a survey from the research
problem alone, without seeing the solution. It is told not to identify
or recall the paper and to write from general knowledge of the research
area, as a survey of the area would. Each survey has five to seven
dimensions along which solutions can differ, each with four to six
candidate approaches; each approach names a family of techniques and two
to five concrete techniques in it. The survey gives no recommendation or
ranking.

#### $`C_{\text{full}}`$ and $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$.

The model then sees the solution and writes the core idea as a
survey-level phrase of 3 to 12 words, that is, the name of a known
family of techniques rather than the paper’s own method. It picks the
candidate line where this phrase belongs. $`C_{\text{full}}`$ lists the
phrase in this line; $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ replaces it with a decoy, a
real technique from the same family that the paper does not use, of
similar length. Any other line that names the core idea is rewritten in
the same way in both versions, so that $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ never names
the core idea and $`C_{\text{full}}`$ names it only once.
$`C_{\text{full}}`$ and $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ thus differ in exactly one
line; both may still list other, non-core components of the paper.

#### $`C_{\text{dummy}}`$.

Starting from $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$, the model replaces every remaining
candidate that offers a component of the paper with an approach of
similar length that the paper does not use, and keeps the decoy.
Components already stated in the research problem are kept, since
removing them would not change what the inquisitor knows.

#### Human check.

Three annotators check the surveys of all 200 papers. For each paper,
they see the solution and the three surveys without their labels, and
decide which survey is $`C_{\text{full}}`$, $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$, and
$`C_{\text{dummy}}`$. All annotators identify $`C_{\text{full}}`$
correctly for every paper. For 7 papers (3.5%), one annotator swaps
$`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ and $`C_{\text{dummy}}`$; in these papers
$`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ lists few components besides the core idea, so
$`C_{\text{dummy}}`$ differs from it in only a few lines.

#### Length and freezing.

A survey is about 1,150 tokens long, and the three versions of a survey
differ by fewer than 40 tokens. All surveys were frozen before any game
was scored.

A model could raise its score under $`C_{\text{full}}`$ simply by
copying the included idea from the survey into its idea card. Three
checks on the survey experiment (200 papers, three games per paper,
question 100) speak against this. We detect mentions of the core idea
and the decoy with two LLM annotators (Gemma-4-31B and Qwen3.8-27B),
which read the questions and the final card of each game, mark the
questions that refer to each idea, and judge whether the card states it;
we count a mention only when both annotators mark it (Cohen’s $`\kappa`$
= 0.67–0.79).

#### The card writer does not see the survey.

The survey appears only in the question prompts. Before any question,
the idea card $`c_{0}`$ therefore scores the same under every condition
(Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ The card writer does not see the survey. ‣ Appendix C Survey Construction ‣ Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game")):
if the card writer saw the survey, $`C_{\text{full}}`$ cards would
already name the idea at question 0.

#### A rejected decoy rarely enters the card.

The survey does steer what models ask: a decoy in the survey
($`C_{\text{decoy}}`$) is asked about in 29–52% of games, against 7–19%
when it is not in the survey ($`C_{\text{full}}`$). But once rejected,
it reaches the final card in only 2–7% of games, while the included idea
under $`C_{\text{full}}`$ reaches it in 47–77%.

#### The included idea is not found by going through the survey.

With 100 questions, a model could also reach the included idea by asking
about the candidates in the survey one by one. Two comparisons speak
against this. First, the included idea is asked about early and
selectively. In the $`C_{\text{full}}`$ games that ask about it, the
first such question comes at a median of question 3 to 6. The core idea
in $`C_{\text{full}}`$ and the decoy in $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$ sit in the
same line of the survey, so going through the survey would reach both
equally often; instead, the core idea is asked about in 67–89% of games
and the decoy in 29–52%. Second, within $`C_{\text{full}}`$, the line
that names the core idea is asked about in 78–90% of games, against
45–67% for the lines within two positions of it, and in 63–73% against
22–42% within the first ten questions. To find which lines a question
asks about, the same two annotators see the survey and the questions but
not the solution ($`\kappa=0.80`$). Part of this preference already
appears under $`C_{\text{decoy}}`$, where the same line carries the
decoy (11 to 29 percentage points above nearby lines), since this line
is the one closest to the paper’s method. Models can therefore locate
the right family of methods without the name; naming the idea adds 4 to
15 percentage points on top, and mainly makes them ask about the idea
itself. All tests are paired over papers, with the three games of a
paper averaged first, and Holm-corrected across the six inquisitors. The
two comparisons above hold for every inquisitor ($`p<0.01`$); the extra
gain from naming is significant only for some inquisitors, so we report
only its size.

## Appendix E Information Bound for Adaptive Questioning

Write $`H_{t}=(Q_{1},A_{1},\ldots,Q_{t},A_{t})`$, with $`H_{0}`$ empty,
and let $`C`$ be the context given before the game (a survey, or nothing
under $`C_{\text{none}}`$). Assume a finite answer alphabet
$`\mathcal{A}`$ and a question policy satisfying
$`Q_{t}\perp S\mid(P,C,H_{t-1})`$. For example, this holds when each
question is generated from the visible inputs and history using fresh
randomness independent of the hidden solution. All logarithms below are
base two.

By the chain rule for conditional mutual information and the policy
assumption,

|     |                                      |                                                                   |             |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle I(S;H_{T}\mid P,C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(S;Q_{t},A_{t}\mid P,C,H_{t-1})`$  |             |
|     |                                      | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(S;A_{t}\mid P,C,H_{t-1},Q_{t})`$  |             |
|     |                                      | $`\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}H(A_{t}\mid P,C,H_{t-1},Q_{t})`$ |             |
|     |                                      | $`\displaystyle\leq T\log\_{2}                                    | \mathcal{A} | .`$ |     |

The last inequality follows because a variable taking at most
$`|\mathcal{A}|`$ values has entropy at most $`\log_{2}|\mathcal{A}|`$.
The result allows noisy answers and adaptive questions. It is an upper
bound, with no positive lower bound on the information supplied by an
additional question.

When a survey is supplied, its information is accounted for separately:

|     |                                      |                                             |             |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle I(S;C,H_{T}\mid P)`$ | $`\displaystyle=I(S;C\mid P)`$              |             |
|     |                                      | $`\displaystyle+I(S;H_{T}\mid P,C)`$        |             |
|     |                                      | $`\displaystyle\leq I(S;C\mid P)+T\log\_{2} | \mathcal{A} | .`$ |     |

A survey constructed using the solution may itself reveal information
about it. Excluding the survey from idea-card prompts restricts how this
information reaches the card, but does not remove it from the
questioning process.

You see only the visible research problem and the transcript so far. The

answerer’s Yes/No/Unknown replies. You do not see the hidden paper

work is sampled from a method-primary paper set. Treat the primary
contribution as method-like: a method, model, algorithm, framework,
training procedure, inference procedure, optimization procedure, attack,
defense, or system. Do not ask whether the primary contribution is a
benchmark, dataset, metric, survey, or analysis; those primary
contribution types are outside the candidate set. Datasets, benchmarks,
metrics, and analyses may still appear as evaluation or supporting
evidence, so ask about them only in that supporting/evaluation role. Ask
exactly one binary scientific-content question that tests a single
atomic predicate and will help infer the hidden scientific idea. Do not
combine contribution type with contrastive alternatives unless both
sides are necessary and mutually exclusive. Bad question:  
"Does the proposed solution focus on developing a benchmark or dataset

Better questions:  
"Is the proposed method an attack method for exposing
vulnerabilities?"  
"Is the proposed method a mitigation or defense method?"

safety benchmark?" Do not ask for names, acronyms, titles, authors,
venues, source URLs, or identity clues. Choose a question whose Yes and
No answers would separate those plausible hypotheses. Prefer questions
