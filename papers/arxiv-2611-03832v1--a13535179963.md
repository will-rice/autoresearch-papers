###### Abstract

Computational drug discovery offers a growing range of tools for
proposing and evaluating molecules, but combining them and deciding what
to do next still requires substantial expert effort. Here, we introduce
El Agente Fármaco, an AI scientist that uses modern llm (llm) technology
to turn discovery goals expressed in everyday language into complex,
scalable computational screening workflows. The agentic harness selects
scientific tools, reviews their results, and adjusts subsequent steps to
the target and the user’s objectives. Our approach centers on a shared
scientific memory: Fármaco Atlas, which stores molecules, structures,
and results together with their provenance—a record of how they were
obtained. The agent uses targeted database queries to retrieve the
evidence needed for each decision. A single request can launch
calculations for an entire collection of molecules, without requiring a
separate llm decision for every compound. This design limits the
information the model must process as screening collections grow, while
keeping the underlying results available for inspection and further
analysis. The integrated tools support molecule generation, prediction
of binding and molecular properties, and searches for compounds
available for purchase. In a retrospective comparison with an expert-led
campaign, the agent proposed chemically distinct candidates with
competitive docking scores and higher calculated drug-likeness under
explicitly imposed selection criteria. In a 22-task benchmark against
plain tool calls and Codex, the agent achieved the highest observed mean
model-judged score, with lower median cumulative token use and peak
context. Fármaco provides a foundation for flexible, scalable drug
discovery workflows that connect scientific reasoning with an enduring,
traceable record of computational work.

## 1 Introduction

Early-stage drug discovery involves identifying and evaluating candidate
therapeutics, including small molecules, peptides, and proteins
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\], as well as other modalities such as messenger RNA
(mRNA) vaccines. Over the past decade, the computational toolkit for
drug discovery has broadened considerably. Established approaches such
as molecular docking and binding free energy calculations
\[[2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5)\] have
been joined by a growing range of data-driven tools, including AlphaFold
for protein structure prediction \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] and Boltz-2 for
predicting protein–ligand complexes and binding affinities
\[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. The growing range of available tools
creates an opportunity to combine complementary evidence when proposing
and prioritizing compounds for experimental testing. However, advances
in individual methods do not automatically translate into more efficient
drug development: declining pharmaceutical research productivity
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\] and high clinical attrition \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]
highlight the continuing difficulty of selecting successful candidates.

Integrating computational tools into discovery workflows requires
deciding which calculations to perform, how to interpret their outputs,
and when to revise a candidate set. Medicinal chemistry optimization
requires balancing multiple molecular properties through successive
hit-to-lead and lead-optimization cycles \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Screening
funnels organize this process through successive selection and filtering
steps \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. In a predefined pipeline, the order of
calculations and filtering steps is specified in advance; changing that
sequence in response to intermediate results requires explicitly
implemented decision logic or manual intervention. The challenge is to
combine screening efficiency with the ability to revise the workflow
around the target, available evidence, and discovery objectives.

ai (ai) scientists are usually harnesses that package a complex array of
scientific tools alongside modern llm to achieve scientific goals. These
systems (harnesses) are often built on agentic architectures that are
familiar to everyday users. A great example of a “viral” agent is
[OpenClaw](https://openclaw.ai/), which attracted over 100,000 GitHub
stars within approximately two months of its creation
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. OpenClaw supports tasks such as communicating with
users through Telegram and managing their email and schedules. In
parallel, coding agents such as [Codex](https://openai.com/codex/),
[Claude Code](https://claude.com/product/claude-code), or
[Pi](https://pi.dev/) have taken the world by storm, enabling
programmers to develop complex software \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. In a
similar fashion, ai scientists are agentic systems that are specialized
to carry out scientific tasks instead (and often alongside) coding
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\].

ai agentic systems offer an opportunity to make these workflows more
adaptive by connecting scientific objectives expressed in natural
language to the selection and execution of computational tools. An agent
can use intermediate results to choose subsequent calculations, revise
candidate selections, and retrieve additional evidence as a discovery
task develops. This exploits the llm’s stochastic inference as a
centrepiece for flexible coordination, with specialized models and
simulation tools performing the underlying scientific calculations.
Previous work in our group has explored this approach in quantum
chemistry \[[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\], quantum simulations
\[[18](#bib.bib18)\], solid-state simulations \[[19](#bib.bib19)\],
atomistic simulations driven by machine-learning interatomic potentials
\[[20](#bib.bib20)\], and molecular structure editing
\[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. Related efforts have addressed structured
scientific execution \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], task-driven generation and
reuse of scientific tools \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], laboratory safety
planning and evaluation \[[24](#bib.bib24)\], and the orchestration of
optimization campaigns in self-driving laboratories
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

In drug discovery and related fields, existing systems apply this
approach at different levels, from assisting interactions with
individual scientific tools to coordinating multiple stages of a
discovery workflow. PyMolAI \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] integrates an agentic
assistant into PyMOL for molecular visualization and tool execution.
BioChemAIgent \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] extends the scope to structure-based
drug discovery through a chat interface connected to tools for molecular
analysis, protein modeling, and docking. AgentD \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]
organizes biomedical retrieval, molecular generation, property
prediction, iterative refinement, and protein--ligand structure
generation into a staged pipeline.¹¹ 1 Both BioChemAIgent and AgentD
expose capabilities through mcp (mcp) interfaces. BioChemAIgent’s [tool
registry](https://github.com/imsb-uke/bcai/blob/main/mcp/server.py)
includes library preparation, screening, and workflow storage and
execution. AgentD’s [documented
pipeline](https://github.com/hoon-ock/AgentD#pipeline-configuration)
uses configurable refinement iterations and candidate-selection
thresholds, saves results in run directories, and optionally generates
complexes with Boltz-2. Related scientific agents illustrate the breadth
of capabilities that can be coordinated in this way: MatSciAgent routes
natural-language requests to specialized agents for data retrieval,
structure generation, and physical simulation \[[29](#bib.bib29)\],
while TxAgent selects among 211 biomedical tools and iteratively
retrieves evidence during therapeutic reasoning \[[30](#bib.bib30)\].
Related commercial platforms and industry initiatives extend agentic
approaches across molecular design, scientific analysis, and research
support. [Boltz Lab](https://boltz.bio/boltzlab) provides agents for
small-molecule hit discovery and protein design, while [Claude
Science](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench)
provides a broader scientific workbench connecting agents to research
tools, data sources, and computing resources, including [access to the
Boltz api (api)](https://boltz.bio/boltz-claude). Insilico Medicine’s
[dora
(dora)](https://insilico.com/news/kv5e80yc41-insilico-open-sources-dora-on-github-sup)
supports literature synthesis and the drafting of scientific documents.
At the level of research infrastructure, the [Lilly–NVIDIA co-innovation
initiative](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-and-lilly-announce-co-innovation-lab-to-reinvent-drug-discovery-in-the-age-of-ai)
aims to connect computational modeling with automated experimentation in
a continuous learning system.

Across these approaches, connecting tools raises a shared architectural
question: how should scientific entities, results, and provenance be
represented and accessed as workflows grow or change? An agent needs to
retain the relationships between candidate compounds, calculations, and
observations across successive decisions, while managing tool
descriptions and retrieved evidence within a finite context window. For
example, a complete pdb (pdb) file can occupy substantial context even
when the agent needs only a summary of protein–ligand interactions or a
comparison between experimental and predicted structures. Separating
persistent scientific state from the llm’s working context allows
detailed records to remain available while the agent retrieves the
evidence needed for the current decision.

Here, we present El Agente Fármaco, an agentic system that combines
drug-discovery capabilities spanning ligand generation to free energy
calculation with Fármaco Atlas, a persistent database of scientific
entities and workflow provenance
([Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Scientific tools operate on database subsets, and the agent receives
compact run summaries from which it can request detailed results as
needed. This design connects workflow planning, execution, and
subsequent analysis through a shared scientific state, while selectively
exposing data and tool parameters to the llm. We describe the database
and tool-execution architecture and evaluate the system through a
22-task benchmark and five computational case studies. These cover
retrospective ligand prioritization, adaptive and prospective candidate
selection, binding-pose assessment, and relative binding free energy
calculations and analogue design.

## 2 A drug design-native database accessible to an agent

At the center of El Agente Fármaco is the Fármaco Atlas, a
SQLModel-based \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] persistent database that stores all
scientific entities, results, artifacts, and workflow state in a unified
relational schema.

This design builds on earlier approaches to storing scientific state
outside the conversation. El Agente persisted information in files
\[[16](#bib.bib16)\], while El Agente Gráfico used a
knowledge-graph-backed Python state for structured access during
agent-steered workflows \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. Files can store structured
data, but a shared schema, query interface, and explicit provenance
relationships must be provided by the surrounding system. Fármaco Atlas
supplies these through a relational database that persists beyond
individual chats and restarts, with large artifacts kept in registered
external files.²² 2 Complementary memory mechanisms include semantic
retrieval of embedded documents or summaries \[[32](#bib.bib32)\] and
event logs recording actions and observations. These mechanisms support
different access patterns: semantic retrieval helps locate relevant
text, while event logs preserve execution history; neither alone defines
the relational schema used here for scientific entities and metrics.

The core database records comprise:

- •
  Protein and ligand entities: Protein targets and ligands defined by
  smiles (smiles) strings define the primary biological and chemical
  objects represented in the database.
- •
  Structures: Materialized structures capture concrete three-dimensional
  objects, including predicted protein structures, docked poses,
  protein–ligand complexes, conformers, and derived structural models.
- •
  Workflow provenance: Tool workflow runs record input parameters,
  detailed results, and generated artifacts, showing which computational
  action was executed, which inputs it used, and what outputs it
  produced.
- •
  Metrics: Normalized metrics store heterogeneous tool outputs as
  queryable scientific observations, including scalar, categorical, and
  Boolean values.
- •
  Subsets: Persistent subsets define reusable collections of ligands,
  targets, structures, or protein–ligand pairs, allowing agents to carry
  curated selections across multi-step workflows.

##### Data ingestion.

User-provided csv (csv) datasets can enter Fármaco Atlas through a
dedicated data_ingestion_expert, which inspects columns and sample rows
before mapping selected fields to target and ligand records and typed
metrics. The import records its source filename in a run, creates
reusable subsets of targets, ligands, and protein–ligand pairs, and
commits the batch transactionally. Separately, externally supplied
protein or ligand structure files can be registered with existing target
or ligand records, allowing downstream tools to use experimental or
refined structures without requiring a public pdb accession. Mapping and
validation details are provided in
[Sec. A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Dataset and structure ingestion ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

##### Subset-based execution.

In Fármaco, scientific workflows operate on predefined database subsets
(see
[Fig. 2](#S2.F2 "In Disclosure of tool parameters. ‣ 2.1 Progressive disclosure of context and tools ‣ 2 A drug design-native database accessible to an agent ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
For example, a workflow screening 200 ligands for admet (admet)
properties receives a single subset_id referencing the collection,
without requiring the agent to include 200 smiles strings in the tool
call. The same mechanism applies to targets, structures, and
protein–ligand pairs.

### 2.1 Progressive disclosure of context and tools

Progressive disclosure treats context retrieval as a query-planning
problem: the agent requests the information needed for the current
reasoning step.³³ 3 Related patterns include incremental retrieval
through [GraphQL cursor
connections](https://graphql.org/learn/pagination/); field selection,
relationship expansion, filtering, and pagination through [OData query
options](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/odata/concepts/queryoptions-overview);
and field-restricted previews of top-ranked records within result groups
using [Elasticsearch top hits
aggregations](https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/aggregations/search-aggregations-metrics-top-hits-aggregation).
For agents, Anthropic describes retaining lightweight identifiers and
loading details on demand as a just-in-time context strategy using
[progressive
disclosure](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents).
In Fármaco, this principle governs both access to scientific data and
exposure of tool parameters.

##### Disclosure of scientific data.

Subset identifiers reference the collections selected for processing,
while run summaries identify the stored results and fields available for
querying. Generated metrics, structures, and artifacts are stored in
Fármaco Atlas or in files registered in the database. The agent
retrieves detailed records through targeted sql (sql) queries and
accesses registered artifacts as needed for subsequent reasoning. The
query interface enforces read-only access and bounded row previews,
rejects broad SELECT \* projections, and reports when results have been
truncated. Oversized previews are shortened before being returned, while
file-reading tools likewise limit the text exposed to the agent
([Sec. A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Fármaco Atlas retains the persistent scientific state while the working
context contains the selected evidence needed for the current task.

##### Disclosure of tool parameters.

Fármaco receives a list of available tools together with high-level
descriptions of their capabilities. Detailed tool parameters are
disclosed to a separate configuration node only after a tool has been
selected. Separating tool configuration introduces an additional llm
call; this design anticipates a substantially larger tool ecosystem than
the one currently implemented. A tool-execution workflow takes only the
requested tool name, a high-level description of the task, such as
“predict aqueous solubilities,” and a description of the target
entities, such as “the 50 ligands with the highest binding affinity.” If
the requested tool requires database entities as input, the first step
is a separate subgraph. This subgraph starts with an llm-backed agent
node that translates the high-level entity description into an sql query
command (see
[Figs. 2](#S2.F2 "In Disclosure of tool parameters. ‣ 2.1 Progressive disclosure of context and tools ‣ 2 A drug design-native database accessible to an agent ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery"),
[7](#A1.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
and [8](#A1.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
After the sql command executes, the workflow verifies that only
subset-native entity IDs are returned, then creates a subset entity from
the resulting ID collection. The tool name and high-level task
description are then passed to another llm-backed agent node. At this
stage, the llm receives the relevant calculation settings and fills in
run-specific parameters. The workflow then resolves each subset into the
corresponding database entities, for example, 50 ligands, and dispatches
their tool calls with bounded concurrency without further llm
interaction. After all tool calls have completed, the workflow records
their outputs and returns a run summary, completing the data-disclosure
cycle described above. Together, these mechanisms limit the tool
specifications and scientific data entering the llm context, reducing
token usage and keeping the available evidence focused on the scientific
decision at hand. Detailed execution and failure policies are described
in
[Sec. A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

### 2.2 Drug discovery tools

The integrated tools cover target and structure preparation, pocket
detection, ligand generation and comparison, pose and affinity
prediction, structural analysis and validation, free energy
calculations, developability assessment, and compound sourcing
([Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Data-retrieval and format-conversion utilities connect these
capabilities. Individual tool descriptions and integration details are
provided in
[appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Integrated scientific tools ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

Tools are deployed through hosted api, containerized services, or local
Python and command-line interfaces. Hosted
[nim](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-nim-offers-optimized-inference-microservices-for-deploying-ai-models-at-scale/)
provide remote model inference, while dedicated Docker containers expose
other tools through FastAPI services to isolate dependencies. Tools with
manageable dependencies run in the agent environment, with Python
wrappers providing access to command-line programs. For remote compute
jobs, a shared client manages submission, input and output artifacts,
waiting, and cancellation
([Sec. A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Remote compute jobs ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

Fármaco consists of one main agent and two specialist subagents. The
main agent, farmaco_expert, serves as the user entry point and
coordinates structure prediction, docking, ligand generation, and
developability workflows. It delegates pocket characterization to
pocket_investigation_expert, which combines complementary pocket-finding
methods into a single report, and dataset import to
data_ingestion_expert, which inspects user-provided datasets and maps
selected fields to targets, ligands, and typed metrics. Broad workflow
planning thus remains centralized while these specialized tasks are
delegated to the two subagents.

## 3 Multi-task benchmark

We compared El Agente Fármaco with its database and workflow interface,
plain tool calls, and Codex across 22 tasks covering molecular
filtering, conformer generation, sequence and structure analysis, ligand
generation, binding-pose assessment, and synthesis-route exploration.
Each configuration ran three repetitions per task with the same
scientific instructions and supplied input files, using gpt-6-sol
throughout. Each result was assessed in three judge sessions using
gpt-6.1-sol, giving 198 agent runs and 594 judge assessments.

[Figure 3](#S3.F3 "In 3 Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
highlights the balance between judged task quality, context use, and
model cost. Fármaco achieved the highest mean judge score observed. Its
smaller median peak context and lower cumulative token use are
consistent with the design goal of keeping detailed scientific state
outside the conversation while retrieving selected evidence for each
decision. Its median model cost was lower than Codex. Plain tool calls
had the lowest median total model cost and latency, highlighting that
reduced context use does not necessarily yield the cheapest or fastest
execution. Median cached input shares were 90.6% for plain tool calls
and 91.8% for Codex, compared with 82.2% for Fármaco
([Tab. 15](#A4.T15 "In D.6 Aggregate results ‣ Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
High cache reuse moderates the cost of repeatedly processing
conversation context and helps explain why Codex’s substantially higher
token use did not lead to a proportional increase in cost. We selected
manageable tasks for repeated execution and assessment, leaving
long-running, large-library discovery campaigns outside the benchmark’s
scope. This task selection likely underestimates the benefits of
subset-based execution for screening thousands or hundreds of thousands
of ligands, where per-ligand outputs accumulate in context. An
additional experiment extended the STK17B⁴⁴ 4 Serine/threonine kinase
17B. ranking task from 48 to 256 ligands to examine execution at a
larger scale
([Sec. D.9](#A4.SS9 "D.9 Additional STK17B ranking experiment at larger scale ‣ Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

These results suggest a continuing role for domain-specific agents
alongside general-purpose coding agents. A further consideration, only
partly captured by numerical benchmarks, is the reliability supported by
established scientific tools and tested workflows. Established
implementations with documented methods and tested behaviour provide a
stronger basis for trust than ad hoc scripts generated during an
individual run. Task definitions, judging methods, total model costs,
and per-task results are provided in
[appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").
A worked example of shape and pharmacophore comparison is provided in
[Sec. D.8](#A4.SS8 "D.8 Worked example: shape and pharmacophore comparison ‣ Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

## 4 Case studies

The following case studies evaluate El Agente Fármaco through
discovery-oriented campaigns and technical workflow benchmarks. For
TBXT⁵⁵ 5 T-box transcription factor T, also known as brachyury.,
compounds proposed by the agent are compared with those selected and
purchased in an independent expert-led campaign using a common docking
protocol and molecular property assessments. For PGK2, with PGK1 as a
counter-target⁶⁶ 6 PGK2 and PGK1 denote phosphoglycerate kinase 2 and 1,
respectively., we examine a workflow that iterates generation and
evaluation until a specified number of candidates meet criteria for
molecular properties, pocket interactions, and predicted score
differences between targets. Further case studies examine hit enrichment
and pose recovery for AmpC⁷⁷ 7 AmpC $`\beta`$-lactamase. and candidate
selection for KpThiL⁸⁸ 8 Thiamine-monophosphate kinase from Klebsiella
pneumoniae.. The final case study examines free energy calculations and
analogue design for TYK2⁹⁹ 9 Tyrosine kinase 2..

### 4.1 Retrospective TBXT ligand prioritization

TBXT is a lineage-survival driver in chordoma and contains a shallow
pocket, denoted A^(′), that has recently been shown to accommodate
small-molecule ligands \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]. Before this study, team
members independently completed a structure-based campaign against this
pocket and purchased 40 compounds for experimental evaluation.
Structures were available for 39 of these compounds; one compound was no
longer listed in the [ChemDiv](https://www.chemdiv.com/) catalogue and
was therefore excluded from further analysis. These 39 compounds
provided a computational reference set selected without Fármaco.

The initial prompt asked Fármaco to prepare the target and pocket,
generate 700–1,000 molecular samples, and prioritize candidates for
catalogue matching
([Sec. C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 TBXT retrospective comparison ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
The workflow produced 705 ligand records.

RDKit returned molecular descriptors for 693 of the 705 generated ligand
records; the remaining 12 were flagged as invalid and excluded from
downstream selection. The agent combined molecular descriptors,
structural-alert screening, and admet predictions to filter compounds by
physicochemical properties, pains (pains) and Brenk alerts, and
predicted hERG and Ames risks, retaining 59 compounds. Fármaco then
organized these compounds into 20 descriptor-based clusters and used
Boltz-2 predictions to select up to two compounds per cluster, yielding
34 representatives. Finally, it searched the ChemDiv and
[Enamine](https://enamine.net/) catalogues, returning matches for 20 and
24 of the 34 representatives, respectively; the 0.7 similarity cutoff
was specified for ChemDiv.

For a common retrospective comparison, the 34 generated representatives
selected by the agent (not the catalogue matches), the 39 purchased
compounds, and five published reference ligands were evaluated with the
same docking procedure against pdb 7ZKF. This redocking was carried out
outside Fármaco using AutoDock Vina \[[34](#bib.bib34)\], applied
uniformly to all three sets so that no set inherited a score from its
own pipeline.¹⁰¹⁰ 10 AutoDock Vina is not among the tools integrated
into Fármaco; the comparison therefore evaluates the compounds Fármaco
proposed, not a docking capability of the system itself. Preparation
settings and software versions are given in
[Sec. C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 TBXT retrospective comparison ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

### 4.2 Adaptive multistage selection of PGK2 ligand candidates using PGK1 as a counter-target

We evaluated an adaptive candidate-selection workflow targeting human
PGK2 with PGK1 as a counter-target
([Sec. C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 PGK2/PGK1 adaptive selection ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
The prompt specified sequential criteria and an adaptive rule that
repeated generation and evaluation within predefined limits until at
least five candidates were obtained. Across two search cycles, Fármaco
conducted four PocketXMol generations and registered 369 molecules
before meeting the stopping condition. In cycle 1, three candidates were
selected from 181 generated molecules according to the prespecified
relative-score criterion; cycle 2 added two candidates from another 188
molecules. Thus, the adaptive iteration rule determined the search scale
rather than a fixed initial library size.

In Stage 1, PocketXMol \[[35](#bib.bib35)\] generated molecules using
the specified PGK2 pocket center and radius. pains screening, qed and
synthetic accessibility thresholds, Lipinski’s rule-of-five, and related
filters were then applied, yielding 76 of 369 molecules that met these
criteria. The passing molecules were docked against PGK2 using DiffDock
\[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\], and protein–ligand complexes
were constructed from the rank-1 poses. plip (plip) \[[38](#bib.bib38)\]
was subsequently used to evaluate hydrogen-bond or hydrophobic
interactions with the prespecified pocket residue tyrosine (TYR) 242. Of
the 76 molecules, 58 satisfied the TYR242 interaction criterion and
advanced to Stage 2
([Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "In 4.2 Adaptive multistage selection of PGK2 ligand candidates using PGK1 as a counter-target ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")a).

In Stage 2, the 58 ligands passing Stage 1 were independently evaluated
against PGK2 and PGK1 by Boltz-2 co-folding, together with DiffDock
calculations against PGK1. The prespecified threshold required the PGK2
minus PGK1 Boltz-2 affinity-score difference to be at least 0.477. Five
molecules met this threshold, with model-derived differences ranging
from 0.501 to 1.076
([Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "In 4.2 Adaptive multistage selection of PGK2 ligand candidates using PGK1 as a counter-target ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")b
and
[Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Adaptive multistage selection of PGK2 ligand candidates using PGK1 as a counter-target ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
In their Stage 1 PGK2 poses, three of these molecules formed hydrogen
bonds with TYR242 and two formed hydrophobic interactions. admet-ai
predictions were subsequently obtained for these five molecules. For the
five selected candidates, predicted PGK2 binding probabilities ranged
from 0.172 to 0.371.

### 4.3 Retrospective AmpC $`\beta`$-lactamase virtual-screening benchmark

The two preceding case studies evaluate Fármaco against an expert-led
campaign and in a prospective pilot, in both cases without experimental
outcomes for the proposed compounds. We therefore also evaluated a
retrospective workflow using published screening outcomes and
experimental structures. Liu et al. docked a 1.7-billion-molecule
make-on-demand library against AmpC $`\beta`$-lactamase, tested 1,521
compounds experimentally, and solved co-crystal structures for four hits
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\]. The released dataset contains molecular
structures, original DOCK scores and global ranks, similarity
coefficients, compound-selection metadata, and experimental outcomes,
enabling assessment of enrichment and pose recovery against experimental
references. Eight scripted prompts and two follow-up questions guided
Fármaco through dataset analysis, retrospective compound selection,
pocket detection, structural comparison, admet triage, and molecular
generation in a single conversation.

For the docking task, Fármaco selected the 50 compounds with the best
original global docking ranks from the 1,521 experimentally tested
compounds. The four available co-crystal structures provided
experimental references for assessing binding-pocket identification and
ligand-pose recovery. Fármaco delegated pocket investigation to its
specialized subagent, which used P2Rank \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] and
Pocketeer \[[41](#bib.bib41)\] to locate the catalytic cleft in the
receptor and co-crystal structures. It then docked the four co-crystal
ligands into the 1L2S-derived receptor with DiffDock and compared their
poses and interaction patterns with the experimental structures. Docking
did not consistently recover the experimental poses or interaction
patterns
([Tab. 8](#A3.T8 "In C.3.3 Pocket detection and structural comparison ‣ C.3 AmpC retrospective benchmark ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

In a follow-up prompt, Fármaco used admet-ai \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] to
assess developability across all 298 compounds with quantified
experimental $`K_{\mathrm{i}}`$ values, which we count as hits. Of the
298 hits, 215 passed the pains/Brenk structural-alert screen, and 71
returned an exact match in the queried Enamine REAL catalogue. Predicted
dili (dili) risk was high for most hits and remained a concern even
among compounds combining activity, clean structural-alert profiles, and
catalogue availability.

The next prompt asked Fármaco to propose analogues of the strongest
experimental hits, aiming to retain similarity to active compounds while
improving predicted developability. The resulting candidates were
assessed using molecular properties, structural alerts, and admet
predictions. The comparison included 80 MolMIM candidates
\[[43](#bib.bib43)\], 25 GenMol candidates \[[44](#bib.bib44)\], 87
PocketXMol candidates \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], and 20 distinct PrexSyn
analogues \[[45](#bib.bib45)\].
[Figure 6](#S4.F6 "In 4.3 Retrospective AmpC 𝛽-lactamase virtual-screening benchmark ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")a
summarizes the properties and predicted toxicity profiles of the 212
candidates analyzed.

To assess predicted binding, a subsequent request directed Fármaco to
submit a ten-compound shortlist drawn from the generated sets for
Boltz-2 co-folding and affinity prediction against the 1L2S sequence
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\]; all ten predictions were completed. The predicted
$`\mathrm{pIC}_{50}`$ values span 3.72 to 5.46, with the two PrexSyn
analogues and one PocketXMol sample ranked highest. The returned binder
probabilities range from 0.160 to 0.410
([Fig. 6](#S4.F6 "In 4.3 Retrospective AmpC 𝛽-lactamase virtual-screening benchmark ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")b).
Six of the ten compounds come from MolMIM, two from PrexSyn, and two
from PocketXMol.

To assess the contribution of database-supported execution, we repeated
the same ten prompts using the plain-tool-call configuration
([Sec. 3](#S3 "3 Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Including the successful Boltz-2 step in both versions, the
plain-tool-call configuration was less expensive (USD 14.74 against
17.66) and faster (1.43 against 2.97 h). For the original ten turns, the
plain-tool-call configuration issued 103 model calls against 303: the
database configuration spends a separate call on each query, workflow
invocation, and schema or subset description. The per-turn comparison
shows where the database reduced or increased costs. The database
configuration was cheaper on four of the original ten turns. Three of
these involved bulk tabular data: retrospective selection over 1,521
compounds (USD 0.78 against 2.03), pocket detection (1.07 against 1.22),
and admet triage over 298 hits (2.42 against 2.62). It was more
expensive on the turns dominated by many small exploratory steps, most
clearly the analogue-search follow-up (3.76 against 1.09, 63 model calls
against five). Holding tabular state in a queried store rather than in
context therefore pays whenever a turn reasons over a large record set,
and costs whenever a turn is a long sequence of individually small
operations. Detailed methods, score definitions, and per-turn results
are provided in
[Sec. C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 AmpC retrospective benchmark ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

### 4.4 Dimeric KpThiL Prospective Ligand Selection

KpThiL is a ThiL enzyme from Klebsiella pneumoniae, represented here by
a newly refined experimental structure not yet deposited in the pdb. The
enzyme is a homodimer comprising chains A and B, each containing one
non-covalently bound tps (tps) molecule. The second substrate, atp
(atp), was not resolved in the experimental density. Inhibitors of
Pseudomonas aeruginosa ThiL have been reported \[[46](#bib.bib46),
[47](#bib.bib47)\]. No ligand-screening dataset currently exists for
this target.

In this context, Fármaco proposed a _ranked, prospective test set of
purchasable small molecules_ for KpThiL. Because no prior binding data
are available, this represents a genuinely _ab initio_ ligand-selection
problem. Accordingly, Fármaco reasoned directly from the experimental
structure, first defining the substrate-binding pocket and then
selecting candidate compounds. Fármaco compared the tps-contacting
residues in the two sites and selected chain A as representative,
retaining the full dimer to account for partner-chain contacts. P2Rank
and Pocketeer identified pockets overlapping the tps-binding region.

Starting from substrate-analogue seeds comprising thiamine, thiamine
monophosphate, pyrithiamine, and oxythiamine, Fármaco queried the
Enamine and ChemDiv catalogues by similarity, yielding 189 raw hits. It
then expanded the chemical space using a MolMIM generative sampler to
produce 40 qed-optimised molecules, whose Enamine similarity searches
returned another 736 hits, bringing the aggregate to 925 raw supplier
hits across the searches. From the pooled candidates, Fármaco selected a
ranked, prospective test set of 50 purchasable Enamine-catalogue
compounds with distinct recorded smiles. All 50 selected compounds
passed pains structural-alert screening and had valid RDKit descriptors.

In a follow-up session, Fármaco generated ten molecules with PocketXMol
using the experimentally defined tps pocket. All ten passed pains
screening and PoseBusters molecular-geometry checks and had valid RDKit
descriptors. PrexSyn searches succeeded for nine generated molecules,
returning 45 analogue hits with proposed synthesis routes.

A subsequent standalone follow-up reassessed all 105 candidates (the 50
catalogue compounds, ten PocketXMol molecules, and 45 PrexSyn hits)
using Boltz-2 and admet-ai. The agent aligned the predicted monomeric
complexes to experimental chain A and assessed ligand placement against
the retained dimer. Nine poses met the tps-pocket proximity/contact
heuristic, three of which had more than three ligand heavy atoms in
potential clash with the experimental protein (distances below 2 Å). The
highest converted affinity score, $`\mathrm{pIC}_{50}=6.66`$, belonged
to a pose whose ligand centroid lay approximately 27 Å from the tps
centroid, illustrating why candidate prioritization considered both
predicted affinity and placement in the tps-binding pocket. The
resulting heuristic ranking placed a tps-like catalogue reference first;
the highest-ranked novel generated molecule had predicted
$`\mathrm{pIC}_{50}=5.50`$, but a binder probability of 0.152 and high
predicted hERG and dili scores. The agent recommended a tps-like
catalogue compound and a related non-phosphorylated comparator for
initial biochemical testing. For follow-up, it proposed a nitrogen-rich
polycyclic lactam generated by PocketXMol and, optionally, a PrexSyn
analogue containing quinoline and piperazine rings, subject to synthesis
or confirmed availability. Detailed methods and results are provided in
[Sec. C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 KpThiL binding-site analysis, generation, and affinity/ADMET follow-up ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery").

### 4.5 Relative binding free energies for TYK2

We evaluated Fármaco on rbfe (rbfe) calculations and analogue design for
a congeneric TYK2 series of 4-aminopyridine benzamides
\[[48](#bib.bib48), [49](#bib.bib49)\]. The first benchmark session
tested a fluorine-to-chlorine substitution on a cis-cyclopropane, and
the second examined network design for 13 ligands. Both sessions used
the protein structure and bound ligand poses of a public free-energy
benchmark set \[[50](#bib.bib50), [5](#bib.bib5)\]. For these sessions,
published $`K_{\mathrm{i}}`$ values were withheld from the agent and are
used here only for retrospective comparison. Calculations were performed
on the Azulene Studio platform provided by [Azulene
Labs](https://www.azulenelabs.com/). The agent submitted calculations as
jobs, recorded them in Fármaco Atlas, and retrieved the results in later
user turns
([appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Integrated scientific tools ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

#### 4.5.1 Network design and initial calculations

Fármaco was asked to plan an rbfe network for 13 ligands. The agent
computed pairwise fingerprint similarities, maximum common
substructures, common-core deviations between the supplied poses, and
protein–ligand clashes. From these, it proposed a 12-edge spanning tree
with seven additional cycle-closure edges. The proposal separated
computed metrics from qualitative judgement. The agent identified four
potentially challenging transformation types: the bridge between acyclic
and cyclopropyl amides, ring-size changes, polar linker changes to urea
and carbamate, and the stereochemistry of substituted cyclopropanes.
Nine of its 19 edges also appear in an 18-edge lomap (lomap) reference
network \[[51](#bib.bib51)\] that was not provided to the agent. Asked
to submit “the first 6 most interesting calculations”, the agent
submitted the first six edges of its table, a star around EJM42. Each
calculation used 10 ps of production per replica. A single collection
call in the next turn returned five results and one failure
([Tab. 3](#S4.T3 "In 4.5.1 Network design and initial calculations ‣ 4.5 Relative binding free energies for TYK2 ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Against the experiment, the five completed edges have a mean unsigned
error of 1.12 kcal mol⁻¹ and four of five correct signs.

## 5 Discussion

El Agente Fármaco brings together adaptive workflow coordination and
persistent scientific state for early-stage drug discovery. In the
22-task benchmark, the database-backed configuration combined the
smallest median peak context with model costs close to those of plain
tool calls and about half those of Codex. The case studies showed how
the agent combined scientific tools with persistent scientific state in
Fármaco Atlas to address early-stage drug discovery tasks. Together,
these results illustrate how a domain-specific harness can support
token-efficient scientific workflows while reliably storing scientific
state with traceable provenance.

The capabilities of an agentic ai system depend on three factors, each
shaped by different stakeholders: i) the capability of the underlying
llm as a reasoning and decision-making engine; ii) access to
computational methods and software, together with their capabilities and
limitations, such as compound generation and the accuracy of co-folding
and docking for early screening; and iii) the support provided by the
harness in which the llm operates. For the first factor, an open
question is whether fine-tuning or training models from scratch for
domains such as drug discovery offers advantages over general-purpose
frontier models with broad training data and cross-domain reasoning
capabilities. The second factor largely depends on progress in
open-source initiatives and models.

For the third factor, we expect future systems to combine the
flexibility of agent platforms such as [OpenClaw](https://openclaw.ai/)
and coding agents such as [Claude
Code](https://claude.com/product/claude-code) and
[Codex](https://openai.com/codex/) with domain-specific infrastructure
such as Fármaco. This infrastructure can improve reliability through
established scientific workflows and persistent, structured storage of
scientific state and its provenance. Tool interfaces should also expose
assumptions, limitations, and informative error feedback, as illustrated
by the AmpC example
([Sec. C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 AmpC retrospective benchmark ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Opaque interfaces can restrict an agent’s ability to investigate and
assess results. In such cases, more flexible architectures that let
agents inspect code, data, and execution environments may better
diagnose and resolve problems. The limiting factor may be access to
computational context, such as why a protein was prepared in a
particular protonation state, even when the model has the scientific
knowledge needed to assess its implications. This is consistent with the
broader argument that agent performance can be constrained by the
execution harness and access to essential information, even when models
have the capabilities needed to solve the task \[[53](#bib.bib53),
[54](#bib.bib54)\]. We view current architectures, including Fármaco, as
intermediate steps towards this broader integration.

Agentic ai will make conventional workflows and data pipelines in fields
such as early-stage drug discovery significantly more adaptive and
customizable. However, integrating a broad range of in silico tools to
identify potential hit or lead compounds is only the first step. Agentic
solutions will realize their full value only when they also incorporate
on-demand experimental data. In drug discovery, this means coupling
Fármaco to automated robots for chemical synthesis and biological
assays. The reasoning agent could then assess the correlation between in
silico predictions and experimental data and suggest modifications to
candidate compounds. Approaches such as Bayesian optimization
\[[55](#bib.bib55), [25](#bib.bib25)\] or active learning
\[[56](#bib.bib56)\] could use these experimental results to guide
compound selection for subsequent testing. Together with virtual
screening, this would close the loop between computational predictions
and experimental feedback. As experimental data accumulate, they could
refine the predictive models guiding these methods, creating a data
flywheel that supports better compound proposals in subsequent rounds.

Closing this loop could, in turn, change how medicinal and computational
chemists contribute to discovery. Medicinal chemists could focus on
overarching design decisions and provide the system with additional
domain- or entity-specific constraints and guidance. They would no
longer need to do the hands-on work of virtually screening a class of
compounds using docking and co-folding methods, checking whether binding
poses align, or ensuring compounds meet structural requirements such as
passing pains filters. In our longer-term vision, identifying candidate
compounds for animal or human studies would become increasingly
automatable, shifting the main bottlenecks towards preclinical and
clinical evaluation and regulatory approval. The roles of medicinal and
computational chemists in drug discovery would merge, with a deep
understanding of interaction mechanisms and downstream constraints on
molecular structures becoming much more relevant than conducting
simulations.

As increasingly capable agentic systems automate computational
execution, human expertise will centre on framing questions, defining
domain-specific requirements, and interpreting results. The
human-in-the-loop would act as a verifier, assessing whether the results
support the conclusions drawn and what scientific or practical
consequences follow.

Our team is working towards the release of the entire family of El
Agente tools in a scalable platform for the general public to employ. We
hope that this action will help democratize access as well as help many
people make advances towards the cure of diseases that plague humanity,
especially those in the neglected disease category \[[57](#bib.bib57),
[58](#bib.bib58)\].

## Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure

### A.1 Dataset and structure ingestion

The ingestion specialist constructs a mapping from database field names
to selected csv columns after inspecting the file schema and
representative rows. Supported entity fields include target names, pdb
accessions, protein sequences, ligand names, and ligand smiles; metric
mappings specify floating-point, string, or Boolean values. The importer
validates mappings and values before writing, creates a csv_import run
recording the source filename, and associates imported metrics with that
run and the relevant entities. It creates ordered subsets of referenced
targets, ligands, and protein–ligand pairs, and commits the import in
one database transaction; an exception rolls back the batch. The
returned summary reports inserted entities and metrics together with the
subset identifiers.

The structure-import interface registers an existing workspace file as a
Structure record with a kind, format, and path, linked to an existing
target, ligand, or both. This makes externally supplied or refined
structures available to workflows consuming persisted structures. Such
imports do not receive a fabricated computational source run.

### A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies

The workflow execution graph is implemented with Pydantic Graph. It
implements selection, parameterization, dispatch, execution, result
persistence, and summary generation as explicit steps.
[Figures 7](#A1.F7 "In A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
and [8](#A1.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ A.2 Validation, bounded retrieval, and execution policies ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
show its execution paths and the nested subset-selection graph. Its
subset-selection subgraph checks the requested subject category, exact
ID-column aliases, entity existence, and, where applicable,
compatibility of ordered structure pairs. sql execution or
selection-validation errors are returned to the selection agent, with a
maximum of three selection attempts. Tool parameters are generated
against a Pydantic model and validated before dispatch, including
tool-specific semantic checks. Resolved inputs undergo a further
preflight check before retryable execution.

Agent-facing sql queries execute in read-only mode and return previews
with selected column names, row counts, and truncation metadata. The
default preview contains at most 20 rows, with a maximum requested limit
of 100, regardless of whether the query itself contains a LIMIT clause.
Broad SELECT \* projections are rejected. The public query wrapper
further removes preview rows when the serialized response exceeds 20,000
characters and advises narrower retrieval. These preview limits do not
truncate the internal ID query used to construct a workflow subset.
File-reading tools return bounded text, and the general file reader
rejects protein-structure formats such as pdb and mmcif (mmcif) in favor
of structure-specific tools.

The execution graph resolves and runs each subject independently and
collects its outcome at a join step. The default worker policy permits
eight concurrent jobs and two retries after the initial attempt, with
exponential backoff; tools may override the concurrency limit and
execution timeout. A returned ok=False outcome is terminal, whereas
operational exceptions enter the retry path. Resolution and preflight
failures are recorded without execution retries. Failure records retain
the run, subject, stage, error type and message, and attempt count.
Successful raw results are persisted before tool-specific
materialization creates normalized metrics or derived entities.
Materialization failures produce separate failure records while
preserving the raw output and allowing later results to be processed.
The run summary counts execution outcomes; materialization failures are
recorded separately and therefore require inspection when assessing
whether all derived records were created.

### A.3 Remote compute jobs

PocketXMol and the optional PrexSyn compute backend use a shared
asynchronous client to submit finite jobs with explicit inputs, resource
timeouts, and required output artifacts. The client waits for a
successful terminal state and confirmed output readiness before
retrieving results. Idempotency keys incorporate the workflow run, tool,
subject, parameters, and workspace to identify repeated submissions. If
waiting fails or is cancelled, the client attempts to cancel the remote
job and confirm its terminal state; a subsequent attempt after timeout
or confirmed abandonment receives a fresh submission identity. This
manages the normal job lifecycle but does not guarantee recovery from
abrupt process termination or cancellation before submission returns a
job identifier.

## Appendix B Integrated scientific tools

The following catalogue describes the scientific methods and their
integration into El Agente Fármaco, grouped by role in the discovery
workflow
([Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
Boltz-2, OpenFold2, OpenFold3, MolMIM, GenMol, and DiffDock are accessed
as nim, hosted by NVIDIA.¹²¹² 12
[https://build.nvidia.com/models?filters=usecase%3Ausecase_drug_discovery](https://build.nvidia.com/models?filters=usecase%3Ausecase_drug_discovery),

### B.1 Target structures and sequence alignments

##### OpenFold2

OpenFold2 is an open-source implementation and extension of
AlphaFold-style protein structure prediction models
\[[59](#bib.bib59)\]. It reproduces the core architecture of AlphaFold2
while enabling greater transparency, extensibility, and integration with
custom training pipelines.

##### OpenFold3

OpenFold3 \[[60](#bib.bib60), [61](#bib.bib61)\] extends the AlphaFold
paradigm to model more complex biomolecular systems, including
protein–protein, protein–nucleic acid, and protein–ligand interactions.
Inspired by AlphaFold3-style architectures, it integrates multimodal
structural inputs and expanded training data to support prediction of
biomolecular complexes, enabling broader applications in structural
biology and drug discovery.

##### Rowan protein preparation

[Rowan](https://docs.rowansci.com/workflows/protein-preparation)
\[[52](#bib.bib52)\] prepares receptor structures for ligand generation,
docking, and binding free-energy calculations. The integrated
rowan_prepare_protein tool supports repair of missing atoms and
residues, terminal capping, protonation at a specified pH, and
hydrogen-position optimization. The integration defaults to PDBFixer
repair, ACE/NME terminal caps, and OpenMM protonation at pH 7.4
\[[62](#bib.bib62)\]. Chain selection and retention of waters, ions, and
cofactors are configurable. Prepared structures are registered in
Fármaco Atlas, with preparation settings, reports, and logs retained for
subsequent inspection.

##### Multiple sequence alignment search

The msa (msa) search tool retrieves homologous protein sequences for a
target and returns the resulting alignment in A3M format. It accesses
NVIDIA’s ColabFold msa Search endpoint and allows the sequence
databases, expectation-value threshold, and maximum number of returned
sequences to be configured. Alignments are cached using a key derived
from the normalized target sequence and search parameters, allowing them
to be reused by downstream structure-prediction workflows such as
Boltz-2 without repeating the remote search.

### B.2 Binding-site identification

##### Pocketeer

Pocketeer \[[41](#bib.bib41)\] is a lightweight tool for identifying
potential ligand-binding pockets on protein structures. It analyzes the
geometric features of protein surfaces to detect cavities that may
accommodate small molecules. pocketeer-0.3.0 is installed in the same
Python environment as the agentic system. In addition, a custom utility
maps the atoms defining a detected pocket to the corresponding amino
acid residues in the protein structure using the Pocketeer pocket json
(json) output and the original pdb file.

##### P2Rank

P2Rank \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] is a ml (ml)–based method for predicting
ligand-binding sites on protein structures. It evaluates local
structural environments on the protein surface using engineered features
and random forest models to estimate the likelihood of ligand binding.
P2Rank is widely used for automated binding-site identification and
prioritization in structure-based drug discovery. p2rank-2.5.1 is made
accessible to the agentic Python environment via installing the
Java-based program in the same Docker container, and building a Python
api around the cli (cli) io (io). An additional utility exposes the
predicted pocket residues as structured data.

### B.3 Ligand generation

##### MolMIM

MolMIM is a probabilistic encoder–decoder model for small-molecule
generation, trained on SMILES representations using Mutual Information
Machine (MIM) learning. It learns distributions over chemical structures
and can generate novel molecules conditioned on target properties or
structural constraints. MolMIM is particularly useful for exploring
chemical space in early-stage drug discovery, enabling scaffold
generation, property optimization, and de novo ligand design.

##### GenMol

GenMol \[[44](#bib.bib44)\] is a generative molecular design framework
that produces chemically valid small molecules guided by structural or
physicochemical constraints. By combining deep generative modeling with
molecular representations such as graphs or smiles strings, GenMol can
propose novel candidate compounds for drug discovery workflows,
supporting tasks such as scaffold exploration, lead diversification, and
property-driven optimization.

##### PocketXMol

PocketXMol \[[35](#bib.bib35)\] is a diffusion-based generative model
for structure-based drug design that generates small molecules
conditioned on a protein binding pocket. It integrates geometric
representations of the pocket with molecular diffusion processes to
propose chemically valid ligands that fit the target site. PocketXMol
enables de novo ligand generation guided by structural context and
binding-site constraints. The integration uploads a persisted protein
structure and submits a remote compute job specifying pocket
coordinates, sampling radius, and the requested number of molecules. The
job returns a structured result file and an archive of generated
molecular structures, which the client retrieves for persistence in
Fármaco Atlas
([Sec. A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Remote compute jobs ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

##### PrexSyn

PrexSyn \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] is a framework for exploring synthetically
accessible chemical space through reaction-based molecular generation.
It constructs candidate molecules by combining 115 reaction templates
with the enamine_rxn115 fragment library containing about 220k building
blocks \[[63](#bib.bib63)\], enabling systematic exploration of
compounds that are likely to be synthesizable. This approach supports
property-directed analog generation within realistic synthetic
constraints. prexsyn-7b1e984 was installed in a Docker container,
including the appropriate weights from
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/luost26/prexsyn-data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/luost26/prexsyn-data).
The integration supports a local http (http) service, selected by
default, and an optional remote compute backend. The latter submits the
query ligand and sampling parameters as a finite job and retrieves a
required structured result file through the shared compute client
([Sec. A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Remote compute jobs ‣ Appendix A Data and workflow infrastructure ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).

### B.4 Pose and affinity prediction

##### Boltz-2

Boltz-2 \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] is a structural biology foundation model for
predicting both biomolecular complex structures and small
molecule–protein binding affinities. Building on AlphaFold-style
co-folding, it integrates enhanced data curation, dynamic ensemble
training, and controllability features (e.g., distance constraints and
template conditioning). Notably, Boltz-2 approaches the accuracy of fep
(fep) methods for affinity prediction while being over 1000× faster.

##### DiffDock

DiffDock \[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\] is a diffusion-based
deep learning model for protein–ligand docking. Instead of relying on
traditional search heuristics and scoring functions, DiffDock models the
docking process as a generative diffusion process that directly predicts
ligand poses within protein binding pockets. This approach improves
robustness to initial ligand placement and enables accurate blind
docking while maintaining competitive computational efficiency.

### B.5 Structural analysis, validation, and free energy

##### plip

plip \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] is a tool for automated analysis of
non-covalent interactions between proteins and bound ligands. It detects
interaction types such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, salt
bridges, $`\pi`$-$`\pi`$ stacking, and halogen bonds directly from
structural data. plip is commonly used to interpret docking results,
analyze binding modes, and characterize protein–ligand complexes.
plip-3.0.0 is installed in the main Docker container hosting the agentic
system.

##### PoseBusters

PoseBusters \[[64](#bib.bib64)\] is a benchmarking and validation
framework for evaluating predicted protein–ligand binding poses. It
performs a series of automated sanity checks on three-dimensional
structures resulting from docking, including geometric plausibility,
chemical validity, steric clashes, and consistency with structural
constraints. posebusters-0.6.3 is installed in the same Python
environment as the agentic system.

##### Azulene Studio binding free-energy calculations

Azulene Studio, provided by Azulene Labs, runs alchemical absolute and
relative binding free-energy calculations, complementing faster learned
affinity and docking scores with physics-based estimates. Fármaco
exposes two submission workflows. opal_abfe_submit takes 1–10 persisted
protein–ligand complex structures and computes an absolute binding free
energy for each from a solvent and a complex leg. opal_rbfe_submit takes
a protein-only structure, a fixed reference ligand pose A, and 1–10
candidate poses B in the receptor frame, and computes
$`\Delta\Delta G=\Delta G(B)-\Delta G(A)`$ for each A$`\to`$B edge. The
agent builds networks from such edges; no all-pairs network is generated
automatically. Both workflows support protonation-state assignment at a
chosen pH and repeated protocols. rbfe equilibration and production
lengths are configurable, with defaults of 5 ps and 10 ps, whereas abfe
(abfe) lengths are currently fixed at these values as a cost guard.
Because the calculations run for hours, submission returns immediately.
Each job is stored as a durable record keyed by its effective input, so
identical requests reuse an existing job rather than being resubmitted.
Separate opal_collect and opal_cancel tools check or cancel given jobs
once per call. Completed free energies, uncertainties, and, for abfe,
standard-state corrections are then persisted as results linked to the
target, ligands, and structures in Fármaco Atlas.

### B.6 Developability, ligand preparation, and compound sourcing

##### admet-ai

admet-ai \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] is an ml platform for predicting admet
properties of small molecules. By leveraging large curated datasets and
deep learning models, it enables rapid in silico estimation of
pharmacokinetic and toxicity-related properties. A Docker container
based on python:3.12-slim hosts admet-ai-2.0.1, which exposes
predictions via a FastAPI service.

##### RDKit-based ligand preparation, filtering, and comparison

Several complementary cheminformatics operations are implemented with
RDKit \[[65](#bib.bib65)\] and exposed collectively through the
database-backed workflow interface. The conformer-generation tool
converts ligand smiles strings into three-dimensional sdf (sdf)
structures using ETKDGv3 embedding, removes geometrically redundant
conformers by root-mean-square-deviation pruning, and optionally
optimizes and ranks the resulting geometries with the Universal Force
Field. The descriptor tool calculates queryable physicochemical and
structural properties, including molecular weight, lipophilicity,
topological polar surface area, hydrogen-bond counts, rotatable bonds,
ring counts, fraction of sp³ carbon atoms, formal charge, qed, and
Lipinski rule-of-five violations. Structural-alert screening applies
selectable RDKit filter catalogues, including pains, Brenk, nih (nih),
ZINC, and ChEMBL-derived rule sets, to flag potentially problematic
chemotypes. For ligand comparison, RDKit shape alignment reports
three-dimensional shape, pharmacophore-feature, and combined similarity
scores relative to a chosen reference conformer. Finally, a
reaction-profile filter evaluates compounds against bundled or
user-authored smarts (smarts)-based rules to determine whether they are
suitable building blocks for transformations such as amidation or Suzuki
coupling.

##### Supplier catalogue search

The supplier-lookup tool searches the ChemDiv and Enamine catalogues for
purchasable compounds using exact, similarity, or substructure queries
derived from a ligand’s smiles representation. A dedicated FastAPI
service provides a common interface to the ChemDiv search service and to
Enamine collections exposed through SmallWorld. Returned catalogue
identifiers, structures, molecular formulae, molecular weights,
availability information, and backend-specific similarity measures are
stored with the workflow result, enabling the agent to prioritize
candidates that can be acquired experimentally.

### B.7 Data retrieval and format-conversion utilities

##### mmcif to pdb Converter

The mmcif to pdb converter \[[66](#bib.bib66)\] is a utility tool for
transforming macromolecular structure files from the modern mmcif format
into the legacy pdb format. Since many computational chemistry and
docking tools still rely on pdb inputs, such conversion utilities
facilitate compatibility.

##### pdb Structure and Sequence Retrieval

This utility provides automated access to macromolecular structures and
sequences from the [rcsb (rcsb)](https://www.rcsb.org) pdb. Protein
structures are downloaded using Biopython’s PDBList interface based on
their four-character pdb identifiers. Downloaded structures are cached
locally to avoid redundant network requests and improve workflow
efficiency. In addition, the tool retrieves the corresponding protein
sequences in FASTA format¹³¹³ 13
[https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/doc/blast-topics/#fasta](https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/doc/blast-topics/#fasta),
accessed March 4, 2026. via the rcsb pdb FASTA endpoint for the
specified pdb entry. Both structural data and sequences are stored
within the agent working directory to enable downstream computational
workflows.

##### Clean up protein structures

This utility cleans protein structure files in pdb format using
Biopython-based parsing and selection \[[67](#bib.bib67)\]. It removes
non-protein entities such as water molecules and hetero atoms, retaining
only standard protein residues. The cleaned structure is written to a
new pdb file in the working directory while preserving the original
structure.

##### Protein–ligand structure merging

This utility combines a protein structure in pdb format with a ligand
structure from an sdf file into a single complex pdb file. Using RDKit
\[[65](#bib.bib65)\], the ligand coordinates are converted into pdb
format and appended to the protein structure.

##### smiles-to-XYZ conversion

This utility generates three-dimensional molecular geometries from
smiles strings using RDKit. The resulting structures are written as XYZ
coordinate files for use in downstream molecular modeling workflows.

##### Web search

ai web search retrieves up-to-date information from the web using a
large language model with integrated search capabilities. It returns
synthesized answers based on external sources.

#### C.1.2 Boltz-2 scoring of the purchased set, and a calibration caveat

The purchased (TBXT Challenge) and agent-selected compounds were scored
by different services at different times, so their Boltz-2 values are
not on a demonstrably common scale and the comparison is descriptive. Of
the 39 purchased compounds, 31 carry values from the original scoring
batch; the other eight are vendor analogues substituted at procurement,
after that batch, and were scored later. Three of the 31 with known
original values (Matter-0031, Matter-0034 and Matter-0020) were rescored
alongside the eight and none reproduced: 4.644, 5.427 and 6.222 became
4.534, 5.108 and 5.396 ($`-0.11`$, $`-0.32`$ and $`-0.83`$, growing with
the value), compressing the controls’ range from 1.58 to 0.86 log units.
The eight later values (median 5.43, SD 0.30, range 4.84–5.71) resemble
the 31 original values (median 5.43, SD 0.30, range 4.64–6.22), so the
shift is visible only in the controls and cannot be read off the eight
values themselves. This is consistent with a change in model or service
between batches, but replicates intended to separate drift from
run-to-run variation were stopped by the provider, so the model’s
variance is unmeasured. We therefore did not rescale the eight values,
which are treated as a separate, descriptive batch. No control was
scored on both services, so comparability is untested between the
purchased and agent-selected sets and fails within the Rowan batches.

#### C.1.3 Complete instruction supplied to Fármaco

The following is the supplied initial natural-language instruction. The
campaign was executed over two consecutive days, with provenance
recorded in the Fármaco Atlas. A follow-up prompt directed the agent to

#### C.1.4 Published reference ligands

The five published reference ligands are the only compounds in this case
study with reported experimental affinities, and they are included so
that the two computationally selected sets can be read against a
measured baseline rather than only against each other. They are taken
from the study that first described ligand binding at pocket A^(′)
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\], and the apparent dissociation constants in
[Tab. 6](#A3.T6 "In C.1.4 Published reference ligands ‣ C.1 TBXT retrospective comparison ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
are the spr (spr) values reported there. Their structures are public and
are released in the artifact package. Docking scores were obtained with
the same common protocol applied to the agent-selected and purchased
sets.

These five compounds provide a limited assessment of how reliably
docking scores rank measured affinity. Across the full series, docking
score and affinity show positive but statistically non-significant rank
correlations (Spearman $`\rho=0.67`$, $`p=0.22`$ for 7ZKF;
$`\rho=0.82`$, $`p=0.09`$ for 7ZK2). However, these correlations are
driven largely by M11, which is approximately 14–43 times weaker than
the other compounds and also receives the worst docking score.

Among the four compounds with comparable potency, docking does not
recover the experimental ordering. M10 has the strongest measured
affinity ($`8\pm 3\penalty\ \mu`$M) but ranks only third of five by
docking score. Conversely, M7 receives the best docking score ($`-7.40`$
kcal mol⁻¹) but has a measured affinity of approximately
$`14\pm 9\penalty\ \mu`$M.

This result supports our use of docking scores as coarse ranking signals
rather than quantitative estimates of affinity. It also suggests that
the small differences between the agent-selected and purchased
distributions should not be interpreted as meaningful. The best scores
were $`-8.5`$ and $`-8.4`$ kcal mol⁻¹ for the agent-selected and
purchased sets, respectively, while their corresponding medians were
$`-7.3`$ and $`-7.5`$ kcal mol⁻¹. Overall, these computational scores
neither establish binding nor predict potency.

receptor preparation and pocket detection are not registered as runs, so
the earlier part of the workflow is evidenced by its output artifacts
rather than by database records; these are described below.

different. The archived execution workspaces are named by the

#### C.2.2 Stage 1: generation, filtering, docking, and contact gate

The adaptive instruction specified repeated generation and selection
until five Stage 2 passers were obtained or a stopping condition was
reached. Four PocketXMol calls used the PGK2 pocket center
$`(52.42,43.78,25.95)`$ Å and an 8 Å radius, registering 87 and 94
molecules in cycle 1 and 95 and 93 molecules in cycle 2, for 369
molecules in total. Each registered molecule was canonicalized without
tautomer enumeration and evaluated using pains catalogues A–C, qed
$`\geq 0.6`$, sa (sa) score $`\leq 5`$, and an extended Lipinski
rule-of-five requiring at least four of five limits: molecular weight
$`\leq 500`$, cLogP $`\leq 5`$, hbd (hbd) $`\leq 5`$, hba (hba)
$`\leq 10`$, and rotatable bonds $`\leq 10`$. The persisted hard-filter
subsets contained 19 and 15 molecules in cycle 1 and 23 and 19 molecules
in cycle 2, yielding 76 passers overall.

The 76 hard-filter passers were docked against PGK2. For each ligand,
the single returned DiffDock pose was merged with the receptor and
analyzed by plip. A Stage 1 pass required a recorded hydrogen bond or
hydrophobic interaction with PGK2 TYR242; a halogen-only contact was not
an accepted gate type, and no manual interaction override entered the
reported selection. The persisted Stage 1 subsets contained 28 ligands
from cycle 1 and 30 from cycle 2, yielding 58 ligands for Stage 2. Thus,
the same explicit filter and interaction rules were applied to all four
generated batches, and the intermediate subsets and structures remained
linked to their originating run records in Fármaco Atlas.

#### C.2.3 Stage 2: target–counter-target comparison and candidate selection

The 28 and 30 Stage 1 passers from the two cycles were evaluated
separately against PGK2 and PGK1 by Boltz-2 co-folding, and PGK1 was
additionally evaluated by DiffDock. The instruction supplied a mapped
PGK1 pocket center, whereas the archived DiffDock request records
contain num_poses=1 but no pocket-coordinate field. Boltz-2 was run
without a template or pocket constraint, and its PGK2 and PGK1 outputs
were retained together with the corresponding confidence, plddt (plddt),
binding_probability, and affinity fields.

The archive also retains the prompt-requested ligand rmsd (rmsd) fields
generated by optimal ligand superposition. A post-run audit found that
these calculations did not constitute an independent DiffDock–Boltz-2
pose comparison, so the values are retained as provenance rather than
used as structural evidence or selection gates in the manuscript. All
five model-score-threshold passers also had archived numerical rmsd
values below the 3.5 Å prompt threshold. The prespecified model-scale
selection rule reported in the manuscript was
$`\Delta\texttt{predicted\_pic50}=\texttt{predicted\_pic50}_{\mathrm{PGK2}}-\texttt{predicted\_pic50}_{\mathrm{PGK1}}\geq 0.477`$.
The PGK2 and PGK1 scores are the archived predicted*pic50 fields and are
treated here as model-output scores. For all 116 archived predictions,
these scores equal $`1.364\,(6-\texttt{binding\_affinity})`$, differing
from the normalized $`\mathrm{pIC}*{50}`$ convention
$`6-\texttt{binding_affinity}`$. The 0.477 threshold was applied on the
recorded Boltz-2 score scale and is not interpreted as a threefold
affinity difference.

4. Apply hard filters: no PAINS structural alerts (catalogs A, B, C) /

#### C.3.2 Retrospective enrichment and selection

The numerical verification script reconstructs the dataset summaries,
rank-bin enrichment, selection-strategy comparisons, selected-subset
outcomes, interaction fingerprints, and admet and generation counts from

20.15 and probabilities of 0.918 and 0.850 to the chain-B and chain-A
catalytic clefts, respectively. Both pockets contain Ser64, Tyr150,
Asn152, Lys315, Thr316, Gly317, and Asn346. Pocketeer also detected the
catalytic region but ranked an inter-chain cavity first.

DiffDock generated five poses per ligand for the top-50 subset and ten
per ligand for the four co-crystal ligands. The latter were docked into
the 1L2S-derived receptor; the co-crystal coordinates were used for
validation. The mapping between catalogue identifiers and pdb entries
agrees with the source study’s data-availability statement
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\]: Z6615020275/9C84, Z6615017509/9C6P,
Z6615017782/9DHL, and Z4462773688/9C81.

The agent calculated ligand rmsd in an interactive Python tool. The
archived recomputation script superposes each co-crystal chain onto the
corresponding receptor chain using shared C$`\alpha`$ atoms and Kabsch
alignment, then compares ligand coordinates using an RDKit mcs (mcs). It
selects the lowest rmsd among the enumerated mappings and the two
chains, requiring coverage of at least 80 % of docked heavy atoms. The
substructure search uses unique matches with a maximum of 100 per
molecule; it does not guarantee enumeration of all symmetry-equivalent
atom mappings. Crystal ligands were reconstructed from pdb coordinates
without restoring bond orders from a chemical template. Consequently,
the mcs covers 22/24, 21/23, 21/24, and 22/24 heavy atoms for 9C84,
9C6P, 9DHL, and 9C81, respectively. The resulting rank-1 rmsd reproduce
as 0.87, 3.27, 2.76, and 5.55 Å to the reported precision. Only 9C84 had
a rank-1 pose within 2 Å of its crystal reference; considering all ten
poses per ligand did not change which ligand met this threshold
([Tab. 8](#A3.T8 "In C.3.3 Pocket detection and structural comparison ‣ C.3 AmpC retrospective benchmark ‣ Appendix C Case-study methods and provenance ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")).
These are partial-heavy-atom mcs comparisons, not standardized
full-ligand symmetry-corrected rmsd.

PoseBusters \[[64](#bib.bib64)\] was run on all 40 co-crystal-ligand
poses using its dock configuration. Each rank-1 result records 25 of 30
checks as passing. The failed entries include missing-reference or
cofactor/water-related checks as well as minimum-distance-to-protein
checks; the aggregate pass fraction should therefore not be interpreted
as a fraction of physically valid poses.

plip \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] was applied to the experimental complexes and
to complexes formed by merging each rank-1 docked ligand with the
prepared receptor. Crystal fingerprints are unions of contacts from both
chains; chain identity is discarded when forming residue/type and
residue-only sets. The docked complexes contain one ligand copy, so the
reported Jaccard values compare that ligand’s contacts with an aggregate
crystal reference. For example, restricting the crystal reference to the
corresponding chain changes the residue/type Jaccard from 0.56 to 0.83
for 9C84 and from 0.42 to 0.56 for 9C6P. Crystal fingerprints also
include water-mediated contacts, whereas the prepared docking complexes
lack those waters. Docked poses frequently contacted Ser64, Lys67,
Asn152, and Ala318. Recurring docked contacts are not necessarily
recovered experimental contacts: the docked 9C81 ligand contacts Ser64
and Lys67, which are absent from its crystal fingerprint.

#### C.3.4 admet triage and generative follow-up

admet-ai predictions, RDKit descriptors, pains/Brenk alerts, and
catalogue matches were used to prioritize the 298
numeric-$`K_{\mathrm{i}}`$ hits. The 71 reported Enamine matches come
from an exact query against REALDB-25Q3-9.4B. A missing match in that
query does not establish that a compound is unavailable; the original
study states that its tested compounds could be purchased from Enamine
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\]. Predicted dili scores were $`\geq 0.8`$ for 241
hits and $`<0.5`$ for only four. None of the nine compounds combining
$`K_{\mathrm{i}}\leq 50`$ µM, a clean alert profile, and an exact
catalogue match had a predicted DILI score below 0.5. admet scores are
model predictions and were not calibrated or experimentally validated
for these compounds. The reported AMES and ClinTox values were retrieved
from complete result payloads. For the active-hit admet run, the
requested name Ames did not match the returned name AMES, and ClinTox
was not requested, so these values are absent from that run’s metric
rows despite being present in its result payloads.

Nine hits with $`K_{\mathrm{i}}\leq 2`$ µM were submitted as MolMIM
seeds with requested parameters of 20 molecules per seed and minimum
similarity 0.6. Four seed runs contributed 20 molecules each to the
80-molecule set. The returned molmim_score is labelled qed in the
successful result payloads and equals the independently stored RDKit qed
for all 80 molecules. The agent nevertheless aliased this field as
similarity in its queries and used it as a similarity threshold and
ranking contribution; one composite score also included a separate qed
contribution. Thus, the numerical values reproduce, but their
interpretation as seed similarity is incorrect and the reported MolMIM
shortlist is not evidence of successful similarity-based prioritization.
The requested generation constraint and the returned qed score must be
distinguished.

The four methods shared property, structural-alert, and admet
assessments, but their prioritization rules were not identical.
Shape/feature comparison to the 9C6P ligand conformer and exact supplier
lookup were additionally performed for PocketXMol and PrexSyn. The
combined property window used in the generation summaries was molecular
weight 250–450, logP 0–4, and tpsa (tpsa) 50–140 Å²; none of the 25
GenMol molecules met all three criteria. Of 100 requested PocketXMol
samples, 85 were marked successful, two incomplete, and 13 bad; the
reported downstream counts include both successful and incomplete
samples. PrexSyn returned 79 hits across nine seeds, including the seed
itself as the top hit for each query; the agent retained 20
non-identical analogues for subsequent analysis. The counts with
predicted DILI below 0.75 were 56/80 for MolMIM, 24/25 for GenMol, 23/87
for PocketXMol, and 0/20 for the retained PrexSyn set. Some PocketXMol
candidates had lower predicted DILI scores than the retained PrexSyn
analogues, but generally showed weaker feature similarity to the 9C6P
reference ligand. The screening columns in
[Fig. 6](#S4.F6 "In 4.3 Retrospective AmpC 𝛽-lactamase virtual-screening benchmark ‣ 4 Case studies ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")a
apply each criterion independently to the complete analyzed set for each
method. Cell values give counts, and color encodes within-set
percentages. The alert screen requires a clean PAINS/Brenk profile; the
other columns use the combined property window, predicted logS
$`\geq-4`$, hERG $`<0.5`$, DILI $`<0.75`$, and Ames $`<0.5`$. The set
sizes and selection rules differ across methods.

PocketXMol used a sampling centre of $`(23.5,5.5,14.5)`$ Å and a radius
of 10 Å. This centre is 6.9 Å from the chain-A catalytic-residue
centroid and 8.6 Å from the P2Rank pocket centre. Both reference points
lie within the sampling sphere, but the effect of the offset on
generated molecules was not tested. No generated compound was
synthesized or assayed, and predicted admet properties, shape/feature
similarity, and structural-alert status do not establish AmpC activity.

### C.4 KpThiL binding-site analysis, generation, and affinity/ADMET follow-up

#### C.4.1 Binding-site analysis

Fármaco defined the substrate-binding pocket using the two bound tps
molecules, one in each protomer. The two sites had contact-residue
Jaccard indices of $`0.73`$ at 4 Å and $`0.83`$ at 6 Å. Chain A was
selected as the representative site while preserving the full dimer
context, since partner-chain residues THR123, THR124, and ASP122 lie
within 4–6 Å of the ligand. Substrate recognition involves a polar
cluster observed in both chains. Key contacts include GLU263, which
forms the closest interaction with the aminopyrimidine N4A atom at
approximately 2.6 Å; the ASN53 backbone carbonyl, which contacts the
ring nitrogens; HIS54, which lines the phosphate region through NE2 at
approximately 2.9 Å; and ASP215, which also contributes to phosphate
recognition. The surrounding contact shell comprises PHE55, LEU56,
LEU168, HIS190, and ASP47. P2Rank identifies the site as pocket 2, with
a closest-pocket distance of approximately 2.6 Å, while Pocketeer
recovers symmetric pockets 4 and 7 together with pocket 5 spanning the
tps contact region. Because atp is absent from the structure, putative
atp-binding subsites, including P2Rank pocket 1 with a reported
probability of 0.95, are labelled as inferred and treated as secondary.

#### C.4.6 Pose checks and heuristic ranking

For each predicted complex, the agent used Kabsch alignment on 315
shared chain-A C$`\alpha`$ positions and applied the same transformation
to the ligand. Eight positions in the 323-residue sequence (1–6, 17, and 18) lacked experimental chain-A C$`\alpha`$ coordinates and were
excluded from the alignment. The median receptor C$`\alpha`$
root-mean-square deviation was 1.22 Å. It classified a pose as a
tps-pocket fit when its ligand centroid was less than 9 Å from the
experimental tps centroid, at least 30% of its heavy atoms were within 5
Å of any tps heavy atom, and it contacted at least two chain-A residues
within 4.2 Å. It counted the ligand heavy atoms whose nearest
experimental protein heavy atom in either chain was less than 2 Å away
as potential clash atoms. This count is neither the number of
overlapping atom pairs nor a force-field clash energy. Nine poses passed
the pocket heuristic: six had at most three clash atoms and three had
more than three; only catalogue*003 had zero. The remaining 96 poses
were classified outside the tps pocket by this rule. The strongest
converted Boltz-2 affinity score, $`\mathrm{pIC}*{50}=6.66`$ for
prexsyn_003_02, corresponded to a ligand centroid 26.87 Å from the tps
centroid.

The agent chose and revised the thresholds and score during execution;
they were not prespecified screening criteria. The final ranking sorted
first by pocket/clash tier and then by a weighted score combining
converted affinity, binder probability, ligand interface confidence,
pocket proximity, clash counts, predicted solubility and permeability,
toxicity/CYP scores, broader structural alerts, and catalogue status.
The three tiers, in order, were tps-pocket fits with at most three
potential clash atoms, pocket fits with more than three, and other
poses; the weighted score ordered candidates within each tier. A final
adjustment applied additional penalties for hERG, Ames, or dili scores
of at least 0.8. The full score and final adjustment are documented in
the artifact README and preserved execution history. Morgan-fingerprint
similarities were reported relative to the initial score order; they did
not contribute to the score and were not recomputed after the final
reranking. The final rank order therefore represents the agent’s
exploratory prioritization rather than a validated or prospectively
fixed selection model.

## Appendix D Multi-task benchmark

### D.1 Scope and experimental design

We evaluated three agent configurations across 22 computational
drug-discovery tasks: El Agente Fármaco with the Fármaco database and
workflow interface (mode A; labelled Fármaco in the figures), the same
domain agent with the underlying scientific capabilities exposed through
plain tool calls (mode B), and the standalone Codex coding agent (mode
C). All 198 runs used gpt-6-sol as their execution model; the Codex runs
used codex-cli 0.162.1. Each task used the same scientific instructions
and supplied input files across configurations, and the agents chose
their methods unless the task specified one, as in the 48-ligand Boltz-2
task. Codex worked in the provisioned Fármaco software environment and
could write and execute code and access the supplied scientific-service
endpoints. The two domain-agent configurations could also use
model-backed web search.

Each task and configuration has three runs, with three separate judge
sessions per run. The benchmark contains 66 runs per configuration
across all 22 tasks, giving 198 agent runs and 594 judge sessions in
total.

### D.3 Evidence-based judging

Each run was assessed in three fresh standalone judge sessions using
gpt-6.1-sol with high reasoning effort. The judge received the original
task, input-file names and unmodified inputs, the final response, a copy
of the run’s saved workspace, and any task-specific rubric. The same
evaluation instructions and task-specific criteria were used across
configurations. The judge could inspect saved files, molecular
structures, tables, and databases and recompute checks in a separate
working directory. Task-specific rubrics emphasized issues such as
alignment depth, scaffold retention, conformer validation, cohort
membership, and distinguishing pose confidence from affinity. The
criteria accepted scientifically valid alternative methods and credited
evidence-supported diagnoses of failures.

The judge assigned separate 0–10 scores for task completion, scientific
correctness, evidence consistency, and overall quality, together with a
pass, partial, or fail verdict. The judge assigned the overall score
directly from its assessment of the complete delivered result. A pass
indicated work acceptable to a domain expert, partial indicated useful
but incomplete or flawed work, and fail indicated an unsatisfactory
result. For each run we used the median of its three judge scores,
calculated separately for the overall and component scores. Mode-level
scores are means of these per-run medians. Verdict percentages use all
198 judge sessions per configuration.

### D.4 Resource accounting and uncertainty

Execution cost covers the agent’s model calls in US dollars, including
calls made inside scientific tools. We also report search-adjusted cost
by subtracting the charges for model calls made by the domain agents’
search tool. Total tokens accumulate input and output tokens over model
calls, including cached input, whereas peak context is the largest
input-token count of any single model call, including cached input. The
cached input share for each run is the number of cache-read tokens
divided by total input tokens, including cached input; we report the
median of these run-level shares. For modes A and B, retry delays
attributed to provider-side issues in model API calls were removed from
the reported runtimes by subtracting 300 s per inferred stalled attempt.
Codex was measured as a black-box service, and its reported runtime is
used directly.

We summarize latency, cost, cumulative tokens, and peak context by their
medians because their distributions are skewed. For each configuration,
we estimated uncertainty by randomly drawing 22 tasks with repeat
selections allowed, keeping all three runs of every selected task, and
recalculating the mean or median. We repeated this process 5,000 times
and used the central 95% of the resulting values as the confidence
interval.

### D.8 Worked example: 3d shape and pharmacophore comparison

The molecular_shape_feature_compare task compares 49 compounds with
aspirin in a supplied 50-compound library. Each configuration generated
conformers, calculated shape and pharmacophore overlay scores, and
returned a ranked table. This example uses the same nine runs included
in the multi-task benchmark.

#### D.8.1 Execution and resource use

In each database-backed run, the agent used two subset-scoped workflows
for conformer generation and reference-based comparison. The workflows
persisted structures and metrics, and the agent assembled the ranking
through sql queries. The requested conformer sampling was one conformer
per compound in one run and ensembles of up to eight or ten conformers
in the other two. The plain-tool-call configuration performed batch
RDKit calculations in its Python tool; one run also used individual
conformer-generation and comparison tools for codeine. Codex wrote and
executed RDKit scripts in the provisioned software environment.

The database-backed configuration had the lowest mean latency and
cumulative token use for this task. Its mean model cost was \$0.129,
compared with \$0.136 for plain tool calls and \$0.418 for Codex. The
mean cached share of input tokens was 73.9%, 93.2%, and 95.4%,

database workflows.

### D.9 Additional STK17B ranking experiment at larger scale

This additional experiment extends the 48-ligand STK17B ranking task in
the multi-task benchmark
([Sec. 3](#S3 "3 Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery"))
to the full 256-ligand input library. Fármaco was instructed to load a
csv file of 256 reductive-amination ligands, predict their binding to
STK17B, also known as DRAK2¹⁵¹⁵ 15 DAP kinase-related apoptosis-inducing
protein kinase 2., using pdb entry 6ZJF with Boltz-2 \[[7](#bib.bib7)\],
return a ranked table with binding metrics, and profile the top five
predicted poses with plip, paying particular attention to hinge-region
backbone hydrogen bonds. In the database-backed run, all five top-ranked
poses showed a predicted backbone hydrogen bond to the hinge residue
ALA111. The highest-ranked pose also showed a contact with GLU109.
[Figure 13](#A4.F13 "In D.9 Additional STK17B ranking experiment at larger scale ‣ Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
shows this predicted complex.

We used this library-scale task to compare agent execution across
configurations, focusing on tool calls, token consumption, llm cost, and
operator intervention. The same initial instruction was given to the
plain-tool-call configuration, in which the Fármaco database is disabled
and the underlying services are exposed as plain per-ligand tool calls.
Both configurations used gpt-5.5, completed all 256 predictions,
returned a ranking, and performed the top-five interaction analysis.
With the database, the agent imported the ligands, resolved the target,
and submitted all 256 protein–ligand pairs through one subset-scoped
workflow call. The workflow persisted the results, metrics, and
predicted complexes without returning individual prediction payloads to
the model’s context. The agent then queried the results, assembled the
ranking, and completed the interaction analysis within a single user
turn. The plain-tool-call configuration issued 256 individual prediction
calls and required six user turns. It stopped before completing the
library three times, completed the predictions on the fourth turn, and
required two further prompts to acknowledge and then complete the
outstanding ranking and interaction analysis. Both configurations cached
the msa and issued one alignment search.

The resource accounting in
[Tab. 18](#A4.T18 "In D.9 Additional STK17B ranking experiment at larger scale ‣ Appendix D Multi-task benchmark ‣ El Agente Fármaco: A Scalable AI Scientist for Drug Discovery")
shows that the principal overhead in the plain-tool-call configuration
was input processing. Despite making fewer llm calls, the
plain-tool-call configuration repeatedly processed a growing
conversation containing individual prediction payloads, whereas the
database-backed workflow persisted results for selective retrieval. The
resulting increase in cumulative input tokens substantially exceeded the
increase in output tokens, indicating that the additional usage
primarily reflected processing conversation history rather than
generating responses. Prompt caching moderated the associated cost, but
did not eliminate the difference between configurations. Fewer llm calls
therefore did not translate into lower token usage or cost. The agent
selected different Boltz-2 sampling settings across configurations, so
the comparison concerns agent execution overhead rather than prediction
quality.
