###### Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents can keep scientific experiments
running for months with little human supervision, but reliable
autonomous research also requires scientific judgment. Evaluating
scientific judgment is difficult because research decisions are
open-ended and depend on project context. We construct Sci$`\Pi`$vot, a
248-case benchmark of scientific judgment based on human corrections.
The benchmark draws on records from six months of research in which
senior researchers ran LLM agents on 26 projects across six scientific
fields and repeatedly intervened to correct the agents’ research
judgments. In each case, an agent chooses between the original LLM plan
and the human-corrected alternative, using only the project record
available before the correction as context. We compare LLM and human
performance and examine the effects of reasoning effort, access to the
project record, and reconsideration. At medium reasoning effort with the
project record, models systematically prefer the original LLM plan over
the human-corrected alternative. Four of five frontier models perform
below chance on the selected cases. The highest accuracy in the project
condition across the tested models and reasoning effort settings is
63.5%, compared with 73.4% for fresh researchers. At medium effort,
every model achieves lower accuracy with the project record than without
it, and reconsideration does not consistently improve overall accuracy.
Even with a human-corrected alternative available, frontier models
struggle with the selected research decisions, while Sci$`\Pi`$vot
supports systematic evaluation of scientific judgment for LLM agents.

## 1 Introduction

Scientific research tackles problems with unknown answers. Formulating
useful questions, designing experiments, and revising assumptions as
evidence arrives make scientific research a demanding test of general
intelligence. An AI system with this ability could study and improve its
own models and methods. Such progress could enable recursive
self-improvement, as better systems conduct better research that helps
create more capable systems ([Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)).

We began with a practical question. Can frontier models conduct
high-quality scientific research autonomously? Using frontier models
with frontier agentic systems, we ran _scientific AutoResearch agents_
on original, PhD-level projects at scale across disciplines. Over
roughly half a year, individual projects ran for weeks to months with
publication as the goal. They involved theory, simulation, computation,
and analysis of existing scientific data using CPUs and GPUs. Keeping
experiments running proved easier than choosing experiments that
advanced the scientific question. Automated checks and review still
depended on models to notice mistaken assumptions and decide how to test
them. Failed judgments could stall projects or produce results that left
the underlying problem unresolved. Senior researchers had to diagnose
the problem and steer the next experiment.

These interventions required anticipating what an experiment could
establish. Should an AutoResearch agent scale up, or first check its
measurements? Does a failed attempt undermine the idea, or only its
implementation? Such decisions repeatedly required human intervention
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Scientific Judgment as a Bottleneck ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
We call this scientific planning and decision-making _Principal
Investigator (PI) Judgment_.

Figure 1: How a case is built and used. The top row shows an original
LLM plan (A), a senior researcher’s intervention, and the project’s
subsequent progress. We use the completed history to reconstruct the
human-corrected plan (B) and the evidence supporting it. Evaluators
receive the pre-intervention project record and both plans, then choose
which to pursue. Choosing B counts as correct.

This dependence raises our next question. How can we improve scientific
AutoResearch agents so that fewer decisions require human intervention?
Agents can readily propose plausible plans, but executing an
uninformative one can waste weeks of research. The bottleneck is judging
which plan merits those resources. Human corrections expose these
decisions and offer a way to teach them.

We introduce Sci$`\Pi`$vot to make these judgments measurable. Its 248
cases from 26 projects pair original LLM plans with human-corrected
alternatives reconstructed from completed project histories. The model
chooses between them using the project record available before the human
intervention, including its code, experimental logs, and progress notes.

At medium reasoning effort with the project record, four of five models
choose the original LLM plan more often than the human-corrected plan on
this selected set. Further deliberation can undo a correct choice and
return to the same error, a failure we call _re-trapping_.

Sci$`\Pi`$vot also connects to core NLP problems. The case pairs provide
preference supervision for scientific corrections rather than stylistic
rewrites. Scoring a choice evaluates the judgment of language agents,
and the protocol exercises how agents use language to read a project
record, compare two plans, and justify a choice. These connections make
Sci$`\Pi`$vot a testbed for NLP training and evaluation methods.

### 2.1 What We Require of AutoResearch

Our AutoResearch program has three joint requirements.

1.  1.  Autonomy. AutoResearch agents should work around the clock with
        minimal human intervention.
2.  2.  Scale and scope. Many projects should run in parallel across
        scientific disciplines.
3.  3.  Scientific quality. Projects should pursue original, PhD-level
        frontier scientific research worthy of publication.

Frequent human intervention limits how many projects a human scientist
can supervise, so autonomy and scale depend on better scientific
judgment.

### 2.2 What Scientific Quality Depends On

Scientific quality depends on three contributors.

1.  1.  Model capability. Models must interpret evidence, adapt to
        experimental results, and choose useful next steps.
2.  2.  The agentic system. Systems must organize experiments, preserve
        project knowledge, and support review across long runs.
3.  3.  Human scientific judgment. Human scientists currently diagnose
        scientific errors and steer experiments toward the research
        question.

Agentic systems could reduce dependence on human judgment by adding
rules and automated checks for observed failures. However, a growing set
of instructions becomes harder for models to follow reliably. Extra
instructions also consume tokens and can impair task performance.
Controlled report-writing experiments show declining adherence and, for
some models, declining report quality as requirements
accumulate ([Jaroslawicz et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)).

Rules also leave scientific questions for the model to resolve. An
instruction to check a measurement does not specify which assumption is
wrong or what evidence would expose it. In our projects, existing checks
and review did not reliably supply these judgments. Senior researchers
reviewed projects several times daily to identify stalled or misdirected
work and choose the next test. Without these corrections, continued
execution could consume computation without resolving the scientific
problem. A capable AutoResearch agent must learn to supply this
diagnosis and change of direction on its own.

### 2.3 Two Examples of Human Scientific Judgment

Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Scientific Judgment as a Bottleneck ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
shows how a human correction changes what a project can learn from its
next experiment.

#### Check the measurement before scaling up.

The Transformer project asks whether reusing a pretrained model’s
layers, with small trainable adapters, can improve performance. It first
needs tasks on which the small base model can be evaluated meaningfully.
After observing zero scores on three LiveCodeBench groups, the agent
proposes moving to a larger model. The senior researcher keeps the small
model and asks the agent to find suitable tasks. A later audit finds
that the scorer failed to extract answers from generated text. Changing
model size with the same scorer would leave this error in place.

#### Question the fit before refining the search.

The cloth project asks how simulation parameters should change with mesh
resolution to preserve realistic motion. Its agent fixes four of six
parameters at zero, a restriction carried forward from its own earlier
runs, and proposes refining the two-parameter search after the best
sampled fits reach the boundary. The senior researcher points out that
missing fitted values do not justify setting the corresponding physical
parameters to zero and calls for fitting all six with the restricted fit
as a comparison.

Both workflows could keep running without testing the mistaken
assumptions that the corrections address. We evaluate whether models
recognize these scientific decisions from the project record.

### 3.1 What Should the Evaluation Preserve?

We build cases retrospectively from completed scientific research
projects. Each project’s senior researcher supervised it from the
initial idea through the final write-up. The full history links human
interventions to subsequent investigation and provides evidence of their
scientific value. We use this evidence to recover useful corrections,
then evaluate them at the earlier decision point. Evaluators see only
the earlier project record and two plans and judge which is more likely
to advance the scientific question while the outcome is still unknown.
Providing the same plans isolates this judgment from generating
plausible alternatives.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
summarizes construction and evaluation.

### 3.2 Recovering a Decision from Its History

Candidate decisions come from human messages and changes to the
project’s human-instruction section. The case writer inspects the full
project history, recovers the original LLM plan, and selects a commit
before the human intervention. It reconstructs the human-corrected plan
using the intervention and subsequent scientific investigation. The
evaluator receives the project record at that commit and the two plans,
without the later history. Accuracy measures how often the evaluator
selects the reference plan.

Grok 4.6 wrote the cases.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Model Results and Statistical Details ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
reports a controlled pilot in which the writer gains no advantage over
the other models on its own cases. The writing instructions exclude
administrative actions, unrecoverable states or decisions, and human
corrections that cannot be linked to an error. Both options follow the
same English Goal: and Plan: format. The prompt requests comparable
detail and length without wording that identifies the reference plan.
The evaluator does not see the writer’s explanations. Each case is
linked to its commit and exact option text.

We selected 248 cases from 26 projects using 120 model responses per
case collected during construction. These responses cover five models,
twelve conditions, and both option orders. We retained cases whose model
responses vary across the two plans. Each retained case keeps a minimum
share of responses on both plans. Four one-sided cases are kept only
after checking their reference plans against documentation at the case
commit. This selection focuses the comparison on cases where model
responses vary. Model-in-the-loop selection follows challenge-set
precedent ([Kiela et al., 2021](#bib.bib13)). The precedent applies to
construction, and the resulting scores are diagnostic and measure
recognition on decisions where models disagree rather than accuracy on
research decisions in general. Case review distinguishes evidence
available at the decision point from later human corrections, outcomes,
and changes in the senior researcher’s goals.

### 3.3 Recurring Failure Modes

The human corrections reveal recurring failures across scientific
research projects
(Table [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 From Human Corrections to Evaluation ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
We name each category after the judgment involved and describe what goes
wrong. These judgments range from adapting to experimental results to
deciding whether the evidence suffices for a conclusion. The focus
category covers experiments that can run correctly yet leave the
research question unanswered. For example, changing a cloth’s physical
area does not answer how its parameters should change with mesh
resolution on the same cloth. The coverage counts and radar scores group
cases by these judgments. All 248 cases have a primary judgment label,
and some carry secondary labels. Historical-error coding assigns 243
cases a primary error label and up to two secondary labels. Coding uses
the original LLM plans and human corrections, without the evaluated
responses
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Judgment Categories and Illustrative Cases ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).

Construction requires a recoverable project state and documented human
correction, so it applies wherever these records exist.

### 3.4 Relation to Existing Benchmarks

Existing benchmarks assess different aspects of scientific research
(Table [3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.3 Recurring Failure Modes ‣ 3 From Human Corrections to Evaluation ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
PaperBench tests replication, while RE-Bench and MLE-bench test progress
against defined research or engineering objectives ([Starace et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26); [Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)). ScienceAgentBench tests executable scientific
workflows ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). The SDE benchmark includes
scenarios from ongoing and past scientific research ([Song et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)); Corral supports scientific environments, trajectory
analysis, and replay ([Ríos-García et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). BLADE
uses analysis decisions from human experts ([Gu et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib7)); TruthInsightBench assesses evidence for scientific
claims ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib33)); SoundnessBench uses human
reviewers’ judgments of proposals ([Ho et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)); and
ForeSci evaluates plans against later literature ([Tian et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)).

Sci$`\Pi`$vot evaluates whether models identify the better-supported
scientific decision using information available before a human
intervention. Its reference plans are grounded in human corrections and
the experimental records that explain them. The AI Scientist systems and
Agon develop workflows for conducting experiments and producing
scientific manuscripts ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Yamada et al.,
2025](#bib.bib32); [Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). Sci$`\Pi`$vot tests
the scientific judgments needed to guide such workflows.

### 4.1 Evaluation Setup

We evaluate GPT-6 Astra ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib19)), Claude Opus
5 ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib1)), Grok 4.6 ([xAI, 2026](#bib.bib31)),
and GPT-5.6 Sol and Terra ([OpenAI, 2026a](#bib.bib18)), abbreviated as
Astra, Opus, Grok, Sol, and Terra. In the _project_ condition, the model
receives two plans and the project record. We ask it to start with the
research idea, proposal, and progress notes, then consult other files as
needed. The project record excludes Git history and later project state.
Network access and research execution are prohibited. The model returns
a choice and an explanation. The _options-only_ condition presents the
same plans without the project record. Client details appear in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Model Results and Statistical Details ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents").
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Evaluation Prompts and Available Materials ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
gives the exact prompts, all twelve conditions, and available materials.

The main comparison requests medium reasoning effort and evaluates every
case in two separate sessions with reversed option order. We average
correctness over the two orders, then give each case equal weight.
Chance accuracy is 50%. Completed responses without a parseable choice
count as incorrect, although all responses in the main comparison have a
parsed choice. We estimate 95% intervals from 10,000 paired bootstrap
samples of cases and report project-cluster intervals in the appendix.

#### Human baseline.

The human baseline comes from fresh researchers with no prior exposure
to these projects and no involvement in supervising them. They answered
through an interface for reading the project, with an agent assistant
available. Each learned the project from the project record and judged a
case, making the final choice. Thirteen PhD students and postdocs took
part, and all were informed about the study, gave consent, and are
reported under anonymous identifiers.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Human Baseline Protocol ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
describes the human baseline protocol.

### 4.2 Models Often Reject the Human-Corrected Plan

At medium effort with the project record, four of the five models select
the rejected plan more often than the reference plan on this selected
set
(Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4 Can Models Recognize Human Corrections? ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
For all four, the upper bounds of both case and project bootstrap
intervals are below 50%. Astra reaches 59.5%, and the five-model average
is 39.2%. The highest observed project score across all tested effort
settings is Astra’s 63.5% at xhigh, compared with 73.4% for the human
baseline.

On 49 cases, all five models choose the rejected plan in both orders; on
16, they all choose the reference plan. The original LLM plan is
on-policy text, while the human-corrected plan is off-policy. A
systematic preference for the original plan is the structural signature
of this asymmetry, not a coincidence.

### 4.3 Different Judgments Reveal Different Gaps

Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 From Human Corrections to Evaluation ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")
breaks down the variation across types of scientific decisions by
scientific field (a) and by the judgment each case requires (b). Each
model point averages the two orders within a case and weights cases
equally within its group. Astra reaches 72.2% on anomaly-awareness
cases, but 56.1% on planning cases and 54.9% on focus cases.

Two cases illustrate the judgment needed to choose an experimental scale
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Judgment Categories and Illustrative Cases ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
In case 21, an untested setup calls for a pilot, yet models retain a
200-item-per-cell batch in seven of ten responses. In case 13, testing
the hypothesis requires a full-length training run, yet models retain
three short gated runs in six of ten responses. Models must allocate
effort according to what the investigation requires, rather than
consistently prefer larger or smaller experiments.

### 4.4 Lower Accuracy with the Project Record

The project record contains observations alongside the original agent’s
explanations, including mistaken interpretations. On this selected set,
every model nevertheless scores higher without the project record at
medium effort. Mean accuracy is 39.2% with the project record and 55.8%
without it
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Models Often Reject the Human-Corrected Plan ‣ 4 Can Models Recognize Human Corrections? ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")).
For four models, the paired 95% interval of this decrease excludes zero,
and Astra’s includes zero. Long contexts can lower task accuracy even
when the added material is informative ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib14)).
The comparison evaluates the complete protocol of the project condition,
including retrieval, interpretation, and the client-specific reading
interface.

The Transformer case illustrates how an inherited explanation can make
the rejected plan appear justified. Opus accepts the progress note’s
claim that scoring faults were excluded and uses the record to support
the original mistake by treating that claim as established evidence. PI
Judgment requires checking the claim against the observations and code.

### 4.5 More Thinking Can Lead Back to the Same Mistake

Test-time scaling can improve model performance by allocating more
computation to reasoning ([Snell et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)). Can further
deliberation help models question these explanations and retain a
correction? We vary requested effort from low through max and continue
the original medium-effort session twice. Each continuation permits
further project reading and a changed answer, without correctness
feedback. Refinement methods that supply external feedback can improve
outputs ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib16); [Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib23)), while reconsideration here receives no feedback, and
self-correction without external feedback can reduce accuracy ([Huang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)). We pair each answer with its recorded parent
to count both repaired and newly introduced errors.

Higher effort sometimes helps, but the gains are inconsistent
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Models Often Reject the Human-Corrected Plan ‣ 4 Can Models Recognize Human Corrections? ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents")a).
From low to max, four models improve while Sol declines from 36.1% to
29.2%. Terra rises from 29.2% to 52.0% after the first review, then
falls to 42.7% after the second. Opus also loses accuracy on the second
review, and both declines exclude zero under paired case and project
intervals.

Across the five models, the first review repairs 422 errors and
introduces 302. The second repairs 264 and introduces 305, partially
undoing the earlier gain. Mean accuracy across the three stages is
39.2%, 44.0%, and 42.3%, with paired intervals in
Table [10](#A1.T10 "Table 10 ‣ A.4 Option Order and Reconsideration ‣ Appendix A Model Results and Statistical Details ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents").

In the Transformer case, Opus first chooses the larger model, then
questions the scorer and chooses the reference plan. On its second
review, it returns to the larger model with a premise that the frozen
code contradicts.

## 5 From Diagnosis to Better AutoResearch Agents

Sustained scientific research exposed decisions that frontier models and
agentic systems still left to senior researchers. Sci$`\Pi`$vot turns
these human corrections into tests of PI Judgment, such as adapting to
experimental results and keeping experiments on the research question.
Even with the project record and further deliberation, models often
return to the decisions senior researchers corrected.

Each case supplies a project record, a preferred human-corrected plan,
and a rejected original LLM plan. These comparisons match the training
format of direct preference optimization ([Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20)). As future work, a preference scorer trained on these
comparisons could supply rewards for research actions in agentic
reinforcement learning. The next test is whether such training improves
decisions in new projects and reduces human intervention.

Learning these shared judgments could support more autonomous scientific
AutoResearch agents across disciplines, including research aimed at
improving AI systems themselves.

## Limitations

Sci$`\Pi`$vot covers theoretical and computational research using CPUs
and GPUs, including simulation and analysis of existing data such as
biological images. Wet-lab execution and workflows using specialized
commercial scientific software such as COMSOL remain outside this
evaluation. The cases test judgments central to PhD research across
disciplines, including experiment selection, anomaly diagnosis, and
revision of conclusions. Evaluation in other settings would test how
these judgments transfer alongside different tools and experimental
procedures.

Sci$`\Pi`$vot measures recognition of corrective scientific decisions
drawn from projects supervised by senior researchers and selected using
the procedure in
Section [3](#S3 "3 From Human Corrections to Evaluation ‣ SciΠvot: Benchmarking Principal-Investigator Judgment at Pivotal Moments of Scientific AutoResearch Agents").
Generating plans and carrying out scientific research require separate
evaluation. The selected cases do not estimate how often these failures
occur across all scientific research activity. Several cases come from
each project, so we report project-cluster intervals alongside
case-level intervals.

The evaluated clients expose different reading tools, and requested
effort settings do not specify equal token budgets. Our effort and
reconsideration experiments test these available controls; they do not
test controlled compute scaling or verifier-guided search.

## Appendix A Model Results and Statistical Details

### A.1 Project-Reading Clients

The requested model identifiers are gpt-6-astra, claude-opus-5,
grok-4.6, gpt-5.6-sol, and gpt-5.6-terra. Opus and Grok use dedicated
file-reading and search tools through one shared client harness, the
Claude client. Shell execution, editing, and web tools are disabled in
that harness. The GPT models use shell commands through Codex, with a
read-only project and web search disabled. All project prompts prohibit
network access and reading outside the project record. The options-only
prompt removes the project record and its reading instructions while
keeping the same plans. The comparison between conditions therefore
includes retrieval, interpretation, and the client-specific tool
interface.

### A.2 Scoring and Uncertainty

The benchmark contains 248 cases from 26 projects, with two option
orders per case. Each medium-effort Project or Options only cell
therefore contains 496 completed responses. We retain the last logged
record for each combination of case, option hash, condition, engine,
requested model, effort, prompt hash, and repeat identifier. This rule
replaces a failed or superseded attempt rather than counting a retry as
another observation. All 29,760 retained runs completed without a
transport error. Eight responses could not be parsed and receive a score
of zero. These are four Sol responses at xhigh, one Sol response at max,
one Terra response at high, one Grok response at xhigh, and one Grok
response after the second review. All eight unparsed responses occur in
project-condition runs. Both medium-effort primary conditions are fully
parsed. Excluding unparsed responses changes any affected cell by at
most 0.23 percentage points.

For each case, we average the two binary scores before estimating
uncertainty. The main intervals use 10,000 percentile bootstrap samples
of 248 cases drawn with replacement. The random seed is 20261004. We
resample the same case indices in both conditions when estimating their
difference. The two option orders are repeated measurements, not
independent cases.

### C.1 Coding the Historical Decision

The codebook describes the original LLM plan and the decision that the
human intervention changed, without using the evaluated models’ answers.
We organize the codebook around three overlapping demands, namely
identifying the research goal, interpreting evidence, and choosing
experiments and resource commitments. The six categories below name the
judgment involved and specify what went wrong in a decision.

- Planning. The scale, training duration, sequence, or parallelization
  of experiments does not fit the question that must be resolved next.
  This includes both launching a full experiment before checking its
  pipeline and using a small experiment that cannot test the proposed
  explanation. Annotators judge scale against the scientific question,
  so a larger experiment can be the appropriate correction.

- Adapting. A relevant result is already available, but the plan does
  not use it to revise a hypothesis, configuration, or priority. The
  annotator must identify the result that should have changed the next
  decision. If the central issue is whether that result is valid,
  anomaly awareness is the more direct category.

- Anomaly awareness. The plan treats an implementation problem,
  measurement defect, confound, or anomalous output as usable scientific
  evidence without an adequate diagnostic step. The issue is whether to
  check the experiment before interpreting its result, rather than
  whether the agent can implement the repair. A correctly executed
  experiment that answers the wrong research question belongs under
  focus.

- Evidence sufficiency. The available evidence does not support the
  decision to declare success, failure, completion, or closure. Examples
  include rejecting several claims after one immature experiment and
  treating a noisy result near a threshold as a decisive verdict. A
  justified negative conclusion or a stop required by an actual budget
  limit is not a failure under this category.

- Repetition. The proposed work reproduces information already available
  or presents reused information as independent new evidence. The
  annotator must explain what remains scientifically unchanged, such as
  the predictions, experimental cell, or estimated quantity. This
  category excludes planned independent replication and a necessary
  rerun under a changed protocol.

- Focus. Even if executed correctly, the proposed experiment would
  answer a different question from the one the project is trying to
  resolve. This includes substituting a proxy for the target, changing
  the wrong experimental variable, or treating an agent’s own assumption
  as a binding human constraint. When a senior researcher introduces a
  new goal, annotators must mark it as a later change rather than
  attribute it to the earlier project state.

### C.2 Boundaries and Review Procedure

The primary error label follows the smallest decision change needed to
correct the original LLM plan. Annotators may add up to two secondary
error labels when these describe distinct errors rather than restating
the same error’s cause and consequence. The six categories have no fixed
precedence. For example, adapting concerns an omitted update, repetition
concerns whether proposed work would add information, and evidence
sufficiency concerns a conclusion or stop already made. A case can
receive a judgment label without a primary error label.

The project record, an agent’s interpretation of it, and the original
human message are separate evidence sources. A project note headed
“human rule” does not by itself establish that a senior researcher
imposed the rule. Outcomes after a human intervention can explain what
happened after the correction, but annotators must not treat them as
evidence visible at the decision point. Related decisions from one human
intervention need not be independent instances of an error.

Annotation status. The historical-error coding assigns a primary error
label to 243 cases. The remaining five cases carry a judgment label
without one. All 248 cases have a judgment label, which names the
category of judgment the case concerns.

The original LLM plan kept a $`3\times 3`$ resolution matrix closed

In a reinforcement-learning project using proximal policy optimization,
the opposite direction was needed. Three seeds gated at 1,000 iterations
could only repeat a known failure with insufficient training and could
not distinguish that problem from sensitivity to the random seed. The
reference plan trained one seed for the full 8,000 iterations of the
historical protocol before adding more seeds. Both cases illustrate
planning decisions, but the appropriate direction of the scale change is
opposite. The 20-then-200 item protocol and the 8,000-iteration budget
are local project requirements, not general optima.

## Appendix D Human Baseline Protocol

Fresh researchers with no prior exposure to the source projects answer
through an interface that presents the two plans and the associated
project record. They learn the project from that record; none of them
supervised the source projects. Thirteen PhD students and postdocs took
part. Each was informed about the study and gave consent. All are
reported under anonymous identifiers. The baseline therefore measures
what a researcher can recover about the senior researcher’s correction
from the project record, as the models do. The interface randomizes
option order. An agent assistant is available to explain the project
record and differences between the plans; the fresh researcher makes the
final choice. The default assistant runs gpt-5.6-terra through Codex,
with Astra and Claude assistants also available.

### D.1 Coverage and Comparison

All 248 cases have recorded human responses. The fresh researchers
answer 182 of the 248 cases correctly, an accuracy of 73.4%. The radar
plots apply this same measure within each scientific field or judgment
category.

## Appendix E Evaluation Prompts and Available Materials

### E.1 Twelve Evaluation Conditions

For each model and case, the evaluation contains ten initial conditions.
These combine the project and options-only conditions with each of the
low, medium, high, xhigh, and max requested effort settings. Two further
conditions continue the medium-effort project session through a first
and then a second reconsideration. Each continuation uses the same
reconsideration prompt below and retains the preceding conversation,
without correctness feedback. We evaluate every condition in both option
orders, giving 24 responses per model and 120 across the five models for
each case.
