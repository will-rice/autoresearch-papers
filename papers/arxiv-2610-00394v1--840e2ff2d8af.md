{metaabstract}

This paper studies _forking_, a generalization failure discovered in
NanoGPT autoresearch. Under data replay, models with an over-encoding
$`n`$-gram memory branch show a sharp separation of training and
validation loss at epoch boundaries, resembling the shape of forks. We
study this phenomenon in a controlled vanilla NanoGPT setting and
reproduce it in a DeepSeek-style model with Engram. Mechanistically,
repeated updates sharpen the continuations observed in training while
suppressing the probability of unseen continuations, whose loss grows
with each pass. The $`n`$-gram module creates weakly interacting
context-specific subspaces, amplifying this effect. Low-frequency
contexts contribute most of the gap, whereas larger training budgets and
heavily crowded tables suppress it. We also observe forking in
short-budget, heavily repeated SFT and RL-like regimes. The
contributions of this paper are twofold: (1) Forking reveals yet another
curious phenomenon in deep learning, in addition to grokking and double
descent. (2) Forking is an unexpected and unpleasant by-product of
tricks proposed by autoresearch agents. While these agents produce an
enormous number of results that seem useful, we should always be careful
with their results. \keywordsdata replay, sudden overfitting,
generalization gap, $`n`$-gram memory, autoresearch

## 1 Introduction

Language-model agents are increasingly used to propose, execute, and
evaluate research ideas. Systems for automated scientific discovery and
model improvement extend this process from individual coding tasks to
iterative experimental search ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib36);
[Recursive, 2026](#bib.bib40)). Such searches can improve a target
metric, but they can also reveal behavior that was not part of the
original objective. Forking is one such discovery from a NanoGPT
autoresearch workflow
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
The figure’s schematic arrows are illustrative; the first-pass traces
come from multi-epoch runs, not a separate no-replay condition.

The agent’s task was to modify the model architecture and trainer to
minimize next-token validation bits per byte (BPB). During a small-data
experiment, an unexpected pattern occurred: training and validation
losses initially improved together, but once the loader began replaying
previously seen text, the curves separated rapidly near the epoch
boundary. Training loss continued to fall, while validation loss stopped
improving or even increased. The gap then grew in steps over subsequent
passes. This replay-aligned divergence is called _forking_ because the
two loss curves resemble a fork
(Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

This behavior resembles overfitting: training loss continues to decrease
while validation loss deteriorates, a pattern previously observed in
language models trained on repeated data ([Hernandez et al.,
2022](#bib.bib19)). However, such a sharp divergence is not an
inevitable consequence of data reuse; previous studies show that
language models can benefit from several passes over a finite corpus
([Muennighoff et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)). Through a series of controlled
experiments, we trace forking to the interaction between data replay and
_over-encoding_, which enriches token representations with additional
embeddings of local token combinations ([Huang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). In our model, an $`n`$-gram module uses the most
recent $`n`$ tokens, including the current token, to retrieve a learned
embedding from a hash-indexed table. This embedding is injected into the
Transformer backbone to help predict the next token. Across the
configurations we test, adding this module produces a pronounced
training–validation gap once the training data are replayed.

To understand why over-encoding promotes overfitting under replay, we
examine how the gap depends on context frequency. Low-frequency
$`n`$-gram contexts account for a substantial share of the additional
gap, and the average gap follows an approximate power-law relationship
with context frequency over the measured range. We show that the
$`n`$-gram table makes optimization local: by assigning distinct learned
representations to different contexts, it lets the model fit each
context’s prediction problem independently, making it easier to memorize
the continuations observed in training. For rare contexts, only a few
valid continuations may be represented in the corpus. Replaying the same
examples reinforces these continuations without expanding their
coverage, potentially suppressing alternatives needed at validation
time. We develop this explanation through frequency-dependent
interventions, a model of continuation coverage, and an analysis of
replay dynamics.

Our main findings are as follows:

1.  1.  Over-encoding amplifies the divergence between training and
        validation loss when training data are replayed.
2.  2.  Incomplete continuation coverage and replay-driven sharpening
        explain why rare contexts exhibit larger gaps.
3.  3.  The gap can grow again during continued training on small replayed
        corpora and in a static RLVR-like proxy, even when the tables are
        frozen.

The paper is organized to follow these three findings.
Section [2](#S2 "2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
establishes the first by recounting the discovery of forking and
reproducing it in a controlled setting;
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G DeepSeek-Style Replication ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
extends the test to a DeepSeek-style architecture.
Sections [3](#S3 "3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
and [4](#S4 "4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
develop the second: the former measures the frequency dependence and
tests interventions, while the latter connects these observations to
continuation coverage and replay dynamics.
Section [5](#S5 "5 Forking in Post-Training ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
examines the third through continued-training experiments and a static

Section [6](#S6 "6 Future Work ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")

## 2 Discovery and Phenomenon

### 2.1 Discovering and localizing forking

Figure 2: Forking and the $`n`$-gram RMSProp update signal near the
first replay boundary (boxed).

As described in
Section [1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"),
forking was discovered during automated NanoGPT research: training and
validation losses separate rapidly when data replay begins, and their
gap grows in steps over subsequent passes. To investigate its origin,
OPHIS, a method that studies training dynamics through observables of
parameters, gradients, and optimizer updates ([Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib52)), was used. Guided by OPHIS, observables were compared
across replay conditions, and some $`n`$-gram update signals associated
with the loss divergence were identified
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Discovering and localizing forking ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay") for
example). An ablation experiment was then conducted in which the bigram
and trigram table contributions were disabled. The table disabling
removed the rapid replay-aligned divergence, and identified the
component to study.
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Localizing the n-gram pathway across controlled replay schedules ‣ Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
gives the discovery setting, screening procedure, and removal
experiment.

### 2.2 Minimal setting

After the discovery-stage signal was localized to the $`n`$-gram
pathway, we reproduced forking with NanoGPT and trainable $`n`$-gram
tables. We take this controlled reproduction as our minimal setting. The
discovery model combined several architectural and optimization choices,
including gated value-path injection and multiple $`n`$-gram tables; the
model below is simpler and distinct from the original autoresearch
configuration in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 The original task and discovery setting ‣ Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").

Figure 3: Three $`n`$-gram injection routes.

We use a NanoGPT backbone with 8 layers, 6 attention heads, a hidden
dimension of 768, a vocabulary of 8192 tokens, and a sequence length of
2048, together with separate bigram and trigram lookup tables. Retrieved
embeddings are injected at one of three routes—token representations
before attention (input), attention values ($`v`$), or attention output
($`y`$)—alongside a no-gram baseline
(Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"));
the input route is the default. The backbone uses AdamW with learning
rate $`6\times 10^{-4}`$; the tables use momentum-free RMSProp at
$`128`$ times the backbone learning rate. Learning rates are warmed up
for 100 steps and then held constant. One epoch over the fixed
single-shard training corpus takes 337 steps, and runs use seed 42
unless stated otherwise.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Minimal Setting, Parameter Choices, and Measurement Conventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
gives the full contract, numerical precision, and implementation
details.

Training loss ($`L_{\mathrm{tr}}`$) is the mean next-token cross-entropy
on the current training batch, computed before the parameter update.
Validation loss ($`L_{\mathrm{va}}`$) is the mean next-token
cross-entropy on fixed held-out validation batches, evaluated after the
update. At each evaluation step, the online gap is
$`G=L_{\mathrm{va}}-L_{\mathrm{tr}}`$.

Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
shows the signature pattern over 2000 updates, with dotted lines marking
the 337-step replay boundaries. During the first epoch, training and
validation losses improve together. Once the same text is replayed,
training loss keeps falling without a corresponding validation
improvement, and the gap widens—not smoothly but in a staircase aligned
with the replay boundaries: most of the damage arrives right after each
epoch transition and accumulates over successive passes. All three
over-encoding routes fork; the attention routes ($`v`$, $`y`$) fork far
more strongly than the input route, while the no-gram baseline stays
small. The phenomenon is not specific to this minimal architecture: a
12-layer DeepSeek-style model with multi-layer Engram input
over-encoding reproduces forking and responds to low-frequency read
masking in the same way
(Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G DeepSeek-Style Replication ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

## 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions

### 3.1 Empirical scaling laws

All experiments below use input injection and, where tables are enabled,
a table learning rate $`128`$ times the backbone learning rate. The
frequency analysis uses seeds 42, 43 and 44; the remaining experiments
use seed 42. All use the online gap of
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").

##### Context frequency.

Enabling both tables ($`2^{20}`$ rows each) and grouping the step-1000
gap by context count $`F`$ in the unrepeated corpus reveals a striking
regularity: the bigram gap follows an approximate power law over three
decades of frequency
(Figure [5(a)](#S3.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
The trigram gap also falls with frequency, with larger departures from a
power law.
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 From the frequency law to novel continuations ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
connects this pattern to the fraction of novel continuations.

##### Table capacity.

Bigger tables make forking worse. Across three decades of table rows
$`R`$ in bigram-only and trigram-only models, the step-1000 gap
generally increases, sublinearly on log-log axes, with the bigram curve
flattening toward the upper end of the measured range
(Figure [5(b)](#S3.F5.sf2 "In Figure 5 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

##### Training corpus size.

Bigger corpora make forking milder. In a trigram-only model with
$`2^{20}`$ rows trained for three epochs, varying the corpus over 20
sizes from 6M to 994M prediction tokens per epoch
($`0.125D_{1}`$–$`20D_{1}`$, where $`D_{1}`$ is the single-shard budget
of 337 batches), the epoch-2 and epoch-3 end gaps fall by more than an
order of magnitude as the corpus grows, reaching 0.13 and 0.22 nats at
$`20D_{1}`$
(Figure [5(c)](#S3.F5.sf3 "In Figure 5 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Two factors can contribute to the decay: a context’s unseen-continuation
mass decreases with its training count
(Appendix [E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Good–Turing estimation and continuation coverage ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")),
and, at fixed table size, a larger corpus spreads each table row over
more distinct contexts, diluting per-context memorization. Both factors
point the same way for production pretraining—a single pass over a
corpus far larger than the memory—where the coverage account predicts
weaker forking; the adverse corner, small corpora replayed for many
passes, is exactly post-training, which we test in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Forking in Post-Training ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").

##### Epoch count.

With the corpus fixed, replay alone drives the growth: over 20 epochs,
epoch-end gaps increase monotonically for the trigram-only and
both-table models while the no-gram control’s gap remains very small by
comparison
(Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")b).
Dense 2000-step traces
(Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")a)
show the growth concentrating at replay boundaries, matching
Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")b.

### 3.2 Causal interventions

Two interventions in the both-table model test the frequency account
directly. Both use the fixed single-shard training corpus, run for 1000
updates, and start the intervention at step 338, after the first epoch;
the two panels of
Figure [7](#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Freezing the tables. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
use independent runs, each with its own unmodified control.

##### Low-frequency retrieval masking.

We count context frequencies in the unrepeated training corpus and mask
the $`n`$-gram contribution whenever $`F\leq\tau`$, applying the rule
separately to the bigram and trigram branches with thresholds
$`\tau\in\{1,2,4,8,200\}`$. The retrieved embedding is set to zero
during both training and evaluation, while the corresponding token
remains in the training loss. Suppressing low-frequency memory access
reduces the gap
(Figure [7](#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Freezing the tables. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")a):
the excess gap is concentrated in rare contexts.

##### Freezing the tables.

We train both $`n`$-gram tables normally during the first epoch, then
freeze their parameters while continuing to update the backbone. A
pronounced training–validation gap still develops after the tables are
frozen
(Figure [7](#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Freezing the tables. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")b):
once the tables hold learned context representations, the backbone alone
can fit them during replay, so freezing the tables does not prevent
forking.

## 4 Mechanism of Forking

The approximate power law in
Figure [5(a)](#S3.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
is our first clue: why should context frequency organize the gap so
simply?

### 4.1 From the frequency law to novel continuations

A rare context has had few chances to reveal its possible continuations.
Call a validation target _novel_ when its context occurs in training but
was never followed there by that target, and let $`f(F)`$ be the
fraction of validation positions at context count $`F\geq 1`$ whose
continuation is novel. Splitting these positions into novel and seen
continuations gives the simple approximation

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\operatorname{gap}_{t}(F)\approx a_{t}f(F)+b_{t}\big[1-f(F)\big],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`a_{t}`$ and $`b_{t}`$ are effective loss differences for novel
and seen continuations, each relative to the training loss in the same
frequency bin. Coverage sets the mixture weights; training determines
the loss penalties. The exact decomposition allows both differences to
depend on $`F`$
(Appendix [E.3](#A5.SS3 "E.3 From novel continuations to the frequency law ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

The novel fraction itself decays approximately as
$`f(F)\propto F^{-\alpha}`$. For bigram contexts, fitting the same 18
frequency bins as
Figure [5(a)](#S3.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
at step 1000 gives exponents $`0.25`$ for the three-seed mean gap and
$`0.23`$ for measured novelty. When the novel term dominates and its
penalty varies slowly, the gap inherits this decay. Good–Turing
estimation and conditional continuation tails connect the novel fraction
to the statistics of the text; the trigram comparison and corrections
from the seen term and frequency-dependent penalties are developed in
Appendix [E.6](#A5.SS6 "E.6 Tests of the coverage approximation ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
The frequency law thus points to continuation coverage. It remains to
explain why replay harms these continuations.

### 4.2 Crowding-out under replay

A finite corpus can underrepresent a continuation: a context whose
population distribution assigns equal probability to $`C`$ and $`D`$ may
appear three times with $`C`$ and once with $`D`$, so fitting the sample
underpredicts $`D`$ on fresh data. But after the first epoch the same
corpus is replayed. Its novel fraction is unchanged, while the gap
continues to grow by comparable amounts over the early epochs
(Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Low-frequency retrieval masking. ‣ 3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay");
Appendix [F.1](#A6.SS1 "F.1 Replay sharpening and crowding-out ‣ Appendix F Mechanistic Experiments and Diagnostics ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Coverage locates the vulnerable positions but does not account for this
time dependence. We therefore hypothesize that each repetition
reinforces seen continuations and progressively suppresses alternatives
in the context-specific subspace.

This would make forking ordinary overfitting confined to very small
problems. For a context $`c_{i}`$, call the next-token logits computed
from it its *context subspace*, and split the vocabulary into the
continuations of $`c_{i}`$ seen in training, those that appear only in
validation, and the rest
(Appendix [E.2](#A5.SS2 "E.2 Crowding-out within a context subspace ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
If training shows a single continuation $`C`$, the loss in that subspace
is $`-\ln p_{C}`$; an idealized step directly in logit coordinates
raises the logit of $`C`$ by $`\eta(1-p_{C})`$ and, through the softmax,
lowers every other logit by $`\eta p_{v}`$. Mass flows from the unseen
continuations onto the seen one, so the seen-to-unseen odds grow by a
factor above one at every epoch with nothing to stop them while
$`p_{C}<1`$ (Appendix Equation \eqrefeq:zt). We call this
*crowding-out*; it provides an idealized account of the growth in the
novel-continuation penalty with replay.

The aggregate measurements show worsening novel-continuation accuracy in
the both-table model while the no-gram control improves; individual
contexts need not sharpen monotonically. Crowding-out also offers an
explanation for continued forking after the tables are frozen
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")):
the context-specific representation remains available, and backbone
updates can continue to favor the observed continuations.

This leaves one question: why does the same replay improve the no-gram
model on novel continuations, while the $`n`$-gram model grows less
accurate? The next experiment shows that the table lets sharpening
proceed independently within each context.

### 4.3 Independency of context subspaces

Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Crowding-out under replay ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
treats each context subspace as evolving on its own. We test this by
ablation: from the start of epoch 2 or 3, we exclude the next-token loss
at a random 2% of training trigram contexts, keeping their inputs and
all parameters trainable, while a disjoint 2% serve as unmasked peers
(Appendix [F.2](#A6.SS2 "F.2 Context locality under loss masking ‣ Appendix F Mechanistic Experiments and Diagnostics ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Once the mask starts, the masked cohort’s mean gap stops growing
(Figure [9(b)](#S4.F9.sf2 "In Figure 9 ‣ 4.3 Independency of context subspaces ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")),
whereas the peers’ and normal training’s jump again at every replay
boundary. In paired one-epoch runs, masking reduces the epoch-2 gap
increase of the selected contexts from $`1.018`$ to $`0.007`$ nats,
while the peers’ increase stays at $`0.997`$–$`0.998`$. Gap growth is
generated inside each context’s own subspace, not imported from the
other 98% of contexts that keep training.

The mass on train-seen continuations shows the same partition
(Figure [9(a)](#S4.F9.sf1 "In Figure 9 ‣ 4.3 Independency of context subspaces ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
In the $`n`$-gram model, unmasked contexts raise it from $`0.43`$ to
$`0.69`$ by epoch 4, while masked contexts end at $`0.40`$. Without the
$`n`$-gram module, masked contexts still gain mass ($`0.22\to 0.39`$,
versus $`0.41`$ unmasked), and replay also raises the validation-only
mass ($`0.013\to 0.06`$). The table therefore partitions optimization
into weakly interacting context subspaces, inside which replay-driven
sharpening proceeds unchecked by other contexts. The shared backbone
still moves individual masked contexts, but it shifts their training and
validation losses together and leaves their average gap flat.

## 5 Forking in Post-Training

Production pretraining rarely replays data: corpora are large enough
that each example is seen once, and
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Empirical scaling laws ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
accordingly shows the gap shrinking as the training corpus grows.
Post-training inverts both conditions. Supervised fine-tuning often
replays small, expensive corpora for several epochs, and
reinforcement-learning methods such as GRPO and PPO revisit the same
prompts across rollout groups and optimization epochs. This section
tests whether forking reappears under exactly these conditions.

Figure 10: Continued training on replayed corpora. Solid lines show
online training loss, dashed lines fixed validation loss, and the lower
row their difference.

##### Continued training on small replayed corpora.

We start from the low-gap $`20\times`$-corpus trigram checkpoint (gap
0.214 at step 20,220), reset both optimizers with a fresh 100-step
warmup, and replay three 333-batch corpora for five epochs: a
same-distribution continuation of the pretraining corpus (ID), Magicoder
(code) ([Wei et al., 2024](#bib.bib45)), and OpenR1-Math (math)
([Hugging Face, 2025](#bib.bib23)). Each corpus is run with the trigram
table trainable or frozen from the start of continued training, while
the backbone remains trainable. With a trainable table the gap reopens
on every source: endpoint gaps are 2.463 (ID), 0.924 (Magicoder), and
0.678 (OpenR1), as shown in
Figure [10](#S5.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 5 Forking in Post-Training ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
Freezing reduces but does not eliminate the gap: the corresponding
endpoints are 0.341, 0.198, and 0.158. Values for ID use seed 42; the
other sources average downstream seeds 42, 43, and 44 from the same
pretrained checkpoint. The full protocol and endpoint table are in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Continued-Training and Replay Stress-Test Protocol ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").

##### Verifier-selected data as an RL proxy.

Verifier-filtered policy optimization revisits prompts across rollout
groups and optimization epochs. As a data-reuse proxy, we replay
RLVR-MATH from the Tulu 3 release ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)),
with the reference answer appended once, using an all-token
language-model objective rather than on-policy reinforcement learning.
The prepared 333-batch pool contains approximately 8.54 raw-source token
cycles per nominal epoch. Mean endpoint gaps are 0.509 (trainable) and
0.434 (frozen), as shown in the RLVR-MATH column of
Figure [10](#S5.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 5 Forking in Post-Training ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay");
the frozen-table residual exceeds that on the three other sources.

These continued-training experiments show that replaying a narrow corpus
can reopen a train–validation gap, and freezing the local memory
mitigates but does not prevent it. They motivate monitoring this
divergence in replay-heavy post-training pipelines.

## 6 Future Work

Forking under over-encoding with data replay is a robust phenomenon, and
its impact is largest in the short-budget, heavily repeated regimes of
SFT and RL. This paper concentrates on the mechanism, which we consider
reasonably complete; the next step is to characterize forking in
production-scale models and to determine which production failures it
accounts for. Such a study is currently limited by the scarcity of open
over-encoding models—the most prominent, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
([DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)), is very recent and very
large—but its importance will grow as over-encoding matures.

Beyond loss regularities, future work should locate the downstream
performance harm of forking and develop ways to avoid it. Masking
low-frequency contexts is a promising candidate. An alternative is to
restrict the over-encoding vocabulary at construction time by BPE-style
aggregation, extrapolating the tokenizer-training algorithm so that
low-frequency n-grams never enter the table; the over-encoding scheme of
SCONE ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)) already adopts this design.

## Appendix A Related Work

##### Over-encoding.

Adding $`n`$-gram embeddings is one way to implement over-encoding. The
Over-Tokenized Transformer adds hashed embeddings of several $`n`$-gram
orders to the input embedding and reports performance comparable to
baselines about twice its size at no additional compute ([Huang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). Related methods construct and use $`n`$-gram
representations in different ways: FastText sums character $`n`$-gram
vectors ([Bojanowski et al., 2017](#bib.bib3)); N-Grammer embeds latent
bigram ids ([Roy et al., 2022](#bib.bib41)); SCONE learns contextual
embeddings for frequent $`n`$-grams and keeps them off-accelerator at
inference ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)); and BLT uses hashed byte
$`n`$-gram embeddings to form patch representations ([Pagnoni et al.,
2025](#bib.bib39)). Engram retrieves hashed $`n`$-gram embeddings at
several layers and gates them into the residual stream, outperforming an
iso-parameter, iso-FLOPs mixture-of-experts baseline ([Cheng et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib9)); an Engram memory with 196B parameters ships in the
open-weight DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ([DeepSeek-AI et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10)). An automated search for NanoChat training recipes
also found hashed bigram and trigram embeddings injected into attention
values ([Recursive, 2026](#bib.bib40)). Beyond these $`n`$-gram
constructions, more general techniques provide ways to represent large
feature sets compactly. Feature hashing maps an unbounded feature space
into a fixed number of rows at the cost of collisions ([Weinberger
et al., 2009](#bib.bib46)), while Hash Embeddings combine shared hashed
components with learned importance weights ([Svenstrup et al.,
2017](#bib.bib42)). In our work, we compare three injection routes under
repeated passes over a fixed corpus and characterize the resulting
forking.

##### Other memory architectures.

Product-key memories ([Lample et al., 2019](#bib.bib33)) and memory
layers at scale ([Berges et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) add large trainable
key–value tables with sparse access, and feed-forward layers can
themselves be read as key–value memories ([Geva et al.,
2021](#bib.bib14)). Non-parametric alternatives interpolate with nearest
neighbors in a datastore ([Khandelwal et al., 2020](#bib.bib30); [Xu
et al., 2023](#bib.bib48)), attend to retrieved chunks ([Borgeaud
et al., 2022](#bib.bib4)), cache recent hidden states ([Grave et al.,
2017](#bib.bib17); [Wu et al., 2022](#bib.bib47)), or query unbounded
$`n`$-gram counts ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)). Memory Grafting
precomputes values for frequent $`n`$-grams with a separate model and
freezes them during pretraining ([Cheng et al., 2026a](#bib.bib8)). An
$`n`$-gram table differs from learned-key memories in that its routing
is fixed by the input tokens rather than learned; we show that this
fixed routing, which gives each context its own coordinates, is what
lets repetition turn into per-context memorization
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Crowding-out under replay ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

##### Training with data replay.

Repeated training can be useful when data are limited: up to about four
epochs of repetition are nearly as useful as fresh data, with rapidly
diminishing returns afterwards ([Muennighoff et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)).
Multi-epoch training is also used in successful fine-tuning recipes;
LIMA trains on 1,000 examples for 15 epochs ([Zhou et al.,
2023](#bib.bib54)).

Other studies document its costs. Repeating a small subset can
disproportionately harm test loss and damage copying and induction-head
circuits ([Hernandez et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)); duplication increases
verbatim extraction, which deduplication reduces ([Carlini et al.,
2023](#bib.bib5); [Lee et al., 2022](#bib.bib34); [Kandpal et al.,
2022](#bib.bib25)). Concurrently with our work, [Jha et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib24) find that mixture-of-experts models overfit repeated
data more than dense models and degrade at lower repetition counts.

Further work suggests that the effects of repetition depend on the
training conditions and the measure of performance. Dataset size, model
size, and training objective affect multi-epoch degradation, and dropout
is the only common regularizer found to help substantially in the study
of [Xue et al. (2023)](#bib.bib49). Hyperfitting reports worse
validation loss but better long-form generation after fitting a small
set to near-zero training loss ([Carlsson et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)).
More generally, neural networks can fit even random labels ([Zhang
et al., 2017](#bib.bib53)); larger language models memorize faster and
forget less ([Tirumala et al., 2022](#bib.bib44)), yet memorization of
specific sequences is hard to predict from smaller runs ([Biderman
et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)). [Howard and Whitaker (2023)](#bib.bib20)
report abrupt decreases in training loss at epoch boundaries, sometimes
accompanied by increasing validation loss, during language-model
fine-tuning. They interpret these patterns in terms of rapid
memorization and overconfidence. Calibration is a separate concern:
neural networks can be overconfident relative to their accuracy ([Guo
et al., 2017](#bib.bib18)). Architecture further changes the response to
replay: in the MoE study, overfitting grows with sparsity as set by
total rather than active parameters, routing stabilizes early, and
expert specialization correlates with overfitting ([Jha et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)). Building on these observations, we investigate
replay-induced train–validation divergence in greater detail and
identify over-encoding as a factor that amplifies it. Through controlled
interventions and context-frequency analysis, we examine how repeated
training sharpens observed continuations while suppressing unseen
alternatives.

##### Natural-language distributions.

Long-tailed distributions make rare events difficult to cover in a
finite sample. Memorizing rare examples can be necessary for
near-optimal generalization under such distributions ([Feldman,
2020](#bib.bib11); [Feldman and Zhang, 2020](#bib.bib12)), while
language models learn knowledge about rare entities poorly ([Kandpal
et al., 2023](#bib.bib26)). Good–Turing estimation infers the
probability of unseen outcomes from singleton counts ([Good,
1953](#bib.bib16); [Gale and Sampson, 1995](#bib.bib13)), and occupancy
theory relates the decay of this missing mass to the tail of the
sampling distribution ([Gnedin et al., 2007](#bib.bib15)). Classical
language models address sparse observations through smoothing:
maximum-likelihood estimates assign zero probability to unobserved
events, while Katz back-off, Kneser–Ney smoothing, and hierarchical
Pitman–Yor models reserve probability mass for them in different ways
([Katz, 1987](#bib.bib29); [Kneser and Ney, 1995](#bib.bib31); [Chen and
Goodman, 1999](#bib.bib7); [Teh, 2006](#bib.bib43)). In our work, we
study these distributional properties at the level of context-specific
continuations, linking poor coverage in rare contexts to forking under
replay
(Appendix [E.3](#A5.SS3 "E.3 From novel continuations to the frequency law ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Low-frequency masking provides a simple analogue of back-off.

##### Automated research.

Automated research spans the evaluation of individual experimental tasks
and the automation of a broader research process. MLAgentBench
benchmarks language-model agents on machine-learning experimentation
([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)). The AI Scientist and subsequent work
extend the scope to idea generation, experiments, and paper writing ([Lu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib36); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib50); [Lu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)). Another line of work focuses on improving
training code under a fixed computational budget. NanoGPT provides a
small language-model training implementation ([Karpathy,
2022](#bib.bib27)), while Autoresearch and Recursive’s searches explore
training recipes using fixed-budget metrics such as validation bits per
byte ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib28); [Recursive, 2026](#bib.bib40)).
These searches offer a setting in which changes to an architecture or
training procedure can be evaluated through repeated experiments. OPHIS
guides such searches with measurements of internal training dynamics
([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)), bringing the behavior of model
components into the investigation alongside the target metric. Forking
was first observed during an OPHIS-guided architecture search on NanoGPT
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Our work illustrates a by-product of automated search: a component
selected for a better target metric under a single-pass budget can fail
under a different data regime. Following this observation, we examine
the failure under replay and use interventions to investigate its
mechanism.

## Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments

### B.1 The original task and discovery setting

NanoGPT RSI searches for changes that reduce validation bits per byte
(BPB). An agent modifies train.py within a fixed compute budget,
evaluates the result, and continues to revise the architecture and
training strategy. The original automated search used a training budget
of approximately five minutes per experiment ([Karpathy,
2026](#bib.bib28); [Recursive, 2026](#bib.bib40)). The small-data
experiment in which forking was discovered instead used 2000 optimizer
steps to inspect a longer trajectory. Forking was an unexpected outcome
of this process.

The text came from the Hugging Face dataset
\hrefhttps://huggingface.co/datasets/karpathy/climbmix-400b-shufflekarpathy/climbmix-400b-shuffle,
using shards 1–10 and 6542. In the discovery-stage split, shards 1–3
were used for training, and shards 4–10 and 6542 for validation. The
loader read and packed text in a fixed order, then restarted after
traversing the training shards. Three shards did not provide enough
unique data for 2000 steps, so the run necessarily entered later epochs.
Training and validation losses initially decreased together. When text
began to repeat, training loss continued to fall while validation loss
flattened or increased, producing a rapid divergence near the epoch
boundary.

The agent-added n-gram module used independent learnable lookup tables
at different layers. Each bigram or trigram context was mapped through
two hashes. The two retrieved half-width vectors were concatenated, then
injected into the attention value branch through an input-dependent
sigmoid gate:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{t}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow v_{t}^{(\ell)}+g_{2,t}^{(\ell)}\odot e_{2,t}^{(\ell)}+g_{3,t}^{(\ell)}\odot e_{3,t}^{(\ell)}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here $`e_{n,t}^{(\ell)}`$ is the concatenated lookup result; the gate is
computed from the corresponding channels of that layer’s input. Hash
constants and table parameters differ across layers. The retrieved
vectors enter the value branch, rather than being added directly to the
token embedding.

### B.2 Localizing the n-gram pathway across controlled replay schedules

We sought observables whose changes consistently tracked forking, so
that their parameter-group attribution could help locate the responsible
component. OPHIS recorded too many observables for manual inspection of
every curve, making an automated screening procedure necessary. Starting
from the single-shard replay experiment, we added controls that changed
when and how training data were reused. We then defined screening rules
from the expected timing and shape of forking in these experiments. Only
observables that passed every rule were retained for manual review.
Finally, we disabled the implicated component to test its contribution
to forking.

#### B.2.1 Four experiments used to screen observables

We varied when training data were first reused and how reuse was
organized, while fixing the model, optimizer configuration, tokenizer,
and step budget. The four experiments distinguish ordinary replay,
delayed replay, random repetition without a full-pass transition, and an
imposed replay cycle.

##### E1 (TR1): sequential replay of one shard.

The loader repeatedly traversed shard 1 in a fixed order. The second
epoch started at step 338, followed by pronounced forking; the third
started at step 687, with a further increase in the gap. This run
supplied the boundary event to be localized.

##### E2 (TR2): sequential replay of two shards.

The loader used shards 1 and 2 before restarting. At approximately step
350, it was still in the first traversal, and the training and
validation curves remained close. The second epoch started at step 683,
and the divergence was delayed accordingly. This control distinguishes a
response to reuse from a change tied only to the absolute training step.

##### E3: random sampling with replacement.

Documents were sampled uniformly with replacement from shard 1, then
tokenized and packed into batches. Repeated documents could occur from
the beginning of training. A gap developed gradually, but there was no
full-pass replay transition near step 350. This supplied a control for
the local boundary event.

##### E4: 50-new / 50-replay.

The loader read 50 consecutive new batches from the shard-1 stream, then
replayed those cached batches in the same order. Training loss fell and
validation loss rose during replay; the curves moved closer when new
data resumed. The resulting cycle of approximately 100 steps provided a
test of whether an observable followed the replay state. Near the end of
a base-stream epoch, a short block was replayed only for its actual
cached length, so the local period could be shorter than 100 steps.

Experiments E1, E3, and E4 used validation shards 2–10 and 6542; E2 used
shards 3–10 and 6542. These validation sets were therefore not
identical. Screening compared the temporal responses of internal
observables; loss curves identified the event within each run. In this
appendix, a step counts completed optimizer updates. The centers 350 and
700 refer to screening windows, while actual epoch boundaries come from
loader logs.

#### B.2.2 Five observable-screening rules

OPHIS records statistics of parameters, gradients, optimizer states, and
update vectors, including their directional relationships ([Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib52)). Candidates were scalar time series shared by the
four runs. We excluded bookkeeping quantities such as data progress,
learning-rate multipliers, throughput, and parameter counts, as well as
some auxiliary fields used to construct other statistics. Window
calculations used finite recorded values without interpolation.

Let $`\mu(X)`$ and $`\sigma(X)`$ denote the mean and population standard
deviation of the recorded values in $`X`$, let $`X\sqcup Y`$ concatenate
both collections, and set $`\varepsilon=10^{-12}`$. To compare windows
or runs with different scales, we used

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D(X,Y)=\frac{|\mu(X)-\mu(Y)|}{\max\{\sigma(X\sqcup Y),\,0.05|\mu(X\sqcup Y)|,\,\varepsilon\}}.
``` |  | (3) |

For a local event compared with a neighboring baseline $`B`$, we used

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(X,B)=\frac{|\mu(X)-\mu(B)|}{\max\{\sigma(X\sqcup B),\,0.05|\mu(B)|,\,\varepsilon\}}.
``` |  | (4) |

The windows were $`W=[325,375]`$, $`L=[255,315]`$, $`R=[385,445]`$, and
the later E2 window $`W^{\prime}=[675,725]`$. Write $`X_{r}(W)`$ for the
values recorded in window $`W`$ of experiment
$`r\in\{\mathrm{E1},\mathrm{E2},\mathrm{E3},\mathrm{E4}\}`$.

##### C1: a within-run change across replay states.

Compare E2 near steps 350 and 700:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{1}=D\bigl(X_{\mathrm{E2}}(W),X_{\mathrm{E2}}(W^{\prime})\bigr)\geq 0.5.
``` |  | (5) |

The early window is within the first traversal; the later one covers the
onset of replay. This rule retains curves that differ across these
states.

##### C2: a cross-run difference at the same training time.

Compare E1 and E2 near step 350:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{2}=D\bigl(X_{\mathrm{E1}}(W),X_{\mathrm{E2}}(W)\bigr)\geq 0.5.
``` |  | (6) |

The runs have the same elapsed optimizer steps and learning-rate
schedule, but only E1 has entered replay.

##### C3: a boundary event stronger than random repetition.

For each run, define

|     |                                                      |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       E_{r}=H\bigl(X_{r}(W),X_{r}(L)\sqcup X_{r}(R)\bigr).
       ```                                                   |     | (7) |

Require

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       C_{3}=\max(0,E_{\mathrm{E1}}-E_{\mathrm{E3}})\geq 0.5.
       ```                                                     |     | (8) |

This retains candidates whose local E1 event exceeds the change at the
same time under random repetition in E3.

##### C4: agreement with the imposed replay period.

Within the first 700 steps of E4, pair all recorded values separated by
exactly 100 steps. Let $`U,V`$ be the paired columns and $`\rho_{100}`$
their Pearson correlation. Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a=\frac{\min\{\sigma(U),\sigma(V)\}}{\max\{\sigma(U\sqcup V),\varepsilon\}},\qquad C_{4}=\max(0,\rho_{100})\min(1,a)\geq 0.15.
``` |  | (9) |

Only pairs with $`t\geq 1`$ and $`t+100\leq 700`$ are used. The score is
zero when fewer than ten pairs are available.

##### C5: a center window distinct from both neighbors.

Require the E1 center to differ from both the preceding and following
windows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{5}=\min\left\{\aligned&H\bigl(X_{\mathrm{E1}}(W),X_{\mathrm{E1}}(L)\bigr),\\
H\bigl(X_{\mathrm{E1}}(W),X_{\mathrm{E1}}(R)\bigr)\right\}\geq 0.5.
``` |  | (10) |

Taking the smaller deviation rejects curves that differ on only one
side.

A candidate had to pass all five thresholds. For manual-review ordering,
the script saturated each score as $`q_{k}=C_{k}/(1+C_{k})`$ and ranked
candidates by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S=100\left[0.7\min_{k}q_{k}+0.3\,\operatorname{mean}_{k}q_{k}\right].
``` |  | (11) |

The windows and thresholds were exploratory heuristics. These scores
narrowed the set for inspection; they were not significance tests or
causal evidence.

#### B.2.3 Manual inspection and component removal

Curves that were identical across the four runs, or differed only by a
fixed scale factor, were merged. Representative statistics were chosen
for clear parameter-group attribution and interpretability. Manual
inspection of the remaining small set highlighted the n-gram table’s
update magnitude and update–parameter direction relationship. The update
magnitude rose after replay began in E1, shifted later in E2, and
followed the alternation between new and replayed data in E4
(Figure [12](#A2.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ B.2.3 Manual inspection and component removal ‣ B.2 Localizing the n-gram pathway across controlled replay schedules ‣ Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
Code inspection confirmed that this logged parameter group consisted of
the bigram and trigram value-embedding lookup tables. The n-gram pathway
therefore became the main candidate for component intervention.

### C.1 Architecture and fixed experimental settings

full causal attention, learned absolute position embeddings,
pre-LayerNorm blocks, and tied token and output embeddings.

##### Why simplify the memory pathway?

The discovery model distributes gated, multi-hash lookup tables across
attention value branches. The minimal model instead adds one full-width
vector per n-gram order directly to the token representation. This makes
the memory pathway easy to remove or change while keeping the backbone
fixed. Bigram and trigram tables retain the two components identified in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Localizing the n-gram pathway across controlled replay schedules ‣ Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay");
separate experiments disable either or both. The input route is the
default, with value and attention-output injection tested as
alternatives in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
The default $`2^{20}`$ rows per table provides a common reference for
comparisons; the capacity sweep varies this quantity explicitly. Here
*clean table* denotes the single-table implementation, not a guarantee
of collision-free lookup.

##### Why fix the data and replay budget?

A single shard permits several complete passes within a short run,
making the onset and subsequent growth of forking observable. Keeping
the tokenizer, sequence length, and batch size fixed preserves the token
and context definitions and the number of prediction tokens per update.
The 1000-step screen spans nearly three passes; 2000-step runs examine
continued replay. Reusing the same held-out batches keeps
validation-sample changes out of the measured trajectory. Seed 42 is the
default reference; additional seeds quantify training-seed variation
only where they were actually run. The model dimensions, batch size,
table size, and default seed are fixed experimental choices, rather than
claims of individually optimal hyperparameters.

### C.2 Selecting the controlled optimization setting

After localizing the n-gram pathway, we selected the controlled
optimization setting in the vanilla architecture of
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
These screens targeted a clear replay gap within a short budget; they
were not validation-loss optimization. The screens use the protocol in
Table [2](#A3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ C.1 Architecture and fixed experimental settings ‣ Appendix C Minimal Setting, Parameter Choices, and Measurement Conventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"):
input injection, clean $`2^{20}`$-row bigram and trigram tables, shard-1
replay, fixed validation, BF16, and seed 42. The backbone uses AdamW
$`(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.8,0.95)`$ with weight decay $`0.1`$; tables
use bias-corrected RMSProp $`(0,0.99)`$ with no weight decay. Both
learning rates warm up for 100 steps from $`0.25\times`$ to their maxima
and then remain constant. The selected maxima are
$`\eta_{B}=6\times 10^{-4}`$ and $`\eta_{T}=128\eta_{B}=0.0768`$.

### C.3 Loss and frequency measurements

The discovery-stage configuration in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 The original task and discovery setting ‣ Appendix B Original Task and OPHIS Experiments ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
and the controlled setting in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
should be distinguished. They use different optimizers and injection
implementations. Frequencies $`F(i)`$ refer to counts in a single
unrepeated training corpus, even when that corpus is consumed many
times.

For online curves, the training loss is obtained from the training
forward pass before the corresponding update, while validation can be
evaluated after that update. A fixed-probe gap instead compares fixed
training and validation batches evaluated at the same checkpoint:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{\mathrm{probe}}(t)=L_{\mathrm{val,probe}}(t)-L_{\mathrm{train,probe}}(t).
``` |  | (12) |

Where a matched no-gram control is subtracted, we explicitly use

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{\mathrm{net}}(t)=G_{\mathrm{ngram}}(t)-G_{\mathrm{nogram}}(t).
``` |  | (13) |

Cross-entropy values are in nats unless stated otherwise. BPB is the
original search metric and should not be numerically equated with token
cross-entropy.

### C.4 Numerical precision and implementation

The main setting uses BF16 autocast for forward computation. Model
weights, n-gram table parameters, and optimizer states remain in FP32.
Cross-entropy is computed from FP32 logits; per-token losses are
returned in FP32 and accumulated in FP64 for frequency-conditioned
diagnostic statistics. The current diagnostic forwards use the same BF16
convention as the main evaluation. Earlier diagnostic runs that used
FP32 forwards retain their recorded precision setting. Asynchronous
aggregation changes execution, not the metric definitions.

FP8 was not used as the baseline. In a setting-validation test, simply
selecting float8_e4m3fn in CUDA autocast did not work on the current
eager nn.Linear/addmm path. FP8 training requires a dedicated
implementation, such as scaled matrix multiplication or Transformer
Engine, together with explicit scaling policies for activations,
weights, and gradients. Precision boundaries would also have to be
specified separately for lookup tables, attention, MLPs, logit
projection, table optimization, and diagnostics.

A historical matched-hyperparameter comparison on an H200 found BF16
approximately 5.3 times faster per step than FP32. At 1000 steps, the
reported raw gaps were 0.825 for BF16 and 0.858 for FP32, with similar
trajectories. This check supported the conclusion that the numerical
format did not remove forking in that setting; it is not a substitute
for the evidence from the current main runs. The default convention
remains BF16 forward computation, FP32 weights and optimizer states, and
no torch.compile. Any future FP8 comparison should report stability,
loss, gap, margins, throughput, and memory use separately.

## Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests

### E.1 Conditional continuation tails and the novel-continuation rate

For a context $`c_{i}`$ with $`F_{i}=F`$, let $`y_{i,(r)}`$ denote the
$`r`$-th most likely continuation and $`p_{i,(r)}`$ its true conditional
probability. Model the observed continuations $`Y_{i,1},\ldots,Y_{i,F}`$
as independent draws, with counts
$`n_{i,(r)}(F)=\#\{k\in\{1,\ldots,F\}:Y_{i,k}=y_{i,(r)}\}`$. The
novel-continuation rate $`f_{i}(F)`$ and its expectation under this
sampling model are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{i}(F)=\sum_{r}p_{i,(r)}\mathbf{1}\left[n_{i,(r)}(F)=0\right],\quad\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(F)]=\sum_{r}p_{i,(r)}(1-p_{i,(r)})^{F}.
``` |  | (14) |

Assume a conditional tail $`p_{i,(r)}\sim A_{i}r^{-s}`$ with $`s>1`$.
For large $`F`$ within the tail’s scaling range, before
finite-vocabulary effects become significant, the dominant probabilities
are of order $`1/F`$. Approximating $`(1-p_{i,(r)})^{F}`$ by
$`e^{-Fp_{i,(r)}}`$ and the rank sum by an integral then gives ([Gnedin
et al., 2007](#bib.bib15))

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\aligned\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(F)]&\sim\int_{1}^{\infty}A_{i}r^{-s}e^{-FA_{i}r^{-s}}\,\mathrm{d}r\\
\sim\frac{A_{i}^{1/s}}{s}\Gamma\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right)F^{-(1-1/s)}\\
\propto F^{-\alpha},\quad\alpha=1-\frac{1}{s}.
``` |  | (15) |

This provides a conditional explanation of the empirical scaling in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 From the frequency law to novel continuations ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
Finite vocabulary limits the power-law regime to an intermediate range.
Heterogeneity across contexts may alter the observed effective exponent.

### E.2 Crowding-out within a context subspace

Fix a context $`c_{i}`$, and let $`a_{v}`$ be the logit of a
continuation $`v`$. Partition the vocabulary into the continuations of
$`c_{i}`$ seen in training ($`\mathcal{S}`$), those that only occur in
validation ($`\mathcal{V}`$), and the rest. With probability
$`p_{v}=\operatorname{softmax}{a_{v}}`$, one can idealize the per-epoch
gradient update on a training target $`C\in\mathcal{S}`$ by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{v}^{(t+1)}-a_{v}^{(t)}=-\eta\frac{\partial\ell_{C}^{(t)}}{\partial a_{v}^{(t)}}=-\eta\left(p_{v}^{(t)}-\mathbf{1}[v=C]\right),\qquad\eta>0,
``` |  | (16) |

where the training loss is $`\ell_{C}^{(t)}=-\ln p_{C}^{(t)}`$.
$`a_{C}^{(t)}`$ is raised by $`\eta(1-p_{C}^{(t)})`$ and every other
logit is lowered by $`\eta p_{v}^{(t)}`$. For any
$`u\notin\mathcal{S}`$, the ratio $`z(t)=p_{C}^{(t)}/p_{u}^{(t)}`$
satisfies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z(t+1)=z(t)\exp\left[\eta\left(1-p_{C}^{(t)}+p_{u}^{(t)}\right)\right]>z(t).
``` |  | (17) |

Therefore, under repeated updates on the same context and seen target,
the probability ratio of the seen continuation $`C`$ relative to any
unseen continuation $`u`$ increase. In particular, validation-only
continuations $`u\in\mathcal{V}`$ become progressively less probable
relative to $`C`$. This provides an idealized mechanistic explanation
for the crowding-out effect described in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Crowding-out under replay ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").

### E.3 From novel continuations to the frequency law

This subsection derives the affine relation of
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 From the frequency law to novel continuations ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
(Equation \eqrefeq:coverage-main). Fix a training step $`t`$ and group
prediction positions by the training count $`F\geq 1`$ of their context.
Within a frequency bin, let $`f(F)`$ be the fraction of validation
positions whose true continuation never followed that context in
training. The token itself may be common; what training never showed is
the context–continuation pair. The gap splits exactly into two terms:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\aligned\operatorname{gap}_{t}(F)&=f(F)D_{t}(F)+\big(1-f(F)\big)S_{t}(F),\\
D_{t}(F)=\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{\mathrm{novel},t}(F)-\ell^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}(F),\qquad S_{t}(F)=\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{\mathrm{seen},t}(F)-\ell^{\mathrm{tr}}_{t}(F).
``` |  | (18) |

Here $`D_{t}`$ is the excess loss of novel continuations over the
training loss (the novel-continuation penalty of
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Crowding-out under replay ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"))
and $`S_{t}`$ is the corresponding seen term; both compare a validation
loss with the same online training loss. All three losses are bin means
at the same logged step and are not matched context by context. Coverage
sets the mixture weights, and the model determines the two loss
differences.

If both differences vary little with $`F`$, the decomposition is
approximately affine in the novel fraction:

|     |                                                   |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{gap}_{t}(F)\approx A_{t}f(F)+B_{t}.
       ```                                                |     | (19) |

In the constant-penalty approximation of
Equation \eqrefeq:coverage-main, $`A_{t}=a_{t}-b_{t}`$ and
$`B_{t}=b_{t}`$. When the seen term is also negligible, it reduces to
$`\operatorname{gap}_{t}(F)\approx D_{t}f(F)`$: the gap is the fraction
of positions exposed to crowding-out times their loss penalty. The
affine relation describes the measured bins well
(Figure [19](#A5.F19 "Figure 19 ‣ E.3 From novel continuations to the frequency law ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")b,c).
Its intercept $`B_{t}`$ need not equal the seen term, because
frequency-dependent components can also produce a close fit
(Appendix [E.6](#A5.SS6 "E.6 Tests of the coverage approximation ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).

Good–Turing estimation connects the missing continuation mass to
singleton counts in the corpus
(Appendix [E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Good–Turing estimation and continuation coverage ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")).
For a fixed context with a conditional tail
$`p_{i,(r)}\sim A_{i}r^{-s}`$, the expected missing mass decays as
$`F^{-(1-1/s)}`$ over an intermediate range
(Equation \eqrefeq:occupancy), which gives $`f`$ a slow, approximately
power-law decay. Pooling heterogeneous contexts and a nonzero intercept
both change the slope that the gap inherits
(Equation \eqrefeq:coverage-effective-exponent).

counts (Equation \eqrefeq:gt-bin) versus the measured novel fraction;

### E.4 Good–Turing estimation and continuation coverage

Good–Turing estimation relates unseen probability mass to observed
singletons ([Good, 1953](#bib.bib16); [Gale and Sampson,
1995](#bib.bib13)). For context $`i`$, let $`p_{iy}`$ be its
continuation distribution and $`m_{i}(F)=\sum_{y}p_{iy}(1-p_{iy})^{F}`$
its expected missing mass after $`F`$ independent draws; this is the
expected novel-continuation rate $`\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(F)]`$ that
Equation \eqrefeq:occupancy approximates. If $`K_{i,1}(k)`$ counts
continuation types occurring exactly once in $`k`$ draws, then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathbb{E}K_{i,1}(F+1)}{F+1}=\sum_{y}p_{iy}(1-p_{iy})^{F}=m_{i}(F).
``` |  | (20) |

This is an expectation identity for a fixed context. The unshifted ratio
$`K_{i,1}(F)/F`$ has expectation $`m_{i}(F-1)`$ and equals one at
$`F=1`$ by construction. Pooling the contexts observed $`F`$ times
requires a model of how context counts arise, given next.

##### Heterogeneous contexts and token weighting.

Assume training context counts $`N_{i}`$ are Poisson with means
$`\lambda_{i}=Tq_{i}`$, continuations are conditionally independent, and
an independent validation token has context distribution $`q_{i}`$ and
the same conditional probabilities $`p_{iy}`$. Write
$`\pi_{F}(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{F}/F!`$. Conditional on the
validation context having training count $`F`$, its population novelty
probability is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\star}(F)=\frac{\sum_{i}q_{i}\pi_{F}(\lambda_{i})m_{i}(F)}{\sum_{i}q_{i}\pi_{F}(\lambda_{i})}.
``` |  | (21) |

Define $`n(F)=\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[N_{i}=F]`$ and
$`N_{1}(F)=\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[N_{i}=F]K_{i,1}(F)`$. Using
$`(F+1)\pi_{F+1}(\lambda)=\lambda\pi_{F}(\lambda)`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\aligned\mathbb{E}N_{1}(F+1)&=T\sum_{i}q_{i}\pi_{F}(\lambda_{i})m_{i}(F),\\
(F+1)\mathbb{E}n(F+1)=T\sum_{i}q_{i}\pi_{F}(\lambda_{i}),\\
f_{\star}(F)=\frac{\mathbb{E}N_{1}(F+1)}{(F+1)\mathbb{E}n(F+1)}.
``` |  | (22) |

This is a ratio of expectations under the sampling model; the
corresponding ratio of observed counts is a plug-in estimate of it. For
a bin $`B`$ of exact counts, the train-only estimator is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{GT}}(B)=\frac{\sum_{F\in B}N_{1}(F+1)}{\sum_{F\in B}(F+1)n(F+1)}.
``` |  | (23) |

Its pooling weights use training counts only, so the estimate uses no
validation data.

##### Corpus check.

We count contexts in training shard 1 (49,716,936 tokens) and measure
novelty directly on 49,102,848 prediction positions of held-out shard 2.
Training counts are taken over the concatenated token stream; validation
contexts follow the model’s 2049-token chunks. In the 18 frequency bins
with $`1\leq F\leq 1000`$, Equation \eqrefeq:gt-bin gives 0.870–0.885
times the measured novel fraction for bigram contexts and 0.857–0.937
for trigram contexts, with no rescaling fitted on validation data
(Figure [19](#A5.F19 "Figure 19 ‣ E.3 From novel continuations to the frequency law ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")a).
At $`F=1`$ the measured novel fractions are 0.790 (bigram) and 0.780
(trigram), against estimates of 0.699 and 0.669. The estimator therefore
tracks the frequency dependence of novelty closely but underestimates
its level. A similar underestimate appears when the training shard
itself is split in two, so a shift between the training and validation
shards is not its only source; natural text also departs from the
Poisson and independence assumptions.

which differs from the within-model train–validation gap of
Equation \eqrefeq:gap-decomp. In particular, they include the $`F=0`$
positions, which the frequency-bin analysis excludes because no training
counterpart exists.

##### Corrections to the leading power law.

Writing $`D_{t}(F)=a_{t}+\delta D_{t}(F)`$ and
$`S_{t}(F)=b_{t}+\delta S_{t}(F)`$ in the exact decomposition gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{gap}_{t}(F)=(a_{t}-b_{t})f(F)+b_{t}+f(F)\delta D_{t}(F)+[1-f(F)]\delta S_{t}(F).
``` |  | (24) |

If $`f(F)\approx cF^{-\alpha}`$, the first term is a power law. The
seen-term offset and the frequency-dependent remainder need not share
its exponent or be small. These terms allow coverage to track the gap
even when their fitted exponents differ.

##### Effective exponent.

If $`f(F)=cF^{-\alpha}`$ exactly and $`\operatorname{gap}=Af+B`$ with
$`A`$ and $`B`$ fixed across $`F`$, the local exponent of a positive gap
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}(F)=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\operatorname{gap}}{\mathrm{d}\log F}=\alpha\,\frac{Af(F)}{Af(F)+B}.
``` |  | (25) |

It equals $`\alpha`$ only when the intercept is negligible; a positive
intercept flattens the gap and a negative one steepens it. Because
$`A_{t}`$ and $`B_{t}`$ change with training step
(Table [6](#A5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Fit quality. ‣ E.6 Tests of the coverage approximation ‣ Appendix E Mathematical Derivations and Model Tests ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"))
and with table capacity, the observed gap exponent can change while the
corpus stays fixed. These tests cover one corpus, optimizer and
injection path, and within that setting support coverage as a
contributor to the frequency dependence of the gap.

## Appendix F Mechanistic Experiments and Diagnostics

### F.1 Replay sharpening and crowding-out

The experiments in this section examine whether replay increasingly
favors continuations observed in training, as proposed in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Crowding-out under replay ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
The temporal motivation is that the corpus remains fixed while the gap
keeps growing. In the 20-epoch both-table experiment with seed 42, the
total online gaps at the ends of epochs 1–4 are $`-0.062`$, $`1.199`$,
$`2.730`$ and $`3.917`$ nats. Their increments are comparable early in
training and later decline, reaching $`0.241`$ nats from epoch 19 to 20.
This is a clue about the model’s response to replay, rather than
evidence for a constant increase in novel-continuation loss.

#### F.1.1 Experimental setup and primary observations

We use checkpoint probes to examine individual continuation
distributions and fixed-validation evaluation to measure their aggregate
behavior. The diagnostics examine models with both bigram and trigram
tables and, where available, a no-gram control. All runs discussed here
use seed 42. A training epoch contains 337 optimizer updates; the five
epoch-end checkpoints are at steps 337, 674, 1011, 1348 and 1685. The
input and aggregation conventions of each diagnostic are specified
below.

##### Aggregate validation diagnostics.

#### F.1.3 Variation across repeated runs

Our training runs do not enforce deterministic floating-point execution,
so fixing the seed does not ensure identical trajectories. In seed-42
reruns, training loss already differed by about $`10^{-5}`$ nats at step
2. A repeated context-only probe of *walk their dog*, whose training
continuation is *down* and whose validation continuations are *daily*
and *for*, retained the overall decrease in training-continuation loss
and increase in validation-continuation loss, but the intermediate token
probabilities were not identical and included reversals. Small numerical
differences can therefore accompany different trajectories for an
individual continuation, even when the overall trend is preserved.

#### F.2.1 Experimental setup and original observations

parameters are retained, and the training loss is averaged over the
remaining positions. A disjoint 2% of contexts (set $`B`$) serves as
unmasked peers, and each intervention is paired with normal training
from the same checkpoint.

#### F.2.2 Further analysis and interpretation

Two separate one-epoch experiments examine whether stable average gap
implies stable predictions for individual contexts. They sample 2% of
exact training trigram contexts uniformly without replacement (selection
seed 20260919). Of 379,789 selected contexts, 5,565 have
fixed-validation support, covering 8,083 positions; a disjoint peer
sample has 5,571 measurable contexts and 8,082 positions. Losses
preserve full causal prefixes and are recomputed at a common checkpoint
over each context’s training and validation occurrences. We report the
equally weighted mean of these per-context gaps; the separately computed
contribution in Equation \eqrefeq:context-gap-contribution instead uses
$`q_{i}=N_{\mathrm{val},i}/589{,}824`$, without renormalizing within a
cohort.

The epoch-2 experiment compares normal and masked training over steps
337–674; the epoch-3 experiment makes the same comparison over steps
674–1011. Both use seed 42. Within each pair, the two conditions begin
from the same checkpoint and retain the original batch-token loss
denominator. These are separate experiments, not segments of the
four-epoch trajectories in
Figure [9](#S4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 4.3 Independency of context subspaces ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
Their settings follow
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Minimal Setting, Parameter Choices, and Measurement Conventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"),
with two $`2^{20}`$-row tables, backbone LR $`0.0006`$ and RMSProp table
LR $`0.0768`$.

For epoch 2, the selected contexts’ mean gap increases by $`1.018`$ nats
under normal training and by only $`0.007`$ with masking; the peer
increments remain $`0.997`$ and $`0.998`$. For epoch 3, the
corresponding selected-context changes are $`0.869`$ and $`-0.065`$
nats, while the peer changes are $`0.859`$ and $`0.856`$. These
comparisons support suppression of average additional gap accumulation.
The masked gap can still rise temporarily within an epoch, and its
pre-existing value does not disappear.

Average stability does not imply that individual predictions remain
fixed. In the masked epoch-3 run, mean training and validation losses
both increase, by $`0.445`$ and $`0.380`$ nats. Their similar changes
produce the small net gap change. The mean absolute per-context gap
change is $`0.710`$ nats in epoch 2 and $`0.737`$ in epoch 3, much
larger than the signed averages; individual contexts move in both
directions. Changes also vary across frequency groups, with sparse
support at high frequencies. Shared-backbone updates still move
individual masked contexts, but they shift training and validation
losses together, so the average gap of the masked cohort stays flat.

A variant of the intervention masks 2% of trigram-table rows instead of
context types. In a seed-42 comparison with masking starting from epoch
2, the online masked-cohort gap contribution is $`0.0353`$ nats at step
670, versus $`0.0247`$ under normal training. With masking from step
675, the corresponding contributions are $`0.0551`$ versus $`0.0492`$ at
step 790. These quantities use the online training mean, whereas the
figure and paired analyses use fixed probes. Under fixed replay, a
single online value depends on where the logged window falls within the
epoch, so we also average the online contribution over each epoch,
omitting short boundary windows. In the four-epoch runs of
Figure [9](#S4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 4.3 Independency of context subspaces ‣ 4 Mechanism of Forking ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"),
this epoch mean for the masked cohort is $`0.0094`$, $`0.0076`$ and
$`0.0059`$ nats over epochs 2–4 when masking starts at epoch 2, and
$`0.0277`$ and $`0.0241`$ over epochs 3–4 when it starts at epoch 3;
under normal training it rises from $`0.0080`$ to $`0.0263`$ to
$`0.0401`$. On this measure, the masked cohort stays close to normal
training during its first masked epoch, and the suppression appears in
later epochs. The row-masking runs end with their first masked epoch,
before this stage, which is consistent with their small differences from
normal training.

## Appendix G DeepSeek-Style Replication

### G.1 Experimental goal

Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Minimal setting ‣ 2 Discovery and Phenomenon ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
and
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Minimal Setting, Parameter Choices, and Measurement Conventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
isolate forking in a minimal Transformer with a simple n-gram memory
pathway. Here we ask two further questions: does replay-aligned
train–validation divergence also occur when memory is embedded in a more
structured architecture, and does suppressing low-frequency retrieval
still reduce it? We therefore train a compact DeepSeek-style model from
scratch, retaining MLA-lite attention, mixture-of-experts (MoE)
feed-forward layers, four-stream manifold-constrained hyper-connections
(mHC), and Engram retrieval and fusion ([Cheng et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib9)). This experiment tests the phenomenon in their
combined setting.

### G.2 Model architecture and memory pathway

The model is a 12-layer decoder with hidden width 768. Engram modules
act at layers 2 and 6, before the attention and feed-forward sublayers.
Each module retrieves bigram and trigram features from the input token
sequence and adds their fused representation to the residual streams. In
the terminology of this paper, this is multi-layer input over-encoding.
Table [7](#A7.T7 "Table 7 ‣ G.2 Model architecture and memory pathway ‣ Appendix G DeepSeek-Style Replication ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
specifies the architecture used by all three runs.

##### How memory enters the model.

The concatenated lookup vectors are projected to a shared value and
stream-specific keys. Each gate compares the RMS-normalized key and
hidden state of its stream, applies a signed square-root transform to
their scaled dot product, and then a sigmoid. The gated value and its
causal convolution are added to that stream before attention. Thus the
retrieved signal depends on both the local input context and the
evolving hidden state, unlike the direct addition of a full-width
embedding in the minimal model.

##### Relation to the minimal setting.

We keep the hidden width, vocabulary, sequence length, batch size, data
replay, and optimization protocol aligned with
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Minimal Setting, Parameter Choices, and Measurement Conventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay").
Relative to the minimal model, the architecture increases depth from 8
to 12 layers and introduces MLA-lite attention, SwiGLU/MoE feed-forward
layers, rotary positions, mHC residual connections, and untied
embeddings. The memory pathway uses compressed multi-head retrieval with
gates and convolution. All architecture settings remain fixed across the
three conditions; model and memory capacities differ from those of the
minimal model.

### G.3 Data, optimization, and intervention protocol

##### Data and training budget.

We reuse the pretokenized shard-1 corpus of the minimal setting in a
fixed order. Its 24,264 packed rows each contain 2049 tokens, yielding
2048 next-token predictions per row. An update uses 72 rows, or 147,456
prediction positions, so one pass takes exactly 337 updates and contains
49,692,672 prediction positions. Training lasts 2000 updates with model
and data seed 42, reaching partway through the sixth pass. The eligible
validation shards are 2–10 and 6542; evaluation always uses the first
four batches of shard 2, totaling 589,824 prediction positions. Reusing
this fixed pool removes changes in validation sampling from the curves.

The shared optimization settings test whether the established replay

onset and persistence of forking. Holding the learning rates constant
after warmup also separates replay boundaries from learning-rate
changes.

##### Three comparison conditions.

The full-memory run retains every retrieval throughout training. Two
further runs apply the low-frequency read-mask intervention of
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Causal interventions ‣ 3 Empirical Laws and Interventions ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay"),
here using thresholds $`\tau=8`$ and $`\tau=100`$. Both masks remain
inactive for the first 337 updates and activate before update 338, the
first update of the second pass. For each order $`n\in\{2,3\}`$, all
eight retrieved head vectors are set to zero when the raw input context
has training frequency $`F_{n}\leq\tau`$, including unseen contexts with
$`F_{n}=0`$. Frequencies count model-input contexts in one unrepeated
training pass, using the same raw-token index and sequence-boundary
convention as the minimal-setting intervention. The same rule acts at
both Engram layers during training and evaluation, after table lookup
and before the key/value projections. All next-token loss terms, table
parameters, optimizer states, projection biases, gates, and convolution
operations are retained.

##### Measurement and comparison.

Each condition is trained independently from initialization with seed
42. Every 10 updates, we record the current-batch training loss before
the update and the fixed-pool validation loss after it. The online gap
is $`G(t)=L_{\mathrm{val}}(t)-L_{\mathrm{train}}(t)`$, measured in nats.

### G.4 Observed forking and response to masking

##### Forking recurs under replay.

Figure [22](#A7.F22 "Figure 22 ‣ Forking recurs under replay. ‣ G.4 Observed forking and response to masking ‣ Appendix G DeepSeek-Style Replication ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
shows that the full-memory model initially improves both training and
validation loss. Once the data repeat, training loss separates from
validation loss. Validation loss continues to improve during the second
pass, reaching its lowest recorded value near update 670, before rising
in later passes. These later passes produce the staircase pattern seen
in the minimal setting: training loss drops near replay boundaries while
validation loss rises, causing repeated increases in the gap. At update
2000, training loss is 1.689 nats and validation loss is 5.005 nats,
giving a gap of 3.316 nats. Forking therefore remains observable with
multi-layer, gated Engram retrieval in this combined architecture.

Figure 22: Forking and low-frequency retrieval masking in the
DeepSeek-style model. Left: current-batch training loss before each
logged update (solid) and fixed validation loss after it (dashed).
Right: their difference. Dotted lines mark completed 337-update passes;
masks activate before update 338. All three independent seed-42 runs

##### Low-frequency masking attenuates the divergence.

At update 2000, masking $`F\leq 8`$ reduces the gap to 1.835 nats, and
masking $`F\leq 100`$ reduces it to 0.817 nats. These are reductions of
44.7% and 75.3% relative to full memory. The smaller gaps accompany
lower absolute validation losses, by 1.067 and 1.600 nats respectively,
as well as higher training losses. Thus the intervention both weakens
fitting to replayed training data and improves held-out prediction in
these runs. It also allows validation loss to continue decreasing over
the first few replay epochs.

### G.5 Conclusions and evidence limits

The experiment supports two conclusions within this small-data replay
regime. First, forking extends beyond the minimal additive-memory
Transformer to the tested DeepSeek-style architecture. Second, its
response to low-frequency read masking is consistent with the
minimal-setting evidence that access to rare contexts contributes to
replay-associated overfitting. Forking persists with the richer
retrieval and fusion pathway in this configuration.

We also attempted a no-gram control using the same backbone architecture
and optimization settings. The training loss became non-finite during
the first pass, and this failure recurred in repeat runs. We therefore
could not obtain a completed no-gram baseline of our model for
comparison.

## Appendix H Continued-Training and Replay Stress-Test Protocol

The trainable- and frozen-table runs in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Forking in Post-Training ‣ Forking: Sudden Overfitting Under Replay")
start from the checkpoint-materialization rerun of the full-pool
$`20\times`$ trigram experiment, with endpoint gap $`0.214`$ at step
20,220. The original scientific run has the same setting and endpoint
gap $`0.220`$ but does not retain final weights. We restart the backbone
AdamW and table RMSProp states and apply a fresh 100-step warmup.

The model is the vanilla 8-layer, 6-head, 768-dimensional NanoGPT with
input trigram injection and a clean $`2^{20}`$-row table. We use BF16
forward computation, no compilation, backbone learning rate
$`6\times 10^{-4}`$, table learning rate $`0.0768`$, and fixed
validation. Each continued-pretraining source has 333 batches and is run
for five epochs (1665 updates); logs are recorded every 10 steps and at
the final boundary. Each batch holds 72 sequences with 2048 prediction
targets, and validation uses four fixed batches. The online gap is
$`G=L_{\mathrm{va}}-L_{\mathrm{tr}}`$, where training loss is measured
on the current batch before the update and validation loss is measured
after the update on fixed batches. In the frozen arm the table receives
no updates from the first continued-training step, while the backbone
remains trainable.

batches form the training pool and the next four batches the validation
pool. Neither pool is cycled, and one source record in each dataset
straddles the boundary between the two pools. All sources are encoded
with the tokenizer used in pretraining.
````
