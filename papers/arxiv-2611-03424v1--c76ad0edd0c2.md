# IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?

Shanshan Zhong¹ Chen Xu¹ Jingjie Ning¹ Zhengzhong Liu² Aviral Kumar¹
Chenyan Xiong¹  
¹Carnegie Mellon University ²Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial
Intelligence

###### Abstract

Research taste, the judgment of what to read, which problems to pursue,
and which methods to try, increasingly shapes the work of AI scientists,
yet it remains far less understood than their ability to execute
research. We introduce IdeaTaste, a benchmark that scores an LLM by how
closely the research proposals it writes from a literature snapshot
frozen at 2025 match the work the same communities published in 2026.
Our experiments span six contemporary language models and four research
areas from established to emerging fields, covering more than 90,000
proposals in total. LLM agents fall well short of human communities in
both the literature they cite and the ideas they state, reaching about
26% of the human level on citation matching and only 18% on idea
matching. Their populations can cite as diversely as a community and
take up its tasks and methods, yet rarely arrive at the concrete
approaches and research plans it pursues. Neither richer field context
nor larger populations substantially close this gap. In blinded expert
review, AI proposals trail the 2026 papers least in presentation and
most in expected success, echoing their gap in concrete approaches, and
those whose ideas no human paper shares fare worst, suggesting weaker
directions rather than overlooked opportunities. These results indicate
that the main bottleneck in AI research taste lies less in finding the
directions a community works on than in turning them into the concrete
approaches it goes on to pursue. We release IdeaTaste as an open,
continuously updated leaderboard
([https://cxcscmu.github.io/IdeaTaste](https://cxcscmu.github.io/IdeaTaste))
that benchmarks contemporary AI proposals against future publication
cycles.

## 1 Introduction

AI scientists can now carry out much of the research process on their
own, from generating ideas and grounding them in prior work to running
experiments and writing papers ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Yamada et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib71); [Baek et al.,
2025](#bib.bib5)). Frontier laboratories are deploying them at growing
scale ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib51); [Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib52);
[Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib50)),
and their work has begun to enter the scientific literature ([Tang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib48); [Bianchi et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)). Yet
executing research well matters only when the research itself is worth
doing, and this choice rests on research taste, the judgment of what to
read, which problems to pursue, and which methods to try. As AI
scientists take on a larger role in discovery, their taste increasingly
shapes the research that comes next, but it remains far less understood
than their ability to execute.

Evaluating research taste is challenging because it requires a reference
for which choices are worth making. Existing work mostly judges one idea
at a time, through expert ratings ([Si et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Si et
al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), benchmarks with fixed targets ([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Qiu et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)), or model judges that predict which directions will
matter ([Tian et al., 2026](#bib.bib37); [Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib38);
[Tong et al., 2026](#bib.bib39)), and such judgments are not yet
reliable ([Sinhahajari et al., 2026](#bib.bib43); [Ho et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41); [Baig et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)). Population-level
studies find that generated ideas are highly similar within and across
models ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Wenger and Kenett,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib45)), but they measure
how much a population repeats itself rather than whether its choices
resemble those of human researchers. What is still missing is a direct
comparison between the research choices of AI scientists and those of
the human community working in the same area.

![Refer to caption](2611.03424v1/introduction.png)

Figure 1: Overview of IdeaTaste. The literature corpus is frozen through 2025. In the upper branch, the human research community goes on to
publish 2026 papers that cite work in the corpus. In the lower branch, a
population of LLM agents retrieves from the corpus and writes research
proposals. Matching compares the two branches, and diversity compares
proposals with one another.

The work a community goes on to publish provides such a reference, since
it records which of the many possible directions its researchers
actually chose. Published papers have already been used to score single
ideas by their later impact and to reconstruct a held-out paper from its
bibliography ([Jiang, 2026](#bib.bib35); [Chen et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib34)). We extend this reference from single papers to
entire communities and introduce IdeaTaste, a benchmark that scores an
LLM by how closely the population of proposals it writes matches a
research community. As shown in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
IdeaTaste freezes the literature of a research area through 2025, lets
LLM agents, standing in for the ideation stage of AI scientists, write
research proposals from this snapshot, and compares them with the papers
the same community published in 2026. A proposal that no 2026 paper
shares may still be a good direction the community has not yet taken, so
we also test, through blinded expert review, whether such proposals are
overlooked opportunities or weaker ideas.

Since new research both builds on prior work and recombines it into new
ideas ([Uzzi et al., 2013](#bib.bib53); [Foster et al.,
2015](#bib.bib54)), we compare proposals and papers at two levels. The
literature they cite reflects what they read, and the ideas they state
reflect which problems they pursue and which methods they propose. At
each level, matching measures agreement with the human papers and
diversity measures how varied the proposals are, and both are read
against the level the 2026 papers reach among themselves. The benchmark
covers four areas ranging from established to emerging fields, namely
machine learning systems, code generation, world models, and automated
research. Four methods differ in what an LLM agent knows about its area,
from nothing beyond the corpus to a single topic ([Lu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)), a map of the whole area, and the proposals its
population wrote in earlier rounds ([Schmidgall and Moor,
2025](#bib.bib4)). We benchmark six recent language models under each
method, two of which were trained before 2026 and thus cannot have seen
any reference paper.

Across more than 90,000 proposals, LLM agents fall well short of the
research taste of human communities. Citation matching reaches about 26%
of the human level on average and idea matching only 18%, and no
population reaches half of it on either metric. The shortfall lies in
what LLM agents propose more than in what they read. Given a direction,
their populations can cite as diversely as a community and take up its
tasks and methods, yet their proposals meet human papers far more often
on problems and motivations than on concrete approaches and research
plans. The gap also resists simple remedies. Models trained before 2026
match more human ideas than later ones that may have seen the reference,
richer field context changes how widely a population spreads far more
than how closely it follows human research, and populations up to 17
times larger level off well below the human level. In blinded expert
review, AI proposals trail the 2026 papers least in presentation and
most in expected success, echoing their gap in concrete approaches, and
those whose ideas no human paper shares fare worst, suggesting weaker
directions rather than overlooked opportunities. The main bottleneck in
AI research taste thus lies less in finding the directions a community
works on than in turning them into the concrete approaches it goes on to
pursue. We release IdeaTaste as an open, continuously updated
leaderboard that scores proposals written today against the research
communities publish next. Our main contributions are as follows.

- •
  We introduce IdeaTaste, a benchmark that evaluates the research taste
  of LLM agents against the papers human communities publish after a
  frozen literature snapshot, scoring matching and diversity on cited
  literature and stated ideas across four research areas.
- •
  Through experiments with six language models and four methods,
  covering more than 90,000 proposals, we find that LLM agents reach
  only a small fraction of the human level, lag more on ideas than on
  citations, and gain little from possible exposure to the reference,
  richer context, or larger populations.
- •
  Through direction coverage, slot-level decomposition, and blinded
  expert review, we show that LLM agents reach the directions a
  community pursues far more readily than the approaches it proposes,
  and that proposals missing the human record are weaker rather than
  merely different.

## 2 Related Work

##### AI scientists and research agents.

AI scientists are now built to run much of the research loop, from idea
to experiment to written paper ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Yamada et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib71)), and the space is
growing quickly ([Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Wei et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)). Agent Laboratory executes a human-provided idea,
Co-Scientist refines hypotheses through a team of agents ([Schmidgall et
al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), SciAgents
and the Virtual Lab carry multi-agent designs into materials and
biomedical problems ([Ghafarollahi and Buehler, 2025](#bib.bib6);
[Swanson et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)), and CycleResearcher adds automated
review ([Weng et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)). Evaluation has largely followed
one unit, a single system, even as agent-written work begins to enter
the record from deployments at scale and a conference with AI authors
and reviewers ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib48); [Bianchi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49); [Zhong et al., 2026b](#bib.bib12)). This paper moves
up a level, to a population of LLM agents proposing research from one
shared literature.

##### Automated idea generation.

Within such systems the proposal comes from an ideation step, where
methods turn retrieved prior work into problems, hypotheses, and
experiment designs ([Baek et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Hu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)), and related work forecasts active topics from a
knowledge graph ([Krenn et al., 2023](#bib.bib27); [Gu and Krenn,
2025](#bib.bib28)). Ideation has also been run at population scale, on a
shared archive or inside a simulated research community ([Schmidgall and
Moor, 2025](#bib.bib4); [Yu et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)), where outputs
turn out to be substantially similar within and across models ([Jiang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Wenger and Kenett, 2025](#bib.bib20)), with
further work relating this to knowledge, group size, and crowding
against human output ([Deng et al., 2026](#bib.bib46); [Chen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib45); [Azad and Baten, 2026](#bib.bib47)), as agent
societies outside research also develop shared conventions and
collective bias ([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib17); [Ashery et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18)) and agent ecosystems concentrate into homogeneous
offerings ([Ling et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). Such comparisons are mostly
internal to the population. This study measures that similarity in the
literature a proposal cites and in the idea it states, and pairs each
with agreement against human papers.

##### Evaluating research ideas.

Evaluation supplies the external reference such comparisons lack.
Experts judge one idea at a time, though the initial advantage of model
ideas narrowed once executed ([Si et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Si et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)), while benchmarks score ideas against fixed targets
([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Qiu et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Chen
et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), often leaving the verdict to model judges
([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib29); [Huang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib25)),
now asked which directions will matter ([Tian et al., 2026](#bib.bib37);
[Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Tong et al., 2026](#bib.bib39)). Those
verdicts are not yet reliable, as judges disagree with experts on
novelty ([Sinhahajari et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), trail them at
comparison ([Baig et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)), and rate unsound ideas as
sound ([Ho et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)), while human review is itself
inconsistent ([Cortes and Lawrence, 2021](#bib.bib40)). Published
research offers a steadier reference, used to score ideas by future
impact ([Jiang, 2026](#bib.bib35)), to recover a held-out paper ([Chen
et al., 2026c](#bib.bib34)), and to profile ideas against a taxonomy on
which model output is narrower ([Chen et al., 2026d](#bib.bib36)),
though citation lists are an imperfect standard ([Sahu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib44)). This paper extends that reference from single papers
to what a research community published after its literature was frozen,
comparing that with what a population cites and proposes.

## 3 IdeaTaste

IdeaTaste evaluates the research taste of LLM agents by comparing the
proposals they write from a frozen literature with the papers a human
community later publishes.
Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Data Construction ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
builds the literature corpus and the human reference,
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Evaluation Design ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
defines four metrics on what each side cites and proposes, and
Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
describes the methods.

### 3.1 Data Construction

We cover four research areas: machine learning systems and code
generation, established fields with large literatures, and world models
and automated research, smaller fields that are still emerging. For each
area, we split the literature at the end of 2025. Papers published
through 2025 form the corpus $`U`$, which LLM agents search and propose
from. Papers published in 2026 form the human reference $`H`$, which
records what the community actually pursued after this cutoff. The
cutoff can be moved forward as new papers appear.

Both sets are collected by following citations from 20 to 22 landmark
papers hand-picked for each area. The corpus first takes in papers
published through 2025 that cite a landmark and then adds every paper
cited by at least two of these citing papers. This second step brings in
shared background that may not use the terminology of the area, such as
program synthesis work for code generation. The human reference combines
2026 papers that cite a landmark with papers returned by topic search,
screens them against a written area definition, and removes surveys. It
covers January to August 2026, and
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 Data Construction ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
reports the sizes of both sets.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Data Construction Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
gives the area definitions, the landmarks, further construction details,
and a coverage audit against authoritative surveys.

### 3.2 Evaluation Design

Writing a research document, whether a human paper or an LLM agent
proposal, involves two choices, selecting the prior work to build on and
formulating an idea in relation to it. LLM agents make these choices
using only the frozen corpus $`U`$, while the human reference $`H`$
records the community’s subsequent choices after the cutoff. We
therefore compare $`H`$ with the proposals $`A`$ of a population at the
two corresponding levels ([Uzzi et al., 2013](#bib.bib53); [Foster et
al., 2015](#bib.bib54)). The resulting information asymmetry across the
cutoff is discussed in
Appendix [E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Leakage and Benchmark Robustness ‣ Appendix E Additional Analyses ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
At the citation level, each document $`d\in H\cup A`$ is represented by
its citation set $`\mathcal{C}(d)`$, the papers it cites that lie in
$`U`$. At the idea level, it is represented by a summary that a language
model extracts from its abstract under a fixed schema of five slots
$`\mathcal{I}`$, namely contribution type, problem, motivation, proposed
approach, and research plan, as detailed in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
At each level, matching measures agreement with the human reference and
diversity measures how varied the proposals are, giving citation
matching Match-Cite, citation diversity Diversity-Cite, idea matching
Match-Idea, and idea diversity Diversity-Idea. All four lie in
$`[0,1]`$, and higher values mean closer agreement with the human
reference or greater variety. Applying them to $`H`$ itself, with each
human paper compared only with the other human papers, gives the human
level against which populations are read.

#### 3.2.1 Level 1: Literature Selection

##### Match-Cite.

We compare a human paper $`d_{h}\in H`$ and a proposal $`d_{a}\in A`$ by
the Jaccard similarity of their citation sets,
$`J(d_{h},d_{a})=|\mathcal{C}(d_{h})\cap\mathcal{C}(d_{a})|/|\mathcal{C}(d_{h})\cup\mathcal{C}(d_{a})|`$,
and match each document with its closest counterpart on the other side:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
J_{A\to H}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum\nolimits_{d_{a}\in A}\max_{d_{h}\in H}J(d_{h},d_{a}),\qquad J_{H\to A}=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum\nolimits_{d_{h}\in H}\max_{d_{a}\in A}J(d_{h},d_{a}).
``` |  | (1) |

$`J_{A\to H}`$ measures whether each proposal cites like some human
paper, which a population can achieve from a narrow corner of the
literature. $`J_{H\to A}`$ measures whether the sources of each human
paper appear among the proposals, which requires the population to
spread across the literature. Match-Cite is
$`M_{\mathrm{cite}}=2J_{A\to H}J_{H\to A}/(J_{A\to H}+J_{H\to A})`$,
which is high only when both are.

##### Diversity-Cite.

Citation diversity asks whether proposals read enough without returning
to the same sources. It is the product $`D_{\mathrm{cite}}=Q\,S`$ of the
sufficiency $`Q`$ and the spread $`S`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum\nolimits_{d_{a}\in A}\min\left(|\mathcal{C}(d_{a})|/\tau,1\right),\qquad S=\left|\bigcup\nolimits_{d_{a}\in A}\mathcal{C}(d_{a})\right|/\sum\nolimits_{d_{a}\in A}|\mathcal{C}(d_{a})|,
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\tau`$ is the mean citation set size of the human papers in the
area. See
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Citation Sufficiency Threshold ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
Because $`Q`$ is capped at one, citing more than a typical human paper
earns nothing, and $`S`$ falls as proposals cite the same papers.
$`D_{\mathrm{cite}}`$ is thus high only when proposals both read enough
and read differently.

#### 3.2.2 Level 2: Idea Formulation

A language-model judge compares two idea summaries slot by slot, with
$`b_{i}(d,d^{\prime})\in\{0,1\}`$ recording whether $`d`$ and
$`d^{\prime}`$ agree on slot $`i\in\mathcal{I}`$. To keep judging
feasible, each $`d`$ is compared only with $`\mathcal{N}_{X}(d)`$, its
five nearest other documents in a pool $`X`$ under hybrid sparse and
dense retrieval over titles and abstracts, and
$`m_{i}(d,Y)=\max_{d^{\prime}\in Y}b_{i}(d,d^{\prime})`$ indicates
whether slot $`i`$ of $`d`$ is matched within a set $`Y`$. As most paper
pairs share a contribution type but few share a research plan, each slot
is weighted by its rarity, $`w_{i}=-\log_{2}p_{i}`$ for $`p_{i}>0`$,
where $`p_{i}`$ is the fraction of human papers $`d_{h}`$ whose slot
$`i`$ is matched within $`\mathcal{N}_{H}(d_{h})`$. With the weights and
their sum $`W`$ fixed per area,
$`s(d,Y)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}w_{i}\,m_{i}(d,Y)/W`$ is the weighted
fraction of slots of $`d`$ matched within $`Y`$.

##### Match-Idea.

A slot of a human paper counts as covered when at least one proposal
retrieved for it matches on that slot, and Match-Idea is the weighted
fraction of human slots covered.

##### Diversity-Idea.

Applying the same matching within a population, we compare each proposal
with its peers
$`\mathcal{P}(d_{a})=\mathcal{N}_{A}(d_{a})\cup\{d_{a^{\prime}}\in A:d_{a}\in\mathcal{N}_{A}(d_{a^{\prime}})\}`$,
namely the proposals it retrieves and those that retrieve it, and
Diversity-Idea is one minus the weighted fraction of its slots that some
peer shares. Both idea metrics are thus averages of $`s`$, taken over
human papers against the proposals and over proposals against their
peers:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{\mathrm{idea}}=\sum\nolimits_{d_{h}\in H}s\big(d_{h},\mathcal{N}_{A}(d_{h})\big)/|H|,\qquad D_{\mathrm{idea}}=1-\sum\nolimits_{d_{a}\in A}s\big(d_{a},\mathcal{P}(d_{a})\big)/|A|.
``` |  | (3) |

Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
gives the retrieval and judging configurations that both idea metrics
depend on, and validates the judge against human annotators.

### 3.3 Methods

All four methods share one LLM agent implementation and differ only in
what it knows about its area. The LLM agent receives the area definition
used to screen the human reference and writes one proposal within a
fixed step budget, taking one action per step: planning, searching the
corpus, pinning retrieved papers, revising the draft, compressing its
history into notes, or submitting. It can cite only retrieved papers, so
its citations always lie in $`U`$. The methods vary its profile and
actions:

- •
  free only states that the LLM agent is a researcher in the area,
  revealing what it pursues without an explicit prior.
- •
  topic assigns one research topic and leaves the choice of problem open
  ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), testing how a broad direction shapes
  proposals. Topics are allocated in proportion to corpus frequency,
  with recent years upweighted.
- •
  landscape assigns no topic but lists the area’s most common task tags,
  method tags, and task–method pairs with their shares among 2025
  papers, plus an action to retrieve papers by tag or pair. It thus
  exposes the area’s structure and how crowded each direction is, which
  researchers weigh when choosing between established and new directions
  ([Foster et al., 2015](#bib.bib54)).
- •
  frontier lets LLM agents see one another’s work ([Schmidgall and Moor,
  2025](#bib.bib4)). It assigns topics like topic but runs in rounds,
  freezing each round’s proposals and research traces into a snapshot
  that later rounds query through two extra actions, as researchers read
  peers’ work. Peer records are separate from literature retrieval,
  uncitable, and marked as not scientifically validated.

These methods test whether an LLM agent’s view of its area changes the
literature and ideas its population selects. See
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Method Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
for more details.

## 4 Experiments

##### LLM agents.

We run the four methods of
Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
in every area with the six language models in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
Each LLM agent has a budget of 20 steps, and each population holds 300
proposals, which frontier writes in two rounds of 150. The six models
form two groups by design. Four were released in 2026 and power current
AI scientists, so their training data may overlap the human reference,
but leaving them out would confine the benchmark to outdated models. The
other two were trained before 2026 and cannot have seen any 2026 paper,
so their results are free of leakage by construction. Comparing the two
groups tests whether possible exposure to the reference helps LLM agents
anticipate 2026 research.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Method Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
gives implementation details.

##### Automatic evaluation.

All populations are scored with the metrics of
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Evaluation Design ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
and scoring the 2026 papers against one another gives the human level.
The slot-level judge is GPT-5.6 Luna, outside the evaluated model
families and validated against human annotators in
Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").

##### Human evaluation.

Beyond whether LLM agents propose what the community pursues, we use
human evaluation to examine how good their proposals are, and in
particular whether those without a human counterpart are weak directions
or sound ones the community has not yet taken. Eleven reviewers, all
with reviewing experience, assess 192 documents without knowing their
source. The documents are split evenly between proposals and 2026 papers
and balanced across areas and models. Half come from matched pairs, each
a proposal and the 2026 paper it overlaps under the judge of Match-Idea,
and the other half have no counterpart on the other side. Each document
receives two reviews. Besides the dimensions of a standard machine
learning conference review form, reviewers rate feasibility and expected
success, since an idea can look original yet fail in execution ([Si et
al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), and guess whether the document is AI-generated.

details the design.

##### Main results.

Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
scores every population on the four metrics against the human level, and
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Human evaluation. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
reports how reviewers rate a blinded sample of proposals and 2026
papers. Together they yield four findings.

- •
  LLM agents fall far short of the research taste of human communities.
  On average, LLM agents reach about 26% of the human level on
  Match-Cite and only 18% on Match-Idea, and no population, under any
  model or in any area, reaches half of it on either metric. The gap is
  narrowest in automated research, the newest and smallest area, and
  widens as the area grows, peaking in machine learning systems and code
  generation. LLM agents thus track a community most closely where it
  has least to choose from and fall furthest behind where it has most.
- •
  The gap lies in what LLM agents propose, not in what they read. For
  five of the six models, matching falls further behind the human level
  on ideas than on citations, and diversity sharpens this split. Once
  topic or frontier assigns a direction, four of the six models cite up
  to three times as diversely as the community, yet no population
  reaches the human level of idea diversity. A population can thus read
  as widely as a community and still repeat itself in what it proposes,
  so drawing on the literature a community builds on is not enough to
  arrive at the ideas it pursues.
- •
  Knowing more, from training or from context, does not close the gap.
  Although four of the six models may have seen the 2026 papers in
  training, the two trained before 2026 match more human ideas, about
  26% of the human level against 14% as
  Appendix [E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Leakage and Benchmark Robustness ‣ Appendix E Additional Analyses ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
  details. Idea matching thus follows neither recency nor capability, as
  Llama 4 Scout matches the most human ideas yet receives the lowest
  ratings in
  Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Human evaluation. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
  More context does no better. Across the four methods, matching stays
  within a narrow band while citation diversity varies about threefold,
  so what an LLM agent is told shapes how widely its population spreads
  far more than how closely it follows human research. The fullest view
  helps least, as landscape ranks last on Match-Cite in 19 of 24
  settings, while the direction of topic and the peer proposals of
  frontier broaden a population without bringing it near the human
  level.
- •
  The quality gap centers on execution and deepens where proposals miss
  the human record. In blind review, AI proposals score below the 2026
  papers on every quality dimension, by 20 points on expected success
  but only 4 on presentation, so the gap lies less in how the proposals
  are written than in whether they would work. Reviewers also flag 86%
  of them as AI-generated, against 39% of the papers. Even
  DeepSeek-V4-Flash, which surpasses the papers on soundness,
  presentation, and contribution, trails them in feasibility and
  expected success. Split by match, both groups of proposals trail the
  papers by the same 11 points in overall rating, but those with no
  human counterpart fall much further behind in execution, as the gap
  widens from 8 to 15 points in feasibility and from 15 to 26 in
  expected success. The proposals that miss the human record are thus
  the ones reviewers expect least to succeed.

Together, these findings show that LLM agents come closer to a community
in what they read than in what they propose, that neither newer models
nor richer context closes this gap, and that the proposals outside the
human record read to reviewers as weaker directions rather than
overlooked opportunities. Low matching thus reflects a deficit in
research taste rather than a mere difference in preference.

examines which part of an idea LLM agents miss and whether scale
recovers it.

## 5 Analysis

The main results show that LLM agents fall furthest behind on idea
matching. We trace where this gap lies, first across research
directions, then within the idea summary and in concrete cases, and
finally ask whether larger populations close it.

##### Do LLM agents cover the human research directions?

Low idea matching may arise because a population never takes up the
directions the 2026 papers pursue, or because it takes them up and still
arrives at different ideas. To tell these apart, we treat a direction as
a task paired with a method, that is, what a paper works on and how, and
label both sides with the task and method vocabulary that landscape
draws on. We run this comparison for DeepSeek-V4-Flash in automated
research, with each population holding as many proposals as the area has
papers so that both sides are compared at equal size.
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Do LLM agents cover the human research directions? ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
reports the share of human tag mass each population reaches. On each
axis alone, the populations given a direction come close to the
community, and frontier misses only a small share of the human task mass
and of the method mass. On pairs, every population falls away, and even
frontier reaches only about half of the human mass, so LLM agents work
on the community’s tasks with its methods but combine the two
differently. Neither kind of coverage, however, carries over to ideas.
Frontier reaches about twice the human pair mass of free but matches
barely more human ideas in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
where the four methods sit within a narrow band. LLM agents thus reach
the directions a community pursues and still propose something else, so
what they miss lies inside a direction rather than in the choice of one.

To locate the difference inside an idea, we decompose Match-Idea into
the slots of the idea summary.

##### What do LLM agents propose instead?

Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ What do LLM agents propose instead? ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
pairs each of two human papers with two proposals from one language
model that share its problem. In both cases, the proposal that misses
the human approach leaves the trained network unchanged and adds
machinery around it, whereas the human paper and the matching proposal
both modify the network itself. The Case 1 paper fine-tunes the router
toward expert reuse, while proposal A predicts expert demand and
prefetches experts around an unchanged network. The Case 2 paper updates
low-rank adapters on a frozen network, while proposal A keeps the
weights fixed and adapts through retrieved transitions. The same problem
thus admits very different mechanisms, and a shared problem does not
imply a shared approach.

##### Can more LLM agents close the gap?

If the gap stemmed mainly from population size, sampling more proposals
should close it. We grow the free and topic populations of Gemini 3.5
Flash-Lite and DeepSeek-V4-Flash in every area from 300 proposals to the
size of the human reference, 3.8 to 17 times larger, and report matching
in
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Can more LLM agents close the gap? ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
Matching grows far more slowly than the population. Every curve rises
only gently and levels off as proposals accumulate, staying well below
the human level even when a population holds as many proposals as the
community holds papers. An assigned direction helps somewhat, as topic
climbs more steeply on ideas than free in most settings, yet both remain
far from the human level. The gap is thus not an artifact of population
size. Together with the analyses above, this shows that neither reaching
more of a community’s directions nor writing more proposals brings LLM
agents closer to the approaches the community arrives at. Improving
research taste therefore means improving how a recognized problem
becomes a concrete approach, not how widely or how much a population
proposes.

Flash-Lite and DeepSeek-V4-Flash from top to bottom, and columns are the

level, circles for Match-Cite and squares for Match-Idea.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced IdeaTaste, a benchmark that measures the research taste of
LLM agents against the research human communities go on to publish. Our
results point to a consistent picture. LLM agents share much of a
community’s literature and directions, yet seldom arrive at the concrete
approaches it pursues, and the proposals that depart from human research
tend to be weaker rather than overlooked. This picture holds across
newer models, richer context, and larger populations. The research taste
of LLM agents is thus limited less by knowing what to work on than by
deciding how to pursue it, which we see as the central challenge for
future AI scientists.

## Appendix A Data Construction Details

Each area is represented by two sets of papers: a corpus $`U`$ of work
published through 2025, which is what LLM agents retrieve from, and a
human reference $`H`$ of papers from 2026, which is what proposals are
measured against. The two are built to different standards. The corpus
has to be wide enough that the background a 2026 paper builds on lies
inside it, and it must carry nothing from 2026. The reference set has to
be as complete as we can make it and has to hold research in the area.
This appendix describes the area definitions, the sources data come
from, data construction details, and the survey reference coverage
audit.

### A.1 Research Areas

Each area is defined in writing before collection begins, and that
definition does not change afterwards. It states what belongs to the
area and what does not, and it is what every screening step below
applies. The LLM agents receive the same text as the description of
their research community, so both sides work under one definition of the
area.
Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.1 Research Areas ‣ Appendix A Data Construction Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
gives the four definitions together with the topic search queries used
to collect the reference set.

### A.2 Data Collection

Collection has to satisfy one requirement: the corpus must hold the
earlier work a 2026 paper builds on. We therefore run a single expansion
out of hand-picked landmark papers along citation edges, and a paper’s
publication year decides which set it joins, the corpus through 2025 and
the reference set in 2026. The reference set carries a second
requirement, since it is the record every metric is read against.
Research the community did that we fail to collect is invisible to the
scores, and a proposal that reached it would count as reaching nothing,
so the set has to be as complete as we can make it. We therefore reach
it by topic search over the area as well as by citation, and screen what
both paths return against the area definition.

##### Landmark papers and citation expansion.

Each area starts from 20 to 22 landmark papers chosen by hand, listed in
Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Landmark papers and citation expansion. ‣ A.2 Data Collection ‣ Appendix A Data Construction Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
The corpus then grows in two steps. Descendants are papers published
through 2025 that cite at least one landmark paper. Ancestors are papers
cited by at least two distinct descendants. The second step brings in
shared background that may not use the area’s terminology, and the
threshold of two is what keeps it from pulling in everything a
descendant happens to cite. Nothing on this side is screened for the
area, since the corpus is a pool to retrieve from and a paper outside
the area costs little as long as the background is there.

### A.4 Interpretation of the Human Reference

The reference we score against is the set of 2026 papers in each area.
By 2026 some of these papers are written with model assistance, and many
are preprints that no venue has reviewed, so the set is not
peer-reviewed, purely human work. This does not weaken its role in the
benchmark as a measure of human research activity. Every paper in the
reference was released by its authors. Releasing a paper is a human
decision: the authors judged the problem worth pursuing and the work
worth putting on the record. That judgment is what we compare against,
and it stands whether or not a model helped write the text and whether
or not a venue has reviewed it. What we compare against is research
taste, i.e., the problems and ideas the authors chose. Whether a 2026
paper is strong or weak therefore does not change the measurement, since
a weak paper still marks a direction a human researcher chose to pursue.
What the benchmark asks is whether an LLM agent population proposes the
problems and ideas that human researchers pursued, that is, whether it
can reach ideas at the human level.

### A.5 Interpretation of research taste.

IdeaTaste uses research taste in a descriptive sense. A research
community faces many plausible problems and methods but chooses to
pursue only a small subset. We therefore treat papers released after the
cutoff as observations of these realized research choices. The human
reference captures which prior work researchers build on, which problems
they pursue, and which approaches they develop. It does not assume that
every published paper is high quality or that publication defines
scientific value. Accordingly, MATCH-CITE and MATCH-IDEA measure how
closely an LLM agent population anticipates the subsequent choices of
the corresponding human community. High matching does not by itself
imply higher research quality, and low matching does not by itself imply
lower quality. The benchmark measures agreement with realized community
choices rather than a normative standard of scientific merit. The
blinded expert study helps interpret the gap beyond matching alone. A
non-matching proposal could represent a valuable direction that the
human community did not pursue. However, non-matching AI proposals are
judged less feasible and less likely to succeed than non-matching human
papers. This suggests that the observed gap is not explained only by
different research preferences. We therefore view IdeaTaste as measuring
one operational aspect of research taste: whether LLM agents anticipate
the research choices that a human community later makes.

### A.6 Interpreting Corpus Size

Corpus ($`\leq 2025`$) size follows from how the citation expansion runs
rather than from how large an area is. Code generation ends with the
largest corpus of the four and a smaller reference set than machine
learning systems, and both follow from the same cause. Several code
generation landmarks are cited across natural language processing,
semantic parsing, and software engineering, so their descendants reach
well beyond the area, while the machine learning systems landmarks are
cited mostly inside the systems community. Ancestors are drawn from
those descendants, so the difference compounds at the second step. For
the reference set the same breadth works the other way, since screening
for the area removes the general natural language processing papers that
cite those landmarks. A corpus size therefore records how widely an
area’s research is built on elsewhere, not how much work the area itself
produces.

### A.7 Remaining Collection Gaps

Three gaps remain that no source we tried closes.

- •
  The corpus records still without an abstract are older closed-access
  work that none of the indexes or publisher services carries.
- •
  Every index also lags on recent preprints, which thins the most recent
  layer of the corpus and leaves a small share of reference papers
  without a parsed reference list; those papers stay in the reference
  set and are left out of the denominator when we report how citations
  resolve into the corpus.
- •
  A preprint that nothing has cited yet may fall outside both collection
  paths, since a paper is reached either by a citation from inside the
  collection or by an index filing it under the area.

problem, motivation, proposed approach, and research plan. A slot whose
content the abstract does not state is recorded as ”None”. The language
model we use for schema extraction is DeepSeek-V4-Pro. We use a strong
model as we want to maximize the accuracy of the extracted schemas. The
prompt is given in

note the shortfall in quality_note. Never repair, sharpen, or complete
the paper. An invented scope, baseline, metric, or failure condition is
a far worse error than a thin field.

mostly not-stated; that is the correct output, not a failure.

(a) Anonymity. Do NOT copy any name this paper coins for its own

### B.3 Candidate Retrieval, Idea Matching, and Slot Weights

##### Retrieval.

Retrieval runs over the title and abstract of each research document and
combines a sparse and a dense channel. The sparse channel is BM25 with
$`k_{1}=1.5`$ and $`b=0.75`$. The dense channel encodes the same text
with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 ([Reimers and Gurevych, 2019](#bib.bib55)) and
ranks by cosine similarity. The two rankings are fused over the top 200
of each channel, where a document placed $`r`$th in a channel
contributes $`1/(60+r)`$, and the five highest fused documents become
$`\mathcal{N}_{X}(d)`$.

##### Pairwise idea matching.

Each pair is judged once. The two summaries are presented in a random
order, which removes a systematic position preference. The judge rules
on every slot separately and also returns an overall verdict on whether
the two documents state the same idea. The prompt is given in
Figure [8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.2 Idea Schema and Extraction ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
The judge is a language model, GPT-5.6 Luna, which is strong enough to
read the summaries and apply the rules.

##### Slot weights.

Weights come from the human reference for each research area. For each
human 2026 paper we ask whether any of the other human 2026 papers
retrieved for it matches on slot $`i`$; $`p_{i}`$ is the fraction of
human papers with such a match, and $`w_{i}=-\log_{2}p_{i}`$. A slot
that two papers share by default therefore carries almost no weight,
while a slot that rarely matches carries more. The weights and their sum
$`W`$ are estimated once per area and held fixed across methods.

##### Sampling design.

The study covers automated research and uses 40 judged summary pairs
containing 160 slot pairs. Half are a proposal against a 2026 paper,
taken from the Match-Idea judgments, and half are a proposal against
another proposal of the same population, taken from the Diversity-Idea
judgments. Sampling at random would leave little to compare, since the
judge finds agreement on the whole idea in fewer than one in a thousand
of the Match-Idea pairs it rules on, so nearly every drawn pair would be
an easy disagreement. We instead stratify on what the judge decided:
pair type crossed with its decisions on problem and motivation gives
eight cells, and we draw five pairs from each. The sample then realizes
14 of the 16 possible combinations of the four decisions an annotator is
asked to repeat, and is close to balanced on each of them as shown in
Table [13](#A2.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").

##### Judge consistency and agreement with annotators.

Three annotators, a master’s student, a PhD student, and a PhD in
computer science, each answer all 40 pairs, so every slot decision
carries three independent human answers, which we reduce to a majority
label. To gauge the judge’s stability, we run it five times on the same
pairs.
Table [13](#A2.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")

runs agree, and how closely it agrees with the annotators’ majority. The
five runs agree 77–$`90\%`$ of the time, so the verdict is largely
reproducible. The judge also tracks the annotators’ majority closely on
the problem and the proposed approach, while the more interpretive
motivation and research-plan slots run lower. On most slots this
alignment matches or exceeds the agreement among the annotators
themselves, so the judge reads a pair about as consistently as a human
would.

### B.5 Human Evaluation of Idea Quality

This appendix describes the questionnaire of
Sec. [4](#S4.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Main results. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"):
what a rater sees, how the sample is composed, who rated it, and the
comparison split by overlap with the human record.

##### Questionnaire.

Figure [10](#A2.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
shows one example. The left pane presents the work as its title,
abstract, and the structured summary of
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Evaluation Design ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
the five slots contribution type, problem, motivation, proposed
approach, and research plan. The right pane collects the ratings:
soundness, presentation, and contribution on the review form’s
four-point scale, feasibility on a five-point scale from not at all to
easily, expected success as one of five probability bands, an overall
rating from strong reject to strong accept, a confidence from one to
five, and a blinding check of real paper, AI-generated, or unclear.

##### Sampling design.

The questionnaire holds 192 research documents, split evenly between LLM
agent proposals and 2026 papers so the blinding check cannot be won by
favoring the more common label. Each area contributes 48 documents and
each model 16 proposals, drawn from the free, topic, and landscape. The
documents come in four kinds, shown in
Table [14](#A2.T14 "Table 14 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"):
an AI proposal that overlaps a 2026 paper, the paper it overlaps, an AI
proposal that overlaps no candidate paper, and a 2026 paper that no
proposal overlaps. Overlap is decided by the slot-level judge of
Match-Idea. Among the overlapping proposals, some match their paper on
the whole idea and some only on the problem, and both are kept.

##### Reviewer recruitment and block assignment.

The documents are split into six blocks of 32, each covering the four
areas and a subset of the models. A proposal and the 2026 paper it
overlaps are placed in different blocks, so no human reviewers see both
and a paired comparison cannot turn into a within-reviewer ranking; the
paired comparison is therefore made across reviewers, with a reviewer
random effect in the analysis. Each reviewer takes one block one time
and rates all 32 documents one time. One reviewer may take multiple
blocks and record their background and experience as shown in
Figure [11](#A2.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?").
We only record reviewers who have review experience. We finally
recruited 11 reviewers and collected two records for each block, that
is, two review records for each research document.

##### Rating scales and score normalization.

As shown in
Figure [10](#A2.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ Judge consistency and agreement with annotators. ‣ B.4 Human Evaluation of the Idea Judge ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
review design mainly follows the official review form of a major machine
learning conference and scores an idea on seven dimensions.

- •
  Soundness, Presentation and Contribution use four levels (1 = poor, 2
  = fair, 3 = good, 4 = excellent).
- •
  Feasibility uses five levels (1 = not at all, 5 = easily).
- •
  Expected success uses five probability bins ($`<`$10%, 10–30%, 30–50%,
  50–70%, $`>`$70%), coded 1–5.
- •
  Rating uses six levels (0 = strong reject, 2 = reject, 4 = marginally
  below, 6 = marginally above, 8 = accept, 10 = strong accept).
- •
  Confidence uses five levels (1 = unable to assess, 5 = absolutely
  certain).
- •
  Blinding check asks whether the reviewer thinks the entry is a real
  2026 paper, an AI-generated proposal, or they cannot tell.

For each population we average the raw scores of a dimension and map the
mean linearly onto $`[0,100]`$ as shown in Table
[3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Human evaluation. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"):
$`s=100\times\frac{\bar{x}-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}`$, where
$`x_{\min}`$ and $`x_{\max}`$ are the lowest and highest levels of that
dimension’s scale.

## Appendix C Method Details

All four methods run the same loop. This section specifies that loop and
then the profile and actions each method changes.

### C.1 Shared Agent Loop

For LLM agents, an agent harness provides the interface through which a
language model observes and acts in an environment. Our harness follows
ReAct ([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib58)), interleaving reasoning, actions,
and observations in the model’s prompt, similar tool-based interaction
loops supporting research planning in Idea2Plan ([Huang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib57)), idea generation in The AI Scientist-v2 ([Yamada et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2)), and skill learning ([Zhong et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib24)). In our setting, one run of the LLM agent within
this harness produces a single proposal. The harness provides the agent
with a profile, a step budget, and a set of available actions. The agent
follows the ReAct loop, deciding for itself which actions to take and
how to spend the budget.

##### Actions.

Every response carries exactly one tagged action, and every step costs
one unit of a budget of at most 20 model calls. plan records the current
working hypothesis and replaces the previous plan. search takes a query
with an optional publication-year filter and runs the hybrid retrieval
above over the area’s corpus. read_papers pins retrieved documents into
a working set of 50 cards, evicting the least recently requested card
when the set is full. draft replaces the working draft. summary
condenses the agent history into notes that replace it, leaving the
working set intact. proposal submits and ends the agent loop.
Figure [C.1](#A3.SS1.SSS0.Px1 "Actions. ‣ C.1 Shared Agent Loop ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
gives the prompt that defines them.

System

You are a research scientist preparing ONE new research proposal.

Your research community is defined as:

\<the area’s definition, as given in
Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.1 Research Areas ‣ Appendix A Data Construction Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")\>

The literature you can access ends at 2025-12-31.

You don’t need to finish in one step --- you may plan, search, read,
draft and revise across multiple steps, one action per step.

You will be provided with:

1. Your researcher profile.

2. Your pinned papers --- the full cards you pinned with
$`<`$read_papers$`>`$, listed in the order you pinned them, with the
count at the end of the block. Nothing is ever lost: the corpus does not
change, so any \[CAND\] id you have seen can be pinned back and its full
card reappears here.

3. Your history --- an append-only log of what you did, one entry per
step, in the order it happened. A $`<`$search$`>`$ entry is followed by
its $`<`$information$`>`$ block (which opens with a receipt counting new
vs. already-retrieved results); a $`<`$read_papers$`>`$ entry is
followed by a receipt; a $`<`$plan$`>`$ or $`<`$draft$`>`$ entry records
only that you rewrote it --- the text itself lives in your workspace.
Past steps are never rewritten --- only a $`<`$summary$`>`$ replaces the
earlier entries wholesale. In the history every \[CAND\] result is a
title-only line; a line marked ’(shown above)’ is one that appeared
earlier.

4. Latest search results --- the full cards of your most recent search.
Once you search again this block is replaced, so pin anything you want
to keep reading.

5. Your workspace --- what you are writing: your latest $`<`$plan$`>`$,
your latest $`<`$draft$`>`$, and any $`<`$feedback$`>`$. A
$`<`$summary$`>`$ clears the plan and the draft but keeps your pinned
papers, which is why that action asks you to carry them into your notes.

6. Your remaining step budget (each response consumes one step).

IMPORTANT: You must strictly adhere to the following rules:

1. Choose ONLY ONE action from the list below for each response, DO NOT
perform more than one action per step.

2. Follow the exact syntax format for the selected action, DO NOT create
or use any actions other than those listed.

3. The corpus is a bounded snapshot of one research community. Thin or
empty results mean "not found in this corpus", NOT proof that no such
work exists anywhere; ground research-gap claims in the papers you
actually retrieved and cited.

4. Form a hypothesis early and let evidence attack it. Put a first
$`<`$plan$`>`$ on the table within your first few steps, then use
$`<`$search$`>`$ to test it --- searching is how you check, refute or
sharpen your idea.

5. The episode ends when you output $`<`$proposal$`>`$, and you may
output it on any step. "Steps remaining: 1" is the deadline: your
response then MUST be the $`<`$proposal$`>`$, otherwise the episode ends
with nothing.

6. Every id in your References section MUST be a paper you actually
retrieved: copy it verbatim from a \[CAND\] line anywhere in your
history --- search results, your working set, or ids you kept in your
summary notes. You are NOT allowed to invent ids or cite papers that
never appeared. List each paper at most once.

7. Cite inline with bracketed numbers \[1\], \[2\], ... matching your
References entries. The same paper always reuses the same number. Do NOT
write raw \[CAND\] ids inside the prose --- they belong only in the
References section.

8. The proposal must be concrete and technically grounded: a specific
falsifiable core idea, not a survey or a vague direction. It must be new
relative to the literature you retrieved, and your proposal must say
exactly what is new: in its related-work discussion, name the closest
papers you found and state what your proposal does that they do not ---
a different problem framing, a different mechanism, a different
evaluation that would settle something they left open. Restating or
incrementally tuning a retrieved paper is not a proposal. Every proposal
has related work: if you found none, that almost always means the search
was too narrow, not that the idea is unprecedented --- claim the latter
only if you are prepared to defend it. Claim novelty only against what
you actually retrieved and cited (see rule 3).

Valid actions:

1. $`<`$plan$`>`$ research state $`<`$/plan$`>`$: your current working
hypothesis and anything else worth carrying forward --- including but
not limited to (a) the idea you are proposing, stated as a specific
falsifiable claim; (b) what would show it is wrong, already done, or not
worth doing; (c) a finding, a constraint you hit, or a question to
settle later. A rough hypothesis on little evidence is fine and
expected. Issue $`<`$plan$`>`$ again to replace it: sharpen it as
evidence accumulates, and when evidence kills it --- the claim is wrong,
or the retrieved literature already does it --- move to a different gap
instead of defending it. Apart from your $`<`$draft$`>`$, this is the
only note that survives into the next step.

2. $`<`$search$`>`$ query $`<`$/search$`>`$: search the local literature
corpus (a short natural-language or keyword query, $`<`$= 25 words).
Optionally append a publication-year filter after a pipe, e.g.
$`<`$search$`>`$ repository level code generation \| years: 2020-2025
$`<`$/search$`>`$ (a single year also works). The engine combines
keyword (BM25) and semantic matching.

3. $`<`$read_papers$`>`$ id1, id2, ... $`<`$/read_papers$`>`$: pin
papers you have already retrieved into your $`<`$working_set$`>`$ ---
use it to read a paper whose card has been compressed to a title-only
line, or to keep an important one in full while you work. Copy the ids
verbatim from \[CAND\] lines. The working set holds 50 cards; when it is
full the least recently requested one is evicted. A card carries the
paper’s metadata and its abstract --- the corpus has no full text, so
the abstract is all there is of a paper’s content.

4. $`<`$draft$`>`$ revised or newly written working draft of your
proposal $`<`$/draft$`>`$: write or revise your working draft based on
what you have learned --- this is where your $`<`$plan$`>`$ hypothesis
becomes an actual proposal. The latest draft replaces the previous one.
Ideally you start drafting while 5-10 steps remain, so there is room
left to sharpen the proposal rather than writing it in the last step.

5. $`<`$summary$`>`$ research notes $`<`$/summary$`>`$: condense your
history into notes. Keep the plan, the current draft state, key
findings, and the \[CAND\] ids you may still want to cite (one line per
id is enough --- you can pin any of them back into your working set
later with $`<`$read_papers$`>`$). Your subsequent history will be
replaced by this summary; your $`<`$working_set$`>`$ is preserved
automatically.

6. $`<`$proposal$`>`$ final proposal in markdown $`<`$/proposal$`>`$:
output the final proposal as a self-contained markdown document:

- First line: "# $`<`$specific title$`>`$".

- Then a paragraph starting with "\*\*Abstract\*\*: " --- 4-8 sentences
summarizing the whole proposal (this is what the community will first
see).

- Then a related-work section: the closest papers you retrieved, named,
and what your proposal does that they do not.

- Then the rest of the proposal body in free-form prose, structured
however serves the idea best. A strong proposal covers: motivation, the
research gap that follows from the related work, the core idea (crisp
and falsifiable), method, implementation plan, experiment plan
(datasets, baselines, metrics, ablations), and expected contribution ---
but the organization, depth, and voice are yours.

- Cite inline with \[1\], \[2\], ... throughout the body.

- End with a "## References" section, exactly one line per cited paper,
in the format:

\[n\] $`<`$the id copied from the \[CAND\] line, WITHOUT any brackets
around it$`>`$ \| role:
method\|baseline\|motivation\|extension\|background \| one sentence on
how this paper is used

For example: \[1\] D3f2a9c01d2e4 \| role: baseline \| We compare against
this retrieval-augmented generator.

Format: use exactly one tag pair per response --- $`<`$plan$`>`$,
$`<`$search$`>`$, $`<`$read_papers$`>`$, $`<`$draft$`>`$,
$`<`$summary$`>`$, or $`<`$proposal$`>`$. No text outside the tags.

### C.2 Method Configurations

The methods differ in the profile placed at the top of that prompt and
in the actions appended to it.

##### Agent profiles.

Free states only that the LLM agent is an independent researcher in the
community defined above, with no direction and no prescription of how to
work. Topic names one research area and adds that the LLM agent chooses
its own problems within it. Frontier reuses the topic profile and
allocation unchanged, so that the only difference between them is access
to earlier rounds.

##### Topic allocation.

The areas named in topic profiles come from the topic labels already
carried by OpenAlex records. The set of topics itself was built from the
citation network. OpenAlex started with works that have incoming and
outgoing citations and clustered them by citation
relationships ([OpenAlex, 2024](#bib.bib69); [van Eck,
2024](#bib.bib70)). We collect topic label on a $`\leq 2025`$ paper, and
we weight topics by an exponential decay in publication year with a
half-life of two years, which favors recent practice without cutting the
earlier years off. The resulting distribution is allocated across the
population by largest remainder. As shown in
Table [15](#A3.T15 "Table 15 ‣ Topic allocation. ‣ C.2 Method Configurations ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?"),
topics read like Topic Modeling, Machine Learning in Materials Science,
Scientific Computing and Data Management, and Multi-Agent Systems and
Negotiation. Some topics look unrelated to the specific research area,
but the topic allocation is a rough guide to the areas the LLM agent is
expected to explore, and it does not constrain the search or the
proposal. The LLM agent may search for any topic in the corpus and
propose any idea it can justify. At a population of 300 the allocation
reaches between 146 and 177 distinct topics out of the several thousand
that carry prior mass.

##### Tag extraction.

The tags behind landscape are extracted from the 2025 papers. A language

the variants that survive, such as modifier and hyphen differences and
the same name appearing on both axes. In automated research this yields
task tags such as autonomous experimentation, equation discovery, AI
scientist, and scientific hypothesis generation, and method tags such as
llm agent, multi-agent system, benchmark construction, and

\<the area’s definition, as given in

area in passing, or uses its tools for another goal, does NOT belong.

the model that screens papers for relevance to a specific research area,

that write proposals.

- •
  Schema extraction. Both sides of every comparison are described by the
  same extractor, with the same prompt and the same retry policy. A
  different extractor on either side would compare two extraction styles
  rather than two ideas, so the model and the endpoint are pinned in
  code, independent of whichever model wrote the proposal. We pin
  DeepSeek-V4-Pro on the vendor’s own endpoint, the largest checkpoint
  in that family rather than the Flash one an LLM agent runs, because
  extraction is an instrument: a field read wrong here moves every score
  computed from the schema.
- •
  Tag extraction. The task and method tags behind landscape are
  extracted by DeepSeek-V4-Flash in JSON mode. The vocabulary starts
  empty and grows batch by batch: each batch sees the vocabulary built
  so far, reuses an existing tag where one fits, and marks a new tag
  explicitly when none does. A separate merge pass resolves the aliases
  that survive.
- •
  Area screening. The area screening model is DeepSeek-V4-Flash in JSON
  mode. It reads a paper’s title and abstract and decides whether the
  paper is in the area, returning a boolean and a confidence score.
- •
  Idea matching. Slot agreement is decided by GPT-5.6 Luna in JSON mode,
  with output capped at 1,200 tokens. Each pair is judged once, and
  which document is shown first is drawn from a hash of the pair and a
  fixed seed: random across pairs, reproducible on a rerun, and
  unaffected by worker or retry order. Every call is logged with its
  input, its output, and the order it drew, since the judge is the only
  model whose output becomes a number we report;
  Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation Details ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
  reports its agreement with human annotators. No population is judged
  by its own model family.
- •
  LLM agents. The six models of
  Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
  run one implementation, one prompt, and one step budget of twenty
  steps. Replies are plain text, and the action tag is parsed from the
  text. The output cap is 16,384 tokens, except on Bedrock, where Llama
  4 Scout is capped at the 8,192 the platform allows.

The request body carries the inputs and nothing else. Temperature,
top-$`p`$, sampling seed, and tool definitions are never sent, so each
endpoint runs at its own default. Two parameters are exceptions. JSON
mode is set wherever the reply is parsed as JSON, and a maximum output
length is sent where the API requires one or where a stage has its own
output budget.

### E.3 The Size of Human Reference

Every score in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
is measured against the complete set of 2026 papers in the area, from
1,136 to 5,188 of them, and the human level applies the same metrics to
those papers themselves, each compared only with the others. The Human
and LLM agent are sized for different reasons. The reference is complete
because it is the record of what a community actually published that
year, and the human level is meant to be a reference line for real
research. Keeping it fixed also gives every population the same
denominator, which is what makes scores comparable across models,
methods, and areas. The 300 proposals per population are a deliberate
trade-off.
Sec. [5](#S5.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Can more LLM agents close the gap? ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
grows populations to the size of the reference, 3.8 to 17 times larger,
and matching rises slowly and then almost flattens, so we can see that
for LLM agents, 300 proposals already place a population close to where
a far larger one lands. Asking for more would raise the inference cost
of running IdeaTaste without changing what it measures. Therefore, we
keep the population size at 300 and let the human reference be whatever
it is. In this setting, we can compare the performance of different LLM
agents, and also see whether any of them reach the human level.

### E.4 Leakage and Benchmark Robustness

Four of the six models in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Methods ‣ 3 IdeaTaste ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
were released in 2026 and may have seen part of the reference, which
covers January to August 2026, so they could in principle reproduce 2026
papers from memory rather than through research judgment. This choice is
deliberate, since a benchmark of research taste should evaluate the
models that current AI scientists are built on. We pair these releases
with two 2025 models whose training data end before the reference year,
which gives a setting free of exposure within the same benchmark. Below
we describe one design constraint that limits exposure, test whether
exposure helps, and check whether the findings differ between the two
settings.

##### Retrieval restricted to the corpus.

An LLM agent cites only papers it retrieves from the corpus of its area,
which ends in 2025, so a 2026 paper can never enter its citation set.
This constraint does not rule out indirect exposure, since a model that
has read a 2026 paper could still reuse its references or restate its
idea. The test below addresses this remaining channel.

##### Models released before and after the reference year.

Meta reports an August 2024 pretraining cutoff for Llama 4 Scout, and
Anthropic reports that the public internet data of Claude Haiku 4.5 end
in February 2025 ([Meta AI, 2025a](#bib.bib61); [Anthropic,
2025a](#bib.bib62)). Neither can have seen a paper first released in
2026, so the two serve as temporal controls for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Qwen3.8-27B, and K2 Horizon 375B A23B. If exposure
helped, the later releases should lead at the idea level, where
memorized content would matter most. They trail instead. Averaged over
areas and methods, the two controls reach 3.10 on Match-Idea against
1.68 for the 2026 releases, and Llama 4 Scout, with the earliest cutoff,
scores highest of all six. The 2026 releases score slightly higher on
Match-Cite, 5.06 against 4.14, but Claude Haiku 4.5 comes within 0.6
points of each of them, so this difference more plausibly reflects model
capability than exposure. The two levels even move in opposite
directions across models, as those with higher Match-Cite tend to have
lower Match-Idea. Access to the relevant literature is therefore not
what limits recovery.

##### Consistent findings in both settings.

The findings of
Sec. [4](#S4.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Main results. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
hold in both groups. Matching stays far below the human level, at 22% on
citations and 26% on ideas for the 2025 models and 28% and 14% for the
2026 releases. No population in either group reaches the human
Diversity-Idea, and landscape ranks last on Match-Cite in most settings
of both. The only difference is the relatively high Match-Idea of Llama
4 Scout, which runs against exposure rather than with it. Including the
2026 releases thus extends the evaluation to the latest models without
changing its conclusions, and IdeaTaste, though built on a time split,
does not rest on memorization.

##### Extending to later years.

The construction of IdeaTaste repeats for any year $`Y`$ by freezing the
corpus at the end of $`Y-1`$, collecting the papers of $`Y`$ as the
reference, and applying the same metrics. Each new model can thus be
evaluated against a reference published after its training data end,
which removes the concern altogether.

##### Information access of LLM agents and human researchers.

LLM agents can retrieve only literature published through 2025, whereas
the 2026 papers are observed after the cutoff and their authors may have
had access to work released during 2026. However, matching the
information of an LLM agent to that of a paper would require knowing
what each author had read and restricting retrieval to it, which cannot
be reconstructed after publication. IdeaTaste aligns what can be
aligned. Every population searches the same corpus, which ends on the
same date, and runs under the same budget, prompt, and judge, so this
design leaves comparisons among LLM agents intact. The human level is
read accordingly, as a reference line for where research actually went
rather than as a competing arm under matched conditions.

### E.7 Overlap with $`\leq 2025`$ Literature

A proposal may be restating work the corpus already holds. Therefore, we
score each document exactly as Match-Idea does, with the same slot judge
and the same weights, but against its nearest papers in the corpus $`U`$
which LLM agent can access. The result is the weighted share of a
document’s slots that some earlier paper already states. Automated
research is used throughout, and every population contributes as many
proposals as the area has 2026 papers, so all rows in
Table [20](#A5.T20 "Table 20 ‣ E.7 Overlap with ≤2025 Literature ‣ Appendix E Additional Analyses ‣ IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers?")
rest on the same number of documents.

Every population repeats the earlier literature less than the 2026
papers do, so the proposals that miss the human record are not
restatements of what the corpus already contains. The difference sits in
the same two slots as the main result. Human papers restate an earlier
approach or research plan many times more often than any population
does, and those two slots carry most of the weight, while on problems
and motivations the two sides are much closer. A proposal therefore
tends to miss both the 2026 papers and the corpus at exactly the point
where it commits to a mechanism. This does not make the proposals more
original than the published work. The human reference contains
cross-year continuations by the same group, which raise its overlap.

##### Case 1: Expert reuse in memory-constrained inference.

Both proposals address expert-fetch overhead. ReMoE and LARC fine-tune
the router to increase temporal expert reuse. TopicRoute instead leaves
the model unchanged and predicts expert demand from prompt topics to
guide prefetching and scheduling. The choice is whether to change
routing behavior or anticipate it.

Human paper: [ReMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27081) ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib74))

Title. ReMoE: Boosting Expert Reuse through Router Fine-Tuning in
Memory-Constrained MoE LLM Inference

Abstract. Fine-grained Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models sparsely activate
only a subset of experts per token, reducing activated computation while
maintaining high model capacity. However, in memory-constrained
inference scenarios, only a small set of experts can be cached. Experts
not in the cache must be fetched from slow external storage (e.g., UFS),
leading to frequent evictions and substantial I/O overhead. We propose
ReMoE, a router fine-tuning framework designed to boost token-wise
expert reuse. ReMoE biases the router toward recently selected experts,
producing temporally stable routing that better matches cache locality
constraints. By increasing short-horizon expert reuse, ReMoE reduces
expert fetches from storage without adding inference-time computation.
Experiments on DeepSeek and Qwen models show that ReMoE improves expert
reuse by 26% while maintaining downstream task performance. Real-system
evaluations further confirm these benefits, improving output throughput
by 8.4% under vLLM GPU-CPU expert offloading and reducing TPOT by
43.6-49.8% under llama.cpp on Jetson Orin NX, corresponding to a
1.77-1.99$`\times`$ decode speedup across diverse workloads. Checkpoints
and usage instructions are available at
[https://github.com/BUAA-OSCAR/ReMoE](https://github.com/BUAA-OSCAR/ReMoE).

Problem. In memory-constrained inference for fine-grained
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, only a small set of experts can be
cached; experts outside the cache must be fetched from slow external
storage, causing frequent evictions and substantial I/O overhead.

Motivation. Reducing expert fetches would cut I/O overhead and improve
throughput and latency in memory-constrained deployments.

Proposed approach. The proposed framework fine-tunes the router of
fine-grained MoE models to bias it toward recently selected experts,
producing temporally stable routing that better matches cache locality
constraints. This increases short-horizon expert reuse and reduces
expert fetches from slow external storage without adding inference-time
computation. Checkpoints and usage instructions are released.

Research plan. The paper evaluates the framework through experiments on
DeepSeek and Qwen models measuring expert reuse and downstream task
performance, and through real-system evaluations under vLLM GPU-CPU
expert offloading and llama.cpp on Jetson Orin NX measuring output
throughput and time-per-output-token across diverse workloads.

Title. TopicRoute: Prompt-Only Topic Modeling as a Zero-Forward-Pass
Expert Predictor for MoE Offloading and Scheduling

Abstract. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLMs deployed on memory-constrained
devices are bottlenecked by expert cache misses, which trigger costly
host-to-device weight transfers on the critical path. Existing
predictive prefetchers rely on intermediate forward-pass activations
\[3,4\] or dedicated predictor networks fed by in-flight computation
\[5,6,10\], so none can issue a prefetch before the first layer
executes. We propose TopicRoute, a serving system that applies a
lightweight online topic model to prompt embeddings at request arrival,
predicts the per-request, per-layer expert activation set with zero
forward-pass overhead, and uses this single topic signal to drive (i)
proactive expert prefetching, (ii) demand-driven cache replacement, and
(iii) routing-preserving request co-scheduling. Our core claim is
falsifiable: on models with high local routing consistency as measured
by the SRP/SCH metrics of recent MoE analysis \[1\], topic-predicted
prefetching reduces time-to-first-token by at least 30% and
host-to-device transfer bytes by at least 20% versus locality-only (LRU,
segment-best) policies at a fixed cache budget, while on
low-routing-consistency models the gain collapses to under 5%, providing
a built-in negative control. The approach is complementary to, not
competitive with, router-decoupled designs \[12\] and device-level
semantic co-scheduling \[18\]. We implement TopicRoute atop an
open-source MoE offloading runtime and evaluate on Mixtral-8x7B,
OLMoE-1B/7B, and DeepSeek-MoE across four workloads against seven
offloading baselines including MoE-Infinity \[2\], ProMoE \[3\], Fate
\[4\], and DuoServe-MoE \[10\], with an ablation matrix isolating the
topic signal, the confidence gate, and each system component.

bottlenecked by expert cache misses, which trigger costly host-to-device
weight transfers on the critical path; existing predictive prefetchers
depend on intermediate forward-pass activations or dedicated predictor
networks fed by in-flight computation, so none can issue a prefetch
before the first layer executes.

Motivation. Removing this first-layer prefetch gap could take
host-to-device transfer latency off the critical path and improve
time-to-first-token for memory-constrained MoE serving.

Proposed approach. The proposed serving system applies a lightweight
online topic model to prompt embeddings at request arrival, predicts the
per-request, per-layer expert activation set with zero forward-pass
overhead, and uses this single topic signal to drive proactive expert
prefetching, demand-driven cache replacement, and routing-preserving
request co-scheduling. A confidence gate is included as a separate
component. The system is implemented atop an open-source MoE offloading
runtime and evaluated on Mixtral-8x7B, OLMoE-1B/7B, and DeepSeek-MoE
across four workloads.

Research plan. (1) The core claim is evaluated by comparing
time-to-first-token and host-to-device transfer bytes against
locality-only (LRU, segment-best) policies at a fixed cache budget,
separating high- and low-routing-consistency models using the SRP/SCH
metrics; the low-consistency condition serves as a built-in negative
control in which the predicted gain should fall below 5%. (2) Evaluation
spans Mixtral-8x7B, OLMoE-1B/7B, and DeepSeek-MoE across four workloads
against seven offloading baselines including MoE-Infinity \[2\], ProMoE
\[3\], Fate \[4\], and DuoServe-MoE \[10\]. (3) An ablation matrix
isolates the topic signal, the confidence gate, and each system
component.

AI proposal B: LARC

Title. LARC: Post-Training Only the Routers of Frozen MoE LLMs for Local
Routing Consistency and Faster Offloaded Inference

Abstract. Expert offloading for memory-constrained MoE LLM serving
hinges on a model property - local routing consistency, the tendency of
consecutive tokens to reuse the same experts - that varies widely across
models and, in the literature we retrieved, has only ever been measured,
never trained \[1\]. We propose LARC (Local-routing-Aware Router
post-Training), a one-time procedure that post-trains only the gating
networks of a frozen pretrained MoE LLM with a windowed, differentiable
expert-set-overlap objective against a running cache-set surrogate,
combined with the language-modeling loss and a global load-balance term.
Our falsifiable claim: this model-side change measurably raises local
routing consistency (SRP/SCH \[1\]) while holding perplexity degradation
to $`\leq`$ ~2%, and - at fixed GPU memory - lowers end-to-end decode
latency under expert offloading by $`\geq`$10% with zero per-step
routing overhead, in contrast to inference-time cache-aware or
batch-aware re-routing \[2\]\[3\], which re-pay a per-token cost and
make a per-request quality trade-off on every request. Where offloading
systems adapt the system to whatever routing the model exhibits
\[4\]\[5\]\[6\]\[7\]\[8\]\[9\]\[10\]\[20\], LARC adapts the model to the
deployment’s memory constraint; where prior routing objectives are
pretraining-time and target convergence, stability, or communication
volume \[11\]\[12\]\[13\], LARC is a cheap deployment-aware
post-processing step on a frozen model. We specify the objective, a
router-only training protocol (100-500M tokens; router weights are
\<0.1% of model parameters, so this is hours-to-a-day per model), an
evaluation plan against system-side, static-compilation, and
inference-time re-routing baselines, and the ablations and kill criteria
that would falsify the claim.

Problem. Expert offloading for memory-constrained MoE LLM serving
depends on local routing consistency-the tendency of consecutive tokens
to reuse the same experts-which varies widely across models and, in
prior work, has only been measured, not trained; inference-time
cache-aware or batch-aware re-routing incurs per-token cost and
per-request quality trade-offs, while offloading systems adapt to
whatever routing the model exhibits.

Motivation. A model-side change that raises local routing consistency
while preserving perplexity could lower end-to-end decode latency under
expert offloading at fixed GPU memory without per-step routing overhead
or per-request quality trade-off, and is cheap because only router
weights are trained.

Proposed approach. The proposed procedure post-trains only the gating
networks of a frozen pretrained MoE LLM. It combines a windowed,
differentiable expert-set-overlap objective computed against a running
cache-set surrogate with the language-modeling loss and a global
load-balance term. Training uses a router-only protocol over 100-500M
tokens; router weights are less than 0.1% of model parameters, making
the one-time step hours-to-a-day per model.

Research plan. The paper evaluates the consistency/perplexity claim and
the latency claim against system-side, static-compilation, and
inference-time re-routing baselines, with ablations and kill criteria to
falsify the claims; fixed GPU memory is stated as the matched condition
for the latency trade-off claim. The abstract specifies the 100-500M
token router-only protocol but does not give exact metrics beyond
SRP/SCH or the kill criteria.

##### Case 2: Online adaptation of world models.

All three works seek adaptation to changing environments while retaining
prior capabilities. SPREAD and DynaLoRA update low-rank adapters on a
frozen model. ERALD instead stores observed transitions and combines

Title. SPREAD: Scalable Pre-Trained World Model for Adaptive Dynamics
Model

Abstract. Autonomous robots must be capable of adapting not only to
training datasets but also to unfamiliar environments. World models,
which learn predictive dynamics of the environment, have been proposed
to overcome the task-specific limitations of conventional RL. However,

highlights world models that autonomously adapt to real-world dynamics.

Motivation. Autonomous robots must adapt to unfamiliar environments

Proposed approach. The method freezes a pre-trained world model and

Model: DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Method: topic.

Title. ERALD: Episodic Retrieval-Augmented Latent Dynamics - Gated
Fusion of Parametric and Memory-Based Next-State Prediction for
Gradient-Free World-Model Adaptation

Abstract. Latent world models of the PlaNet/Dreamer family predict
environment transitions with fixed network weights and consequently
degrade the moment the environment changes (mass, friction, gravity,
layout). Existing remedies either fine-tune the transition model on
replay mixtures, inheriting a plasticity-forgetting trade-off and
stale-data interference, or compress experience into an amortized
context vector that fixed weights must still decode. We propose ERALD, a
hybrid latent world model that keeps a frozen recurrent state-space
model as a parametric prior and adds a bounded, continuously maintained
episodic store of latent transitions. At every step of an imagined
rollout, a contrastively learned, dynamics-aware key retrieves stored
(state, action, next-state) items whose local dynamics match the current
regime, and the parametric forecast is fused with the kernel-weighted
episodic estimate by an online, residual-driven arbitration rule rather
than by learned gating parameters. A regime shift is absorbed by
appending observed transitions to the store and re-optimizing the policy
inside ERALD imagination, with zero updates to the transition-model
weights. We claim that under abrupt, recombinatorial dynamics switches,
ERALD recovers a target return in fewer environment steps than replay-
and generative-replay-based continual world models, achieves lower
multi-step predictive error than both the frozen parametric model and a
pure search-based world model given identical memory, and exhibits no
measurable forgetting of the source regime. The claim is falsified if
the hybrid never beats the better of its two components, if the online
arbitration rule adds nothing over a fixed blend, if a replay-based
baseline reaches the target return within the same environment budget,
or if a dynamics-agnostic key space performs equally.

Problem. Latent world models of the PlaNet/Dreamer family predict
environment transitions with fixed network weights and consequently
degrade when the environment changes (mass, friction, gravity, layout);
existing remedies either fine-tune the transition model on replay
mixtures, inheriting a plasticity-forgetting trade-off and stale-data
interference, or compress experience into an amortized context vector
that fixed weights must still decode.

Motivation. Continual model-based control needs to adapt quickly to
abrupt environment changes while retaining prior regimes, and avoiding
transition-model fine-tuning removes the plasticity-forgetting trade-off
and stale-data interference of existing remedies.

Proposed approach. The proposed hybrid latent world model keeps a frozen
recurrent state-space model as a parametric prior and adds a bounded,
continuously maintained episodic store of latent transitions. During
imagined rollouts, a contrastively learned dynamics-aware key retrieves
stored (state, action, next-state) items whose local dynamics match the
current regime. The parametric forecast is fused with a kernel-weighted
episodic estimate by an online, residual-driven arbitration rule rather

Title. RDKV: Rate-Distortion Bit Allocation for Joint Eviction and
Quantization of the KV Cache

Abstract. Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance
across diverse tasks, but their inference with long input contexts is
bottlenecked by memory size and bandwidth. The Key-Value (KV) cache size
grows linearly with sequence length and needs to be re-read from
off-chip high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to on-chip memory at every decoding
step, resulting in memory-bound inference. Existing methods reduce the
cache by either eviction or quantization, but typically treat the two in
isolation. In this paper, we cast KV cache compression as a
rate-distortion problem, under which eviction and quantization are two
end-points of the same bit allocation scheme. This exposes the need to
optimize them jointly, motivating our method, RDKV (Rate-Distortion KV
cache compression). RDKV derives the weight of each token or channel
from the distortion that compression induces on the attention
computation. Based on these weights, it assigns each token or channel a
bit-width ranging from full precision down to zero bits guided by
reverse water-filling, applied once after the prefilling stage.
Experiments on LongBench, RULER, and InfiniteBench show that RDKV
outperforms the best evaluated baseline by 9.1% on average. On LongBench
it recovers 97.81% of full-cache accuracy with only 2.48% cache
retention. Compared with full-cache FlashAttention-2 decoding, it
achieves 4.5x decode speedup and 1.9x peak memory reduction with 128K
context length, while maintaining comparable performance.

Problem. Inference with long input contexts is bottlenecked by memory
size and bandwidth because the KV cache grows linearly with sequence
length and must be re-read from off-chip high-bandwidth memory to
on-chip memory at every decoding step, making decoding memory-bound.

Motivation. Existing KV cache compression methods reduce the cache
through eviction or quantization in isolation, leaving unexploited the
potential gains from jointly optimizing the two under a shared bit
allocation.

Proposed approach. The method casts KV cache compression as a

Research plan. Evidence consists of experiments on LongBench, RULER, and

paged layout where demoted tokens live in separate low-precision pages
````
