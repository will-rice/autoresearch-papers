# NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research

Wei Xiong    Jiangtong Li    Han Zhao Affiliation: Tongji University
 Ant Group  Shanghai Jiao Tong University    Linxiao Yang    Jie Li
Affiliation: Translational Research Center, Shanghai Yangzhi

Tongji University    Changjun Jiang

###### Abstract

EEG analysis and decoding underpin brain-computer interfaces, cognitive
assessment, and sleep monitoring. Fixed pipelines and reward-driven
search do not explicitly determine how signal structure,
representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics contribute to
performance or generalization gaps, leaving intervention choice
underdetermined. We introduce NeuroAct, an autonomous research agent
framework for EEG analysis and decoding. NeuroAct implements dual-loop
autonomous research across EEG foundation models, task-specific
predictors, and model combinations. In an inner loop, research,
development, and review roles use evidence on signal structure,
representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics to test
hypotheses, select analyses or interventions, and refine candidates
using performance, stability, and cost feedback. An outer loop distills
outcomes into conditional, testable recommendations with task and model
conditions, then validates and revises them on subsequent tasks. We
evaluate four settings: (A) EEG General Analysis, (B) Cross-task Model
Optimization, (C) Single-task Open Research, and (D) Collaborative
Prediction. In Setting A, NeuroAct’s 80.88 macro-average exceeds
BrainAgent’s 63.13 by 17.75 points. In Setting B, NeuroAct raises
EEGPT’s mean balanced accuracy (BA) by 7.34 points and HBN Pearson’s
$`r`$ from 0.6894 to 0.7362; in Setting C, it improves ADFTD BA by 16.33
points; and in Setting D, it raises Workload BA from 79.69% to 85.02%.
Experience reuse raises BCIC-IV-2a BA from 34.66% to 50.81% while
reducing LLM tokens by 64.02%, indicating recommendations improve
cross-task performance. NeuroAct enables evidence-guided EEG research
improved by cross-task experience.

## 1 Introduction

EEG analysis and decoding support cognitive-state assessment, sleep
monitoring, and brain-computer interfaces. Pretrained EEG foundation
models (EEG-FMs) provide reusable representations across diverse
tasks ([Jiang et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib26); [Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib28); [El Ouahidi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Döner et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). Effective task
adaptation requires assessing how signal structure, representations,
predictor behavior, and training dynamics contribute to performance or
generalization gaps, then selecting evidence that distinguishes
competing explanations. Fixed pipelines and reward-driven optimization
do not make this diagnostic decision explicit, motivating an autonomous
research agent that improves within-task EEG decoding and refines
research strategies across tasks.

Task-specific EEG analysis must connect signal structure to how a model
represents, predicts, and learns. Benchmark studies show model rankings
and transfer depend on task and readout design, encoded features can
differ from those used for prediction, and token preservation affects
frozen-encoder decoding ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib24); [Širca et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)). When refinement of a single EEG model yields
diminishing gains, this diagnosis can test whether other models correct
its errors on samples. Error overlap determines whether complementary
prediction merits a model-combination experiment.

Autonomous research systems combine hypothesis generation,
experimentation, and interpretation in continuous workflows ([Lu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)). Model-development systems further use experimental
feedback or accumulated experience to revise their
approaches ([alphaXiv, 2026](#bib.bib1)). In the EEG domain, BrainAgent
orchestrates analysis tools ([Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib40)),
BrainPilot combines domain knowledge and research skills ([Li et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib14)), and NeuroWeaver searches task pipelines ([Wang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). For EEG research, open questions concern how
diagnostics guide interventions across foundation-model adaptation,
task-specific modeling, joint training, and model combination, and how
cross-task validation and revision make conditional recommendations
useful on subsequent tasks.

We introduce NeuroAct, an autonomous research agent framework for EEG
analysis and decoding. NeuroAct implements a dual-loop autonomous
research framework: an inner loop selects analyses and interventions
from evidence on signal structure, representations, predictor behavior,
and training dynamics, while an outer loop distills, validates, and
revises conditional recommendations or experiences across research
tasks. Within the inner loop, the Coordinator directs the Researcher,
Developer, and Reviewer to test competing diagnostic hypotheses through
executable analyses or model changes, with predictive performance,
stability, and cost informing the next action for each task. Each
experience item is a conditional recommendation that records applicable
EEG task and model conditions, a proposed action, supporting and
counterevidence, and reassessment conditions. Subsequent tasks test its
utility, while counterevidence narrows, suspends, or replaces the
recommendation.

We organize NeuroAct’s evaluation around four research settings: (A) EEG
General Analysis, (B) Cross-task Model Optimization, (C) Single-task
Open Research, and (D) Collaborative Prediction. The evaluation covers
six datasets (HBN ([Alexander et al., 2017](#bib.bib42); [Shirazi et
al., 2024](#bib.bib43)), Workload ([Zyma et al., 2019](#bib.bib44)),
PhysioMI ([Schalk et al., 2004](#bib.bib45)), BCIC-IV-2a ([Tangermann et
al., 2012](#bib.bib57)), ADFTD ([Miltiadous et al., 2023](#bib.bib46)),
and HMC ([Alvarez-Estevez and Rijsman, 2021](#bib.bib47))), three LLM
backend families (DeepSeek-V4 Flash ([DeepSeek-AI and others,
2026](#bib.bib48)), GPT-5.6 Luna ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib49)), and
Qwen3.8 Flash Next ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib50))), and the available
external baselines CodeAct ([Wang et al., 2024c](#bib.bib25)),
BrainPilot ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)), and BrainAgent ([Zhou et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib40)). With DeepSeek-V4 Flash, NeuroAct reaches an
80.88 macro-average score in Setting A and exceeds 75 with Luna and Qwen
on BrainBench ([Zhou et al., 2026a](#bib.bib41)). In Setting B, NeuroAct
raises mean balanced accuracy (BA) from 47.50 to 54.84 and HBN Pearson’s
$`r`$ from 0.6894 to 0.7362. NeuroAct gains 16.33 BA points on ADFTD in
Setting C and raises Workload BA from the best single model’s 79.69% to
85.02% in Setting D. Experience reuse transfers a PhysioMI-derived
recommendation to BCIC-IV-2a, raising test BA from 34.66% to 50.81%
while reducing LLM tokens by 64.02%. Our contributions are:

- •
  Evidence-guided inner-loop EEG research. NeuroAct tests alternatives
  and selects analyses or interventions using signal, representation,
  predictor, and training evidence.
- •
  Outer-loop conditional strategy improvement. NeuroAct tests
  conditional recommendations across tasks and revises them using
  matched performance and cost comparisons when warranted.
- •
  Executable EEG analysis and decoding. NeuroAct operationalizes
  evidence through EEG General Analysis, Cross-task Model Optimization,
  Single-task Open Research, and Collaborative Prediction.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 EEG Decoding Models

Task-specific architectures including EEGNet and EEG Conformer encode
local and temporal EEG structure ([Lawhern et al., 2018](#bib.bib51);
[Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)). Pretrained models including BENDR and
EEGPT learn reusable representations, while other EEG-FMs differ in
treating channels, signal structure, and recording
heterogeneity ([Kostas et al., 2021](#bib.bib53); [Yang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib54); [Jiang et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib26); [Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib28); [El Ouahidi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Döner et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). These
approaches expand candidate space from specialized decoders to
transferable EEG representations.

Benchmark studies show model rankings, transfer, and generalization vary
with task, dataset, and readout design ([Banville et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2); [Xiong et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). Adaptation studies identify cohort shift, data
availability, channel adaptation, and training regime as
consequential ([Nguyen et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib34); [Kokate et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Kommineni et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). NeuroAct turns evidence on signal structure,
representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics, including
cross-model error overlap, into diagnostic decisions selecting analyses
or interventions across adaptation, shared-model training, and model
combination rather than a fixed recipe.

### 2.2 Autonomous Research Agents

Autonomous research systems coordinate hypothesis generation,
experimentation, and interpretation in sustained workflows ([Swanson et
al., 2025](#bib.bib23); [Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Ghareeb et
al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). Computational agents extend this workflow
through executable tool use, candidate search, targeted refinement,
repository-grounded development, diagnostic code updates, and training
feedback, using execution outcomes to focus candidates ([Jiang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Nam et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20); [Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Li et al.,
2026d](#bib.bib13); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib37); [Li et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib15)). Long-horizon systems support asynchronous
experiments and interactive debugging ([Hambardzumyan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)) and allocate effort across competing plans using
feedback and remaining resources ([Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib35)).

Within EEG and brain-signal research, EEGAgent and BrainAgent
orchestrate signal-processing tools and domain agents, while CogEEGAgent
emphasizes deterministic cognitive-analysis execution and independent
confirmation ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Zhou et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib40); [Hou et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)). BrainPilot combines
knowledge, skills, and specialists for architecture development under
fixed preprocessing and training, whereas NeuroWeaver searches
domain-constrained pipelines through tree selection, code refinement,
and multi-objective rewards ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14); [Wang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). NeuroAct extends the workflow with an EEG
evidence loop that diagnoses gaps using signal, representation,
predictor, and training evidence before selecting executable analyses or
interventions.

### 2.3 Experience Reuse

Experience-transfer methods reuse outcomes across attempts or tasks.
Reflexion and ExpeL reuse verbal feedback or contrasting trajectories,
while ReasoningBank and EvoScientist distill and refine research
strategies from outcomes ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib55); [Zhao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib38); [Ouyang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56); [Lyu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)). Voyager and Recuris reuse executable skills or
revise the memory governing their use, while SkillsBench and controlled
studies examine when prescribed skills, tools, or guidance granularity
hinder stronger strategies ([Wang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib27); [Yu et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib36); [Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib16); [Xu and Wu,
2026](#bib.bib31)). NeuroAct treats experience as a conditional,
testable recommendation under explicit EEG task and model conditions,
validating its use on new tasks through outcomes and cost before
retaining, revising, or narrowing applicability.

## 3 Method

NeuroAct organizes EEG analysis and decoding through two coupled
research loops. The inner
loop (Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Inner Loop: Autonomous EEG Research ‣ 3 Method ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research"))
decomposes a shared objective into parallel questions, with the
Coordinator directing specialists in separate contexts and selecting
subsequent actions from reviewed evidence, experimental feedback, and
remaining budget. The outer
loop (Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Outer Loop: Experience-Based Self-Improvement ‣ 3 Method ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research"))
distills outcomes into conditional recommendations and revises them
across tasks.

### 3.1 Inner Loop: Autonomous EEG Research

Specialized collaboration. NeuroAct separates persona contexts and
permissions so that scientific assessment, implementation, and review
remain distinct. The Coordinator decomposes the objective into parallel
questions and assigns each to a separate research cycle. The Researcher
investigates an assigned question using literature, dataset semantics,
and EEG evidence, then develops a feasible proposal. The Developer
implements the selected proposal and executes the required analyses or
training. The Reviewer examines the solution, implementation, and
evidence, challenges unsupported conclusions, and returns the reviewed
outcome to the Coordinator. Each _handoff_ records the requested
deliverable, supporting evidence, unresolved issues, and an actionable
recommendation; the Coordinator integrates these handoffs, resolves
disagreements, and selects subsequent actions.

Distinct investigations, coordinated decisions. For objective $`g`$, EEG
data $`\mathcal{D}`$, and available model and code assets
$`\mathcal{A}`$, the Coordinator selects a research decision $`u_{t}`$
and specialist assignments $`\mathcal{J}_{t}`$,

````math
computation is required, the Researcher specifies it for the Coordinator
to assign to the Developer. The Developer executes it or implements
scoped changes to input processing, pretrained-model adaptation,
predictors, or training. Analytical tasks return computed results with
evidence-supported interpretations.

Feedback redirects research. The Coordinator compares observed results
and resource consumption with expected effects before selecting the next
investigation. Reviewer challenges to an implementation or explanation
can trigger further investigation. Auxiliary metrics and training
behavior distinguish improved discrimination from decision-rule changes
or unstable fitting. This evidence updates the solution, research state,
and next decision.

### 3.2 Outer Loop: Experience-Based Self-Improvement

Distillation from contrasting outcomes. The outer loop distills
experience about which experimental actions help under particular task
and model conditions. The Coordinator compares an intervention’s
expected and observed effects with considered alternatives, producing a
conditional recommendation,

|     |                        |     |     |
|-----|------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       k=(c,a,e^{+},e^{-},b),
       ```                     |     | (3) |

where $`c`$ specifies applicable task and model conditions, $`a`$ the
recommended action, $`e^{+}`$ and $`e^{-}`$ supporting and conflicting
evidence, and $`b`$ the conditions for reassessment.

Matching and adoption. Retrieval filters ineligible or incompatible
entries and ranks candidates by task and model compatibility,
research-goal overlap, source evidence, EEG paradigm, and class
structure. Similar recommendations are grouped into bounded summaries
with source links. The Coordinator compares this synthesis with the
target model and training conditions, choosing adoption, a bounded
applicability check, or rejection. Candidate advice is shown first to
the Coordinator; specialists receive only adopted content relevant to
their assignments. Active recommendations enter role contexts within a
character budget, with full evidence accessible on demand.

Revision through subsequent research. Target outcomes can narrow a
recommendation, revise its action, or suspend its use. Matched trials
assess transfer through performance and research cost. Study $`j`$ both
uses and updates shared experience,

|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{K}_{j,t}=\operatorname{Context}_{\mathrm{C}}(\mathcal{E}_{j};g_{j},\mathcal{D}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j},\mathcal{H}_{j,t}),\qquad\mathcal{E}_{j+1}=\operatorname{Revise}_{\mathrm{C}}(\mathcal{E}_{j};\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\mathrm{dev}}),
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\operatorname{Context}_{\mathrm{C}}`$ combines retrieval with
the Coordinator’s applicability decision, while
$`\operatorname{Revise}_{\mathrm{C}}`$ distills or revises
recommendations from eligible development outcomes. Revised experience
changes decisions in
Eq. ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Inner Loop: Autonomous EEG Research ‣ 3 Method ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")),
generating evidence for further revision. Unlike interaction memory,
this recursion improves the agent’s research strategy by revising which
experiments it recommends and when, based on subsequent outcomes ([Yu et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib36)).

### 3.3 Four Settings for EEG Research

EEG General Analysis (A). Given a question and EEG recordings, NeuroAct
selects computations and sources to produce an evidence-supported
answer. This setting exercises analytical reasoning and the
investigative capabilities used in subsequent decoding research.

Cross-task Model Optimization (B). Each program adapts one pretrained
encoder across heterogeneous datasets using task-specific adapters and
output modules. NeuroAct can revise task sampling and loss
contributions, then inspect the effects of shared updates on each
target. Joint classification and regression create coupled objectives
requiring balancing predictive utility across tasks.

Single-task Open Research (C). Given a dataset and prediction objective,
NeuroAct can refine a strong candidate or explore another model family,
including pretrained and task-specific predictors. This setting
exercises open development guided by EEG evidence.

Collaborative Prediction (D). NeuroAct examines aligned errors or
regression residuals for complementarity and develops a combination or
routing program,
$`\hat{y}=F_{\omega}\!\left(\{f_{m}(x)\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}},z(x)\right),`$
where $`\mathcal{M}`$ denotes members and $`z(x)`$ contains
inference-time EEG features or outputs. Research can modify
$`F_{\omega}`$ and, when permitted, train additional members to
outperform the strongest predictor.

## 4 Experiments

Experimental setup. We evaluate how effectively NeuroAct links EEG
analysis to predictive research and improves later studies through
experience. Four settings test the breadth of these capabilities;
mechanism ablations and experience studies measure effects on prediction
and research cost. BrainBench (Setting A) evaluates instruction-driven
EEG analysis across foundational, sleep, neurocognitive, and
physiological domains. Settings B, C, and D cover Workload, BCIC-IV-2a,
ADFTD, HMC, and HBN; PhysioMI supports experience and supplementary
studies. DeepSeek-V4 Flash is default LLM; GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3.8
Flash Next assess provider consistency, with CodeAct, BrainPilot, and
BrainAgent as baseline agents. For Settings B–D, we adapt the baselines’
task and execution interfaces.

### 4.3 Research Decision Traces

EEG analysis shapes an executable predictive method. In an ADFTD study,
the Researcher examines the recording montage and investigates spectral
features suited to the task. The absence of a declared reference makes
absolute power difficult to compare, motivating within-window relative
power and frequency-band ratios. The Developer implements these features
with regional aggregation and a gradient-boosting classifier. Error
analysis finds low recall for frontotemporal dementia (FTD), with
nonzero recall for every class. The agent treats class separation as the
next research question and tests complementary connectivity and
spatial-covariance features. The spectral solution remains the strongest
evaluated approach in this investigation, reaching 67.27% BA and 0.811
AUROC. EEG-specific analysis thus informs both implementation and the
next experiment.

Model feedback changes the intervention. Workload research illustrates
two ways to act on model behavior. In one study, early overfitting and
variable validation performance motivate training-time perturbations
scaled to the EEG amplitude. The resulting CBraMod comparison increases
BA from 65.98% to 71.62% and AUROC from 0.768 to 0.805. In another,
lowering the encoder learning rate leaves validation BA unchanged at
70.44%. Investigation of class imbalance then leads to a class-weighted
objective, reaching 76.89% validation BA and 75.55% test BA. The traces
illustrate how feedback can redirect an intervention toward a
better-supported explanation.

Experience focuses the next experiment. The PhysioMI recommendation
directs BCIC-IV-2a research toward retaining CBraMod patch information.
The first implementation also changes the training recipe, so the
Coordinator requests a narrower comparison that preserves the
established recipe. The resulting solution reaches 50.81% BA with 64.02%
fewer research tokens than the study without experience. Reuse thus
provides a concrete research direction, while target-side inspection
refines how it is tested.

## 5 Conclusion

NeuroAct advances autonomous EEG analysis and decoding by making domain
evidence part of experimental decision-making rather than treating
adaptation as undirected search. Its inner loop turns investigation into
executable interventions, while the outer loop carries useful experience
into later research and revises it with new evidence. The four settings
demonstrate EEG General Analysis, Cross-task Model Optimization,
Single-task Open Research, and Collaborative Prediction. Fixed-model
ablations support the contribution of specialized research capabilities,
and experience studies across datasets and model families show both
predictive gains and reduced research cost. These results establish a
path toward EEG research agents that improve current solutions,
accumulate validated recommendations across tasks, and conduct
subsequent studies more effectively.

demographic and recording-condition shifts.

## Appendix A Data and Prediction Tasks

### A.1 Prediction targets and evaluation units

HBN provides pediatric age regression from resting EEG ([Alexander et
al., 2017](#bib.bib42); [Shirazi et al., 2024](#bib.bib43)); Workload
distinguishes mental-arithmetic conditions ([Zyma et al.,
2019](#bib.bib44)); PhysioMI ([Schalk et al., 2004](#bib.bib45)) and
BCIC-IV-2a ([Tangermann et al., 2012](#bib.bib57)) provide four-class
motor imagery; ADFTD distinguishes Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal
dementia, and healthy controls ([Miltiadous et al., 2023](#bib.bib46));
HMC provides five-stage sleep classification ([Alvarez-Estevez and
Rijsman, 2021](#bib.bib47)). Recordings are partitioned at the required
subject or recording level before constructing prediction units.

HBN metrics are computed over windows, with MAE and RMSE in years and
MSE in squared years. For classsification tasks, BA is mean class
recall. Macro-F1 averages class F1 scores. AUROC uses the recorded
binary or multiclass one-versus-rest calculation. Evaluation units and
metric implementations are held fixed within each comparison.

### A.2 Model inputs and pretraining provenance

Predictor inputs use 200 Hz, except EEGPT and LUNA at 256 Hz. EEGPT
converts microvolt ($`\mu`$V) signals to millivolts (mV) by multiplying
by 0.001; CBraMod, LaBraM, and CSBrain multiply $`\mu`$V values by 0.01.
The ADFTD ShallowFBCSPNet uses $`\mu`$V signals directly.

BENDR maps each window to $`[-1,1]`$ using its minimum and maximum over
all channels and time points. BIOT divides each channel by the 95th
percentile of its absolute values within the window. EEGNet and LUNA
apply channel-wise window z-scores. REVE also uses channel-wise window
z-scores and clips values to $`[-15,15]`$. Its HBN data undergo
channel-wise standardization over complete recordings before
windowing ([Banville et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)); candidate settings
either preserve these values or apply an additional window-level
z-score.

LaBraM and EEGPT include PhysioMI in their pretraining corpora ([Jiang
et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib26)). Main
comparisons for Settings B, C, and D and the main CBraMod ablations
therefore use BCIC-IV-2a for motor imagery. REVE’s pretraining corpus
also includes HBN releases, creating dataset overlap for its HBN
evaluations and collaborative predictors containing REVE ([El Ouahidi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4)). Following NeuralBench ([Banville et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)), we retain HBN to evaluate agent-driven improvements
under pretrained initializations with known data overlap.

average of their scores. Execution errors remain in the denominator.

## Appendix B NeuroAct Harness and Implementation

### B.1 Runtime, roles, and execution

NeuroAct implements the dual-loop framework using the OpenHands Software
Agent SDK ([Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib58)) and tools version 1.42.1.
Its Agent and Conversation abstractions handle LLM interaction and tool
execution. NeuroAct builds the research harness on this foundation,
coordinating specialists and providing EEG analysis, literature tools,
and executable model experiments. It manages evaluation and research
evidence, with progressive disclosure of tools and runtime skills and
support for experience reuse and revision. All four personas use one
runtime definition, configured with shared base instructions and
role-specific prompts and permissions. Shell access and workspace
editing are assigned to the Developer. Researcher and Reviewer use
read-only tools; the Reviewer can inspect the Developer’s code changes
together with run and evaluation records. Tool access follows the role
bound to each conversation, keeping these permissions consistent as
assignments change.

To make concurrent investigations complementary, the Coordinator
specifies distinct questions and investigative paths before marking
independent Researcher and Reviewer assignments for parallel execution.
Each receives its own conversation and resource allowance, including
concurrent assignments to the same role. Sequential assignments can
reuse a compatible conversation to preserve useful context. The
coordination host runs authorized assignments within the concurrency
limit and forwards specialist handoffs to the Coordinator. This provides
separate reasoning contexts for competing approaches while retaining a
common point for their scientific assessment.

NeuroAct initializes a workspace at a user-specified location and uses
bubblewrap to isolate the Developer’s terminal, mounting research code
as writable and reference resources as read-only. A submission specifies
the implementation and its task and input configuration. Before
training, preflight checks verify input shapes, channel and unit
requirements, and executable interfaces. Submitted jobs run under the
experiment service, which manages progress and cancellation or
resumption independently of LLM turns. The experiment host returns
execution feedback to the Developer’s conversation. The Developer
inspects the results and reports its findings in a handoff to the
Coordinator. Run summaries expose training behavior and evaluation
aggregates alongside resource use, supplying the concrete evidence
needed for subsequent review and research decisions.

### B.2 Tools and progressive disclosure

The runtime exposes four tool families, neuro_act_research,
neuro_act_experiment, neuro_act_knowledge, and neuro_act_collaboration.
The research family groups workspace, resource, literature, and analysis
capabilities. Initial context contains a compact capability index. The
agent obtains the selected callable contract before invocation, then
requests detailed outputs when needed. Known capabilities can also be
accessed directly. Run histories begin with compact summaries, with
selected curves and artifacts retrieved on demand.

NeuroAct’s EEG analysis tools provide signal summaries and configurable
measurements for specific research questions. Spectral analysis uses
Welch estimates and configurable frequency bands. Spatial analysis
summarizes covariance and channel relationships. Domain-shift analysis
compares permitted partitions. Computed signal descriptors include band
power, Hjorth measures, spectral entropy, peak frequency, and regional
summaries; event-response and connectivity measurements are available
for relevant tasks. Model analyses inspect input adaptation, gradients,
optimization behavior, representations, perturbations, prediction
errors, and complementary outputs. The agent selects analyses according
to the current research question.

### B.4 Experience formation, reuse, and revision

Formation from research records. To form an experience, the knowledge
service assembles the decision rationale and expected effect with linked
analyses and experiment outcomes. The Coordinator examines the
implemented intervention and the alternatives that informed it, then
specifies when to recommend the action and what would warrant
reassessment. Each entry links supporting and conflicting evidence to
its source records. The shared catalog indexes these entries by source
task and model characteristics for retrieval across workspaces.

Retrieval and synthesis. Retrieval first assesses compatibility between
the source and target prediction tasks and model conditions, then ranks
recommendations by a deterministic relevance score. Exact model
agreement contributes 5 points and EEG-paradigm agreement 5; matching
prediction type and dataset each contribute 3. Prediction and
generalization units and input characteristics refine the score, while
goal and analysis-term overlap adds up to 4 points. Results expose
matched conditions and potential incompatibilities for the Coordinator’s
assessment. Repeated descriptions of the same source evidence are
deduplicated, then entries are grouped by source task and model
conditions and action terms.

Adoption and task context. The Coordinator reviews the synthesis and
relevant source details before assigning adopted recommendations to
target tasks with a context priority. Provisional recommendations
receive lower priority and a proposed low-cost applicability check. The
runtime exposes adopted experience to the Coordinator and specialist
contexts for the applicable tasks, grouped by action theme and ordered
by priority. A default 6,000-character budget bounds this context
summary, which retains actions and their conditions with references for
on-demand access to complete entries. Long entries are abbreviated
across their fields to retain the essential actions. When an experience
changes a research decision, the Coordinator links the decision to the
recommendation and revision used, connecting the subsequent outcome to
that advice.

Revision from subsequent evidence. Target results can lead the
Coordinator to amend an adopted recommendation’s action or
applicability, update its priority, or suspend its use, with supporting
observations and counterevidence attached to the revision. These
task-specific updates retain the original source recommendation. To
improve the shared recommendation itself, the Coordinator can reassess
its original evidence or incorporate later outcomes, recording a revised
recommendation with the reason for the change and links to preceding
evidence. Context refreshes propagate active revisions to subsequent
decisions and exclude withdrawn or superseded recommendations.

## Appendix E Controls, Costs, and Transfer

### E.1 External framework adaptations

CodeAct uses the implementation released with BrainBench and its Python
execution and feedback loop ([Wang et al., 2024c](#bib.bib25); [Zhou et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib41)). We use BrainPilot’s open-source
implementation, in which the Principal Investigator coordinates
specialist agents using the framework’s tools and research skills ([Li
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)). BrainAgent uses a paper-method
reimplementation of Supervisor-led task decomposition and domain-agent
execution ([Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib40)), extended with general
Python execution for training and prediction programs.

The adapters provide access to task data and model assets, together with
interfaces for executing programs and evaluating predictions. Execution
logs and metric feedback return to each framework’s own research loop,
allowing it to revise experiments and select a final predictor.

Setting B requires a shared encoder with task-specific predictors,
trained from the supplied LaBraM model and delivered as one checkpoint
covering all five targets. BrainAgent uses a custom encoder partly
initialized from LaBraM, with per-task sample limits to avoid execution
errors during shared-model training. Setting C permits new scientific
computing code for preprocessing and model training. Setting D provides
member predictions and development labels for learning sample-level
prediction selection. External baselines return a member choice per
sample, while NeuroAct additionally supports learned convex
combinations.

### E.2 Mechanism ablations

The ablations fix CBraMod and DeepSeek on Workload, BCIC-IV-2a, and
ADFTD to examine how individual research capabilities affect
optimization within the same backbone family. Each condition removes one
capability through runtime permissions or context filtering. Without
specialist personas, a single agent performs the research and
implementation in one conversation, with access to the remaining
research tools. Without EEG diagnostics, the analysis interface provides
descriptive data and signal-quality statistics, while task-directed
signal and model analyses are unavailable.

The code-editing control allows the agent to select registered training
recipes and adjust their exposed parameters; the full framework also
permits new scientific computing code in the workspace. Removing runtime
skills disables their contextual guidance and retrieval, with tool
interfaces and usage descriptions retained. The auxiliary-metric control
exposes only the primary BA score, withholding both auxiliary predictive
metrics and training dynamics. Full additionally receives macro-F1 and
AUROC, together with training-loss and gradient information. Each agent
conducts its own experiments in response to the feedback available in
its condition.

## Appendix F Model Interventions and Continued Research

This appendix describes the predictive programs developed by NeuroAct in
Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research").
It specifies how the agent implements model and training changes and
combines predictions, connecting research proposals to executable
interventions.

### F.1 Single-task model development

The ADFTD CBraMod study tests whether the predictor benefits from access
to individual patch representations. The Developer replaces average
pooling with a predictor consuming all patch outputs, while retaining
the pretrained encoder and full fine-tuning procedure. Both
configurations use 20 epochs, batch size 64, and learning rate
$`5\times 10^{-4}`$ on the same data split. This revision changes both
the information available to the predictor and its capacity, increasing
BA from 47.78% to 60.88%, macro-F1 from 0.481 to 0.611, and AUROC from
0.570 to 0.752. On BCIC-IV-2a, the selected REVE program similarly uses
a flattened predictor, with batch size eight and learning rate
$`2.4\times 10^{-4}`$ scaled by 0.1 for the encoder. It reaches 60.59%
BA against the restricted-research reference of 52.60%.

Training interventions test whether the existing architecture can
generalize better under a revised learning procedure. In Workload
research, NeuroAct implements Gaussian input perturbations whose
magnitude scales with the EEG amplitude; the transformation operates
during training, while inference uses the unmodified signal. A separate
study responds to majority-class predictions by implementing a loss
weighted by training-class frequency. A separate Workload study with
REVE explores stronger regularization by increasing weight decay from
0.01 to 0.1 and dropout from 0.3 to 0.5. These interventions change the
training program while preserving the prediction task.
App. [G](#A7 "Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
gives the perturbation formula and the feedback that motivates the loss
revision.

Open research also permits the agent to construct a different predictive
method. For ADFTD, one program computes relative spectral power and band
ratios with regional aggregation, then fits a gradient-boosting
classifier. Another implements ShallowFBCSPNet in place of EEGNet while
preserving class weights derived from the training data. The latter
raises BA from 54.93% to 71.26%. These are distinct research solutions,
with full results in
App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Detailed Results for the Four Research Settings ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
and the spectral development trajectory in
App. [G](#A7 "Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research").

### F.2 Joint training and shared-model refinement

Shared-model programs jointly train one encoder with task-specific
predictors for classification and HBN age regression. Input adapters
satisfy each target’s input requirements; per-task batch counts, loss
weights, and loss scales control contributions to shared updates.
Evaluation returns each target’s metrics and a joint utility with
specified metric directions, scales, and weights. In the LaBraM study,
utility is the equal-weight mean of four classification BA fractions and
HBN Pearson $`r`$. After developing a flattened predictor, NeuroAct
reduces the encoder learning-rate multiplier from 1 to 0.5 while
preserving the remaining configuration, raising validation utility from
0.5184 to 0.5520.
App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Detailed Results for the Four Research Settings ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
reports initial and revised shared-model results across encoder
families.

### F.3 Combination and routing programs

The programs in Setting D use complementary member predictions at
different levels. Workload changes how a pooled probability becomes a
class decision, HBN learns a global regression mixture, and ADFTD adapts
the mixture to each EEG window.

Workload decision rule. Let $`p_{m}(x)`$ be member $`m`$’s
positive-class probability and $`\mu(x)`$ their mean. The training
positive-class fraction $`q`$ determines a threshold $`t`$, taken as the
$`\lceil qN\rceil`$-th largest pooled score among the $`N`$ training
examples. NeuroAct implements the adjusted score

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(x)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\mu(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}-t,\min_{m}p_{m}(x),\max_{m}p_{m}(x)\right).
``` |  | (5) |

The shift in
Eq. [5](#A6.E5 "In F.3 Combination and routing programs ‣ Appendix F Model Interventions and Continued Research ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
moves the decision threshold toward the training-derived operating
point. Clipping keeps the score within the range of member
probabilities, so sample-dependent convex weights can represent it. The
program returns these weights and applies the usual 0.5 decision
threshold to the combined probability. Relative to equal weighting, BA
increases from 77.95% to 85.02%, with AUROC changing from 0.895 to
0.897.

HBN regression mixture. NeuroAct fits a nonnegative weight vector
summing to one by maximizing Pearson correlation on the development
predictions. It then shrinks the vector 10% toward equal weights. Nested
validation grouped by subject compares this mixture with a single-member
alternative and determines which to deploy. The resulting vector is
fixed across prediction examples, giving $`r=0.7801`$ and MAE 1.585. The
method uses the complementary errors of the members through a shared set
of weights.

ADFTD sample-dependent combination. The selected program computes 44
features from member posteriors, including confidence and disagreement,
and uses a linear softmax gate to assign member weights for each window.
The combined class probabilities are the convex sum of the member
outputs under these weights. Training minimizes class-weighted
cross-entropy with Adam at learning rate 0.05 for 300 steps;
subject-grouped validation compares gate configurations before the final
fit. The selected gate reaches 74.89% BA, compared with 71.26% for the
strongest member and 64.44% for equal weighting.
App. [G.6](#A7.SS6 "G.6 Turning complementary predictions into sample-dependent decisions ‣ Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
explains the analysis and successive revisions that lead to this
program, and
App. [D](#A4 "Appendix D Detailed Results for the Four Research Settings ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
provides complete metrics for all collaborative-prediction tasks.

### G.1 From montage inspection to spectral feature development

The ADFTD investigation in
Fig. [3](#A7.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ G.1 From montage inspection to spectral feature development ‣ Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
begins by examining the 19-channel montage and the available predictive
implementations. The Researcher identifies six scalp regions and notes
that the recording reference is unspecified. This favors spectral
features normalized within each window, whose construction does not
depend on comparing absolute power across recordings. The proposal
combines relative band power over 1–45 Hz with logarithmic ratios
involving delta, theta, alpha, and beta activity. Regional averages
preserve coarse spatial structure, while alpha peak frequency and
normalized spectral entropy summarize spectral shape.

![Refer to caption](2610.25211v1/case_g1_adftd_spectral.png)

Figure 3: ADFTD spectral development guided by montage and
prediction-error analysis.

The Developer implements a workspace prediction program using Welch
spectra with two-second segments and 50% overlap. Feature
standardization is fitted on training data, and a gradient-boosting
classifier learns the three-class decision function. The initial
validation BA of 53.91% warrants further investigation. Prediction-error
analysis finds that recall for frontotemporal dementia is 24.5%,
motivating a search for information complementary to spectral power. The
agent implements coherence features and a separate spatial-covariance
predictor; the latter maps shrinkage covariance matrices to a tangent
space with a training-fitted reference.

The coherence and covariance alternatives obtain 34.80% and 47.32%
validation BA, below the spectral predictor’s 53.91%. Their outcomes
keep the spectral method as the preferred measured solution, whose test
BA is 67.27% with macro-F1 0.648 and AUROC 0.811. The case demonstrates
method construction from EEG conditions and feedback-driven allocation
of further experiments, with each tested alternative answering a
specific question about the available signal.

### G.2 Input perturbation and generalization

In the Workload CBraMod study shown in
Fig. [4](#A7.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ G.2 Input perturbation and generalization ‣ Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research"),
the initial learning curve peaks early and then deteriorates,
accompanied by large gradient norms and variability across completed
training runs. This motivates regularization experiments, including a
custom training program that adds Gaussian perturbations scaled to the
current EEG tensor. The implementation uses

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x^{\prime}=x+0.01Q_{0.9}(|x|)\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`Q_{0.9}`$ is the 90th percentile of absolute input amplitude.
Perturbations are applied during training, while prediction uses the
unmodified input. This preserves amplitude-relative noise strength as
the signal scale changes.

![Refer to caption](2610.25211v1/case_g2_workload_perturbation.png)

Figure 4: Amplitude-scaled training perturbations in the Workload study.

The completed comparison raises test BA from 65.98% to 71.62%, macro-F1
from 0.635 to 0.654, and AUROC from 0.768 to 0.805. The case links a
diagnosed training behavior to an implemented data-side intervention.

### G.3 Training feedback redirects optimization toward class balance

In the separate Workload investigation summarized in

adjustment to an authored loss function illustrates the capability
tested by the code-editing ablation.

### G.5 Using complementarity at the appropriate prediction stage

In the Workload collaborative prediction study shown in
Fig. [7](#A7.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ G.5 Using complementarity at the appropriate prediction stage ‣ Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research"),

decision. NeuroAct implements a shift based on the training class

result. BA reaches 85.02% and AUROC increases to 0.897, with macro-F1

single-member alternative. The resulting program reaches Pearson

intervention at different stages of prediction according to the observed

### G.6 Turning complementary predictions into sample-dependent decisions

The ADFTD study in
Fig. [8](#A7.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ G.6 Turning complementary predictions into sample-dependent decisions ‣ Appendix G Case Analyses ‣ NeuroAct: Advancing EEG Analysis and Decoding via Dual-Loop Autonomous Research")
investigates how to combine member predictions into a predictor that
improves on the strongest member. The initial investigation compares
member errors and class recall, then evaluates routing rules with
subject-grouped validation. Posterior-based probes outperform their
counterparts with additional spectral and Hjorth features, directing
development toward the information already present in model outputs. The
first evaluated sample-dependent router achieves 69.56% BA and 0.866
AUROC. Its strong ranking performance, alongside BA below the strongest
member’s 71.26%, leads the Coordinator to request a revision focused on
class-balanced decisions.

![Refer to caption](2610.25211v1/case_g6_adftd_routing.png)

Figure 8: ADFTD router development from prediction analysis to a
sample-dependent combination.

Using fixed member predictions, the Developer implements convex
combinations with per-window softmax weights, an optional
class-conditioned component, and shrinkage toward the strongest member.
The router uses 44 features summarizing member probabilities,
confidence, and disagreement. It fits a class-weighted cross-entropy
objective, evaluates held-out subjects in 11 outer folds, and selects
configurations using grouped validation within each training fold. The
final fit selects the sample-dependent gate without the optional
class-conditioned component or anchor shrinkage.

The Coordinator selects the revised router, which reaches 74.89% BA,
0.729 macro-F1, and 0.843 AUROC. BA improves by 3.63 points over the
strongest member and 10.45 points over equal weighting. Frozen
deployment weights contain 789 distinct rows at four-decimal precision
across 1,155 windows, with every member receiving the largest weight on
some windows. An analysis of the outer-fold validation predictions
further shows how the decision rule redistributes errors. Relative to
ShallowFBCSPNet, it corrects 234 previously incorrect windows and
changes 161 correct predictions to errors, raising validation BA from
37.70% to 45.03%. Recall for frontotemporal dementia increases from
3.15% to 20.98%, alongside changes in the other classes. The case
illustrates how analysis of complementary predictions and metric
feedback can guide the agent toward a useful sample-dependent
combination.
````
