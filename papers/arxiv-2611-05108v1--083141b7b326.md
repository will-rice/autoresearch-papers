###### Abstract

Autoresearch has shown that agents can autonomously write code, run
experiments, and iteratively improve machine learning solutions. Yet
most evaluations start a new agent session from scratch for each new
problem, making them costly and leaving unclear whether agents can
retain and apply prior insights when adapting to new tasks. We introduce
Continual AutoResearch, an open-ended environment that compares
persistent and reset instances of the same agent under matched task
streams, data, and time budget. Continual AutoResearch covers nine task
families, spanning synthetic tasks, symbolic regression, time-series
forecasting, and tabular AutoML. Across five frontier agents, we measure
first-solution quality, research efficiency, and final held-out
performance. To distinguish insight transfer from session continuity, we
run a memory ablation that vary what information agents retain from
prior tasks. We show that persistent state improves research efficiency
of all five agents, allowing them to find stronger solutions sooner.
However, these gains do not consistently translate into better final
solutions. Overall, our findings suggest that retaining experience and
utilizing it effectively are distinct capabilities.

## 1 Introduction

Recently, there has been a growing interest in scaling frontier language
models to different AI R&D processes ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib22);
[Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). From optimizing a set of hyperparameters
to post-training small language models, agents are becoming more
autonomous, and more capable of tackling complex and lengthy tasks
([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib15); [Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). As these
agents shift towards longer horizon tasks, they increasingly accumulate
insights, strategies, and practical experience. In real-world research,
scientists often carry lessons from one project to the next, whether by
reusing code or reapplying a specific post-training algorithm for
example. We ask the question of whether experience acquired from one
problem can benefit subsequent ones. Answering this requires
understanding whether agents can retain knowledge across tasks and use
it to adapt more effectively to new ones, bridging continual learning
and meta-learning ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Asawa et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4); [Shu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). We study this setting
without updating model weights, in which case experience is transferred
only through the state accumulated during execution.

Existing evaluations treat each R&D problem in isolation by resetting
the agent from scratch before each new task. Although autoresearch-style
loops allow agents to use feedback to optimize solutions iteratively,
the acquired experience is discarded once the task is finished.
Consequently, these evaluations cannot assess if lessons about
experimental design, modeling choices, or execution failures can be
applied to related tasks and improve future research. While resetting
prevents irrelevant or misleading experience from carrying over, it may
also force agents to rediscover useful practices or repeat avoidable
mistakes. However, retaining experience is not necessarily beneficial
([Shu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). Experience becomes useful only to the
extent the tasks share transferable structure, such as common
data-generating factors, modeling principles, or experimental
approaches. Thus, whether autonomous research agents can identify and
adapt such transferable experience to new tasks remains largely
unevaluated.

We introduce Continual AutoResearch, an agent-agnostic evaluation
environment for studying whether agents can retain and use prior ML
experience while adapting to new research tasks. We compare persistent
and reset instances of the same fixed model under identical tasks, data,
and time budgets. Our environment combines controlled synthetic tasks,
symbolic regression, time series forecasting and tabular ML problems. It
measures initialization, search efficiency, and outcome gains. However,
a stateful session incorporates multiple potential sources of
improvement, such as transcript history, code reuse, procedural
familiarity, and scientific insight. Continual AutoResearch contributes,
to our knowledge, the first study of a continual AI R&D setting
analyzing whether agents can efficiently adapt to new research tasks.

Contributions Our main contributions are:

- •
  We introduce Continual AutoResearch, an open-ended environment for
  evaluating whether agents continually adapt by applying prior
  experience to new ML research tasks. We pair persistent and reset runs
  on matched task streams spanning nine task families across synthetic
  tasks, symbolic regression, time series forecasting, and tabular

- •

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Automated AI R&D

Automated AI R&D studies whether agents can do research with limited
supervision. Karpathy’s autoresearch ([Karpathy, 2026](#bib.bib15))
introduces a minimal loop in which an agent edits a training script,
evaluates the result, and keeps or discards the change. PostTrainBench
([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)), InferenceBench ([Yeon et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)), and Claudini ([Panfilov et al., 2026](#bib.bib3))
extend local optimization to LLM post-training, inference systems, and
adversarial attack discovery. AutoLab ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib16))
broadens evaluation to long-horizon closed-loop optimization across
research and engineering tasks, while AutoScientists ([Gao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)) studies self-organizing agent teams that share
experimental state during long-running scientific search.
AutoResearchBench ([Xiong et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) focuses on
multi-step scientific literature discovery, and AutoResearch AI ([Tie et
al., 2026](#bib.bib19)) surveys automation across the broader research
workflow. However, these works primarily measure progress within a
single research problem. We instead ask whether experience transfers
across ML tasks and measure its effect on initialization, search
efficiency, and final selection.

### 2.2 Continual Learning in Language Agents

Continual-learning benchmarks evaluate whether language agents can use
experience from earlier tasks. Earlier systems such as ExpeL ([Zhao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib21)) study extracting and reusing textual experience
across tasks, while LifelongAgentBench ([Zheng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)) evaluates knowledge accumulation over interdependent
tasks in interactive environments. Continual Learning Bench ([Asawa et
al., 2026](#bib.bib4)) spans software engineering, signal processing,
epidemiological forecasting, database querying, strategic games, and
demand forecasting. AgentCL ([Shu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)) constructs
controlled streams across coding, deep research, and language reasoning
to evaluate memory and cross-task composition. FutureSim ([Goel et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6)) studies world-event forecasting from chronologically
revealed news, while SWE-Bench-CL ([Joshi et al., 2025](#bib.bib7))
organizes software-engineering issues into repository-level sequences.
Continual AutoResearch differs in what is being evaluated: every task is
an iterative, executable ML research loop in which an agent forms
modeling hypotheses, edits and runs code, generates multiple solutions,
and selects a final solution under a fixed budget. We measure how prior
state changes initialization, research exploration, and final outcome
across a sequence of these loops.

## 3 Framework

### 3.1 Setup

We formulate continual AI R&D as an ordered sequence of research tasks
$`\mathcal{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{K})`$. A task $`T_{i}`$ consists of a
task description and instructions, training and development data, and a
fixed wall-clock budget $`B_{i}`$. Within $`B_{i}`$, the agent can
experiment without restrictions. The agent can submit multiple solutions
and need to select a final one before the budget expires. All
submissions are then evaluated on a hidden test set after the run. To
prevent the agent from reward hacking, intermediate submissions test
scores are hidden. The environment does not provide a specific harness
for planning or memory management. Agents operate through their native
CLI interfaces and can modify files, execute code, and inspect visible
feedback autonomously. Models and budget values are specified in the
experimental setup described in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?").

### 3.2 Stateful and Stateless Agents

We compare two evaluation settings: stateful execution, in which the
same agent instance persists across tasks, and stateless execution, in
which each task resets with a fresh instance of the same agent. Let
$`s_{i}`$ denote the agent’s working state when it begins task
$`T_{i}`$, including its session history, files, experimental results,
and execution experience. Under stateful execution, $`s_{i}`$ may
include information retained from tasks $`T_{1},\ldots,T_{i-1}`$; under
stateless execution, the working state is reset before each task and
contains no information from earlier tasks.

Stateful execution. We resume the same native agent session and
workspace across the stream. Conversation history, files, experimental
results, execution traces can then carry from one task to the other.
Feedback from earlier tasks remains visible in this state. We do not
provide an additional harness-written memory or summary. Everything is
handled through their native CLI harness.

Stateless execution. We launch a new agent session and clean workspace
for every task. No transcript, files, code, or execution state from
earlier tasks is available. We otherwise match the task, model,
environment, wall-clock budget, and evaluation procedure to the stateful
run. Since task $`T_{1}`$ doesn’t have access to any prior sessions, our
transfer results are computed over $`T_{2},\ldots,T_{K}`$.

### 3.3 Measuring Cross-Task Improvement

We use different metrics for different tasks following their original
setup. This includes accuracy, F1 for classification, RMSE for time
series, and $`R^{2}`$ for symbolic regression. Following the
normalized-score approach used by SELA ([Chi et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)),
we normalize each metric between $`[0,1]`$, where higher is better. This
transformation allows aggregate gains to be comparable across task
families and allows the framework to include tasks with different
evaluation metrics. Let $`m`$ denote a raw metric value and $`\mu`$ its
metric type. The transformation is:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
r(m;\mu)=\begin{cases}m,&\mu\in\{\mathrm{Accuracy},\mathrm{F1}\},\\
(1+m)^{-1},&\mu=\mathrm{RMSE},\\
\bigl(2-\min\{m,1\}\bigr)^{-1},&\mu=R^{2}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (1) |

Accuracy and F1 are already bounded between 0 and 1, so they are left
unchanged. RMSE is a lower-is-better metric that can take on any
positive real value; $`(1+m)^{-1}`$ maps zero error to one and larger
errors toward zero. RMSE is standardized using the standard deviation of
each channel’s training set before the transformation. $`R^{2}`$ is
higher-is-better, bounded above by one, and unbounded below;
$`(2-\min\{m,1\})^{-1}`$ maps one to one and increasingly negative
values toward zero. These transformations preserve the ordering of
solutions within each task while placing every metric on a common scale.
We do not average unnormalized raw metrics across heterogeneous tasks.
For any higher-is-better measure $`q`$, let $`M^{q}_{i,c}`$ denote its
value on task $`T_{i}`$ under setting $`c`$. We define its paired gain
in normalized reward points as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{i}^{q}=100\left(M^{q}_{i,\mathrm{stateful}}-M^{q}_{i,\mathrm{stateless}}\right).
``` |  | (2) |

A positive gain indicates that stateful execution performs better. We
report gains for three complementary metrics that capture different
parts of the research process: final selected reward
($`q=\mathrm{out}`$), first-solution reward ($`q=\mathrm{init}`$), and
fixed-horizon search utility ($`q=\mathrm{search}`$). These metrics
respectively measure the quality of the final solution, the quality of
the agent’s initial solution, and its ability to find and explore strong
solutions efficiently within the task budget. For aggregate results, we
first average gains within each task stream and then average across
streams.

Final performance alone does not reveal how prior experience changes the
research process. In practice, agents run under limited time and compute
budgets, making both the quality of their first solution and the speed
at which they explore other strong solutions important. We define
initialization quality as the hidden-test reward of the first valid
submission and use the corresponding initialization gain
$`\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{init}}`$ to compare the two settings. To measure
efficiency over the full budget, the evaluator scores every valid
submission after the run and tracks the best reward achieved by each
point in time. For setting $`c`$, let $`r_{ijc}`$ be the hidden reward
of attempt $`j`$ completed at time $`t_{ijc}`$, and let $`B_{i}`$ be the
task budget. The normalized area under this best-so-far curve defines
the fixed-horizon search utility:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
U_{i,c}=\frac{1}{B_{i}}\int_{0}^{B_{i}}\max_{j:t_{ijc}\leq t}r_{ijc}\,dt.
``` |  | (3) |

Before the first valid submission, we set the curve to zero. If a run
ends early, its last value is carried to the end of the budget. Higher
utility therefore means that better solutions are found earlier: the
metric jointly captures solution quality and time of discovery. We set
$`M^{\mathrm{search}}_{i,c}=U_{i,c}`$ and call
$`\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{search}}`$ the time-convergence gain. Our primary
transfer metric is its mean over $`i=2,\ldots,K`$; initialization and
outcome gains provide complementary views of the first valid and final
selected solutions. The curve is constructed only after the run and is
never shown to the agent in case it tries to reward hack.

## 4 Settings

### 4.1 Design Rationale

We evaluate agents on ordered task streams whose tasks share reusable
structure but differ in their target, dataset, or sampling process. This
prevents the solution of the previous problem to be resubmitted directly
by the agent. Instead, agents must identify what transfers, re-estimate
what is task-specific, and use model feedback to verify earlier
assumptions. The settings progress from controlled synthetic tasks with
known recurring structure to realistic tasks with similar methodological
approaches. This allows us to study transfer progressively, from
recurring scientific structure to more general research practices. We
report public dataset names for clarity, but agents receive neutral
identifiers during evaluation to prevent the agent of recognizing the
dataset and taking shortcuts.

### 4.2 Evaluation Settings

#### Synthetic AutoML.

Synthetic AutoML models a setting where the same underlying dataset is
reused across multiple tasks, as often occurs in organizations that
build different models from a shared pool of data. The setting is
composed of three streams that contain five classification tasks with
different target rules. In the shared-latent stream, observed features
are noisy mixtures of recurring latent factors, so representation
discovery can transfer while the predictive factors change. In the
shared-sparse stream, each target depends on a different subset of a
small pool of relevant features, making feature-selection and
regularization strategies transferable even though the exact predictive
features change across tasks. In the shared-regime stream, a recurring
regime variable affects each target differently, rewarding agents that
retest regime-dependent interactions rather than purely relying on
previous solutions.

#### Symbolic regression.

Symbolic regression is an interesting setting that tests transfer of a
scientific search strategy rather than a fitted predictor. The five-task
canonical-coordinates stream contains equations that become simpler
after an appropriate change of variables, including centering,
rescaling, or combining variables into a radial coordinate. We also
include an asymmetric control task to test whether the agent verifies a
previously useful symmetry instead of applying it automatically. Given
noisy training and development samples, the agent submits an explicit
expression that is evaluated on private interpolation and extrapolation
samples using mean $`R^{2}`$.

#### Time-series forecasting.

Time-series forecasting provides three levels of naturally occurring
similarity. The ETT cross-resolution stream ([Zhou et al.,
2021](#bib.bib10)) varies station and sampling frequency within the same
electricity-transformer system, requiring periods and context lengths to
be recalibrated. The PEMS stream ([Song et al., 2020](#bib.bib11)) moves
across traffic sensor networks and time periods, preserving domain-level
seasonality while changing channel relationships. The energy-system
stream ([Lai et al., 2018](#bib.bib12); [Godahewa et al.,
2021](#bib.bib14)) spans broader sources and sampling rates, allowing
for more open-ended transfer across tasks. Across the streams, agents
can reuse procedures for normalization, lag construction, periodicity
detection, seasonal baselines, and chronological validation, but must
avoid overrelying on prior solutions. For evaluation, agents submit
executable forecasts, which are then run on hidden test splits using
RMSE.

#### Tabular AutoML.

Tabular AutoML uses a five-task subset of the datasets used in SELA’s
evaluation ([Chi et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)), drawn from AMLB ([Gijsbers
et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)) and Kaggle competitions, and a three-task
slice of MLE-Bench Lite ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). We use fixed
subsets so that the full task stream remains feasible to run across all
agents while preserving the same task order and per-task budget. Unlike
the controlled settings, each task uses a different real-world dataset
with its own characteristics and requirements. These streams test
whether agents can transfer useful research practices across datasets
while adapting to the specific requirements of each new task.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate five frontier agents via their native CLI harnesses: Sonnet
4.6 and Opus 5 through Claude Code, GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.6-Sol through
Codex, and Kimi K3 through OpenCode. The evaluation covers four task
domains and nine task families, each of which contains three to five
tasks. The task budget $`B_{i}`$ is set to default to 30 minutes unless
otherwise specified. For each ordered stream, we compare a stateful run
with reset runs using the same exact setup. To assess run-to-run
variability, we repeated the matched stateful–reset comparison three
times for GPT-5.6-Sol (Appendix
Figure [4](#A2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Outcome and initialization gains varied by at most 0.75 points, and
time-convergence gains by at most 7.14, both small relative to the
aggregate effects. Since repeating the full benchmark is costly, all
other comparisons use a single matched run pair.

### 5.2 Persistent State Improves Research Efficiency

Mean time-convergence gain is positive for all five agents: $`+21.3`$
normalized reward points for Kimi K3, $`+20.9`$ for Sonnet 4.6, $`+7.5`$
for Opus 5, $`+6.1`$ for GPT-5.6-Sol, and $`+3.0`$ for GPT-5.4
(Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.2 Persistent State Improves Research Efficiency ‣ 5 Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Time-convergence measures fixed-budget research efficiency using the
highest hidden-test reward generated by each submission within the
budget, with hidden scores computed post hoc. This metric measures both
speed and quality: a solution contributes more the higher its reward and
the earlier it is produced. The reward trajectories
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Tabular AutoML. ‣ 4.2 Evaluation Settings ‣ 4 Settings ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")
show that persistent agents produce higher reward solutions sooner,
while reset agents often close the gap near the deadline. To determine
how long this advantage persists, we compare the best reward achieved at
25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the budget (Appendix
Table [8](#A2.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Sensitivity to the evaluation horizon. ‣ B.1 Time-Convergence Robustness ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Stateful execution leads for all five agents at 25% and 50%, but the
mean gap decreases from $`+24.0`$ points at 25% to $`+1.2`$ at the
deadline, where only two agents remain positive. Persistence therefore
only shows consistent advantage early in the research process. On the
controlled synthetic streams, this advantage increases with task
position
(Figure [11](#A2.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
For all models except Sonnet 4.6, the mean advantage across the
synthetic task families is larger on every later task than on Task 1,
suggesting that accumulated experience becomes more useful as the stream
progresses.

To determine what drives the time-convergence gains,
Table [7](#A2.T7 "Table 7 ‣ B.1 Time-Convergence Robustness ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")
reports time to the first valid solution, first-solution quality, valid
candidate counts, and gains after controlling for candidate count and
first-solution latency. Stateful execution reaches the first valid
solution sooner for every agent, with the largest reductions for Kimi K3
($`9.2`$ minutes) and Sonnet 4.6 ($`8.6`$ minutes). It also produces
fewer valid candidates for four of five agents. The gain is then not
explained by producing more candidates; invalid, unchanged, or
lower-reward submissions do not improve the best-so-far curve.
First-solution quality is also higher for four agents, most clearly
Sonnet 4.6 ($`+6.8`$ points) and GPT-5.4 ($`+3.7`$). Sonnet benefits
from both faster initialization and a stronger first solution, whereas
Kimi’s large full-budget gain is driven mainly by reaching its first
valid solution sooner.

Matching candidate counts reduces the gain for every agent. Sonnet keeps
a $`+6.5`$-point advantage, Kimi K3 and GPT-5.4 retain smaller gains,
and Opus 5 and GPT-5.6-Sol fall near zero. Starting each trajectory at
its first valid solution produces the same pattern, although this
control still preserves differences in first-solution quality. Under a
stricter control that also subtracts each run’s first-valid reward, the
gain becomes negative for every agent
(Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Time-Convergence Robustness ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Persistent state therefore improves research efficiency by producing a
valid solution sooner or by producing a stronger first solution. The
current results do not show a general improvement in optimization after
initialization.

### 5.3 Faster Search Does Not Consistently Improve Final Performance

Final outcome gains are less consistent than time-convergence gains.
Sonnet 4.6 is the only agent with a substantial mean final outcome gain,
at $`+7.6`$ points. Kimi K3 and GPT-5.4 remain near zero at $`+0.4`$ and
$`+0.2`$ points, while GPT-5.6-Sol and Opus 5 decline by $`-0.8`$ and
$`-0.9`$ points. We also report outcome win rate which is the proportion
of transfer tasks on which the stateful final solution outperforms the
reset solution. Unlike outcome gain, it measures the consistency of
transfer across tasks rather than its average magnitude. It ranges from
$`34.3\%`$ for Opus to $`66.7\%`$ for Sonnet.

Faster search also does not necessarily improve the final solution. A
reset run may reach a solution of comparable quality later in the task
budget, or an agent may generate a strong candidate and select a weaker
one that overfits. We measure the latter with selection regret: the best
hidden reward generated during a run minus the hidden reward of its
selected solution. A positive stateful-minus-reset difference means that
stateful execution has greater selection regret. This difference ranges
from $`-0.7`$ to $`+1.3`$ points across agents and remains within
$`\pm 0.8`$ points for four of five agents. Selection failures can be
severe on individual tasks (Appendix
Figure [16](#A4.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ D.2 Finding a Strong Candidate Is Not Enough ‣ Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")),
but they do not explain the aggregate difference between search
efficiency and final outcomes.

Transfer gains also depend on model capability. Opus 5 and GPT-5.6-Sol
have the strongest reset performance, but their initialization and
outcome gains are smaller than those of Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4,
respectively (Appendix
Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Sonnet 4.6 obtains $`+7.0`$ initialization and $`+7.6`$ outcome gains,
compared with $`+1.3`$ and $`-0.9`$ for Opus 5. GPT-5.4 similarly
obtains $`+3.0`$ and $`+0.2`$, compared with $`-1.0`$ and $`-0.8`$ for
GPT-5.6-Sol. More capable models may already produce strong solutions
from a reset state, leaving less room to benefit from persistence.
However, model capability does not consistently predict time-convergence
gain. Transfer also varies by task family, with no family except SELA
AutoML improving uniformly across models (Appendix
Figure [8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

### 5.4 Time Budget Changes the Value of Persistence

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.4 Time Budget Changes the Value of Persistence ‣ 5 Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")
compares stateful and reset execution of GPT-5.6-Sol on Shared Regime
under three different time budget: 15min, 30min and 60min. As the time
budget increases, the mean final outcome gain becomes smaller while the
mean initialization gain grows. We also observe a decrease in
time-convergence gains, which is consistent with what we would expect of
a reset run narrowing an early stateful advantage through within-task
exploration. Conversely, longer preceding tasks may give the stateful
agent more experience to transfer to its first solution. Together, these
observations emphasize the tradeoff of the different gains with respect
to the task time horizon.

### 5.5 Explicit Memory Changes Search but Is Not Reliably Beneficial

To separate explicit knowledge transfer from uninterrupted session
state, we run a memory ablation on the Energy-systems stream with three
conditions: matched memory, shuffled memory, and no memory. Every task
starts with a fresh agent session, and information can transfer only
through insight cards. These cards are agent-authored summaries of
earlier experiments that record claims, supporting evidence, confidence,
scope, recommended actions, and failure conditions. In the
matched-memory condition, before each task, the agent receives the full
set of cards accumulated from earlier tasks in the same stream. In the
shuffled-memory condition, it follows a similar setup, but the cards
come from an unrelated stream. In the no-memory condition, it receives
no cards. Comparing matched with shuffled memory tests whether the
relevance of the transferred content matters, while comparison with no
memory tests the effect of providing cards at all.

Relevant cards don’t consistently result in gains. Compared with
shuffled cards, matched cards improve initialization for Opus 5
($`+2.6`$ points), GPT-5.4 ($`+5.9`$), and GPT-5.6-Sol ($`+3.9`$), but
reduce time-convergence for all three. Sonnet shows the reverse pattern
where initialization declines by $`2.9`$ points while time-convergence
improves by $`25.6`$. Final matched-versus-shuffled outcome effects are
small for Sonnet, Opus, and GPT-5.4, and negative for GPT-5.6-Sol.
Matched and shuffled cards also produce comparable final gains over no
memory for Sonnet and GPT-5.4. These results show that relevant
information about prior experiments affect initialization and search
efficiency but do not consistently improve final performance.
(Appendix [B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Memory Contrasts ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

The logged agent traces also illustrate how persistent memory change
agent behavior. Stateful Sonnet reuses and recalibrates a forecasting
pipeline; matched cards lead Codex to adapt a seasonal-residual
hypothesis to a new sampling rate; and highly specific cards anchor
Sonnet to a weaker model family
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Agent Trace Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
The failure cases further show agents replacing robust candidates with
overfitted ones or applying an earlier operational lesson too strongly
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
These cases are good examples of why reuse, verification, exploration,
and final selection must be evaluated separately.

## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 What Persistence Changes

Persistent state improves mean fixed-budget time-convergence for all
five agents, primarily by helping them reach valid solutions earlier
and, for some agents, with higher initial quality. Sonnet benefits from
both, whereas several other agents benefit mainly from earlier
initialization
(Table [7](#A2.T7 "Table 7 ‣ B.1 Time-Convergence Robustness ‣ Appendix B Complete Results ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
Only Sonnet retains a clear advantage after matching candidate counts
and excluding the latency before the first valid solution. Once each
run’s first-solution reward is also subtracted, no agent retains an
advantage. These process gains do not consistently translate into better
final performance.

The benefit of persistence also varies across the stream. The
task-position analysis shows larger search gains on later synthetic
tasks, suggesting that useful experience can accumulate. However,
variation across task families and the memory ablation show that
additional history is not automatically beneficial. Within the Claude
and GPT model families, agents with lower reset performance have more
room for initialization and outcome gains, while time-convergence gains
follow a different pattern. Persistence is therefore not uniformly
beneficial across agents, tasks, or metrics.

### 6.2 Implications for Continual Research Agents

Overall, our findings favor persistent execution when compute or time
budgets are limited. In the time-horizon ablation, the gains from
persistence for both final outcome and time to convergence decrease as
budgets grow. This is consistent with our observation of reset agents
partially catching up through within-task optimization. Persistence is
therefore most valuable when budgets are tight, where its advantage
narrows as they grow.

Our results also identify two ways agents can fail to adapt effectively
across tasks. First, agents can apply an earlier lesson too broadly,
narrowing their search around assumptions that do not hold on the
current task. Second, agents can find a strong candidate early but later
replace it using unreliable development evidence
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).
These observations do not suggest a single memory or search policy, but
they show that retaining experience is insufficient without mechanisms
for verifying and revising it. These risks may become more important for
deployed agents operating across longer task sequences. An incorrect
assumption, brittle code path, or obsolete instruction retained in a
workspace can influence multiple later tasks. When state includes
externally supplied artifacts, persistent reuse could also allow
misleading or malicious content introduced during one task to affect
future behavior. We do not evaluate adversarial manipulation in this
work, but the observed failures motivate testing safeguards such as
provenance tracking, explicit applicability conditions, and
evidence-based memory revision. They also motivate process-level
evaluation: final performance alone cannot distinguish late candidate
discovery, execution failures, weak candidate generation, and incorrect
final selection.

### 6.3 Limitations and Future Work

We evaluate each model through its native CLI scaffold, so stateful and
reset comparisons are matched within each agent, but cross-agent
differences may reflect both the model and its scaffold. Our ablations
also remain limited in scope. Future work should separately test
insights, code, transcripts, and execution state, together with explicit
mechanisms for selecting or forgetting memories. More broadly, our
streams contain only three to five tasks with 30-minute budgets. It
would be interesting to scale to streams with hundred of tasks, or with
tasks that require much longer time budget such as LLM post-training
like PostTrainBench ([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) who are of the
order of 10 hours. These experiments would test further mechanisms that
are less studied in our work such as compaction, etc. Another
interesting direction along this line of work is to study continual
learning in a multi-agent system. At larger scale, research is also
likely to be distributed across multiple agents working in parallel
similar to a research team or research company. Continual improvement is
comparatively straightforward to measure for a single agent. In a
multi-agent system, however, what each agent learns and how that
knowledge spreads depend on the interactions between agents and the
topology connecting them, which makes cross-task improvement harder to
attribute and evaluate.

### 6.4 Conclusion

We introduced Continual AutoResearch, an environment for evaluating
research agents across related tasks rather than in isolation. Across
five frontier agents and nine task families, persistent state improves
mean fixed-budget research efficiency by helping agents produce stronger
solutions sooner, with the balance between speed and quality varying by
agent. Sonnet 4.6 is the only agent that retains a substantial advantage
under the candidate-matched and post-first timing controls and obtains a
substantial aggregate final-outcome gain. Retaining experience and
leveraging it well are therefore separate capabilities: prior code,
hypotheses, and procedures can accelerate and strengthen initialization,
while weak follow-up search, anchoring, or poor final selection can
erase the benefit. Effective continual research requires selective
memory, explicit tests of transferred assumptions, continued exploration
of alternatives, and robust candidate selection.

## Ethics Statement

We propose a new environment for studying AI agents that carry
experience across research tasks, a capability we show could accelerate
automated machine learning research. The long-horizon autonomy of the
agents naturally introduces certain risks such as unmonitored code
execution, compute usage, credential leakage, etc. We log the agents’
edits, executions, and submissions for post-hoc analysis. Empirically,
we report performance and failures so that failure modes of agent
trajectories remain visible. We encourage active monitoring of
autonomous agents’ reasoning and actions to ensure safe model behavior.

We used LLMs to assist with code development, figure plotting, table
formatting and writing polishing for grammar and syntax errors. All
generated outputs were reviewed and revised by the authors.

All datasets, prompts, instructions, and others are provided in the

## Appendix A Detailed Setting Descriptions

In the following appendix section, we provide details about each stream
in terms of design and configuration.

### A.1 Dataset Anonymization

To prevent agents from recognizing popular benchmark names such as ETTh1
or PEMS07 and immediately applying a known solution, we give them
neutral task and dataset identifiers during their evaluation. This
prevents inflation of first-solution quality and apparent search
efficiency without any need for cross-task learning.

### A.2 Synthetic AutoML

We design the synthetic AutoML suite so that it isolates transfer across
prediction objectives over a shared data source. This is inspired by
companies having different modeling objectives using the same client
database. The setting contains three task families each with five
classification tasks generated from the same feature matrix, but with
different target rules. We give the agent the knowledge that all tasks
share the same data, but do not specify what is reusable across tasks.

#### Reported configuration.

For each task family, the data generator creates one $`6000\times 150`$
feature matrix and divides its rows, without overlap, into 2,000
training, 2,000 development, and 2,000 private-test examples. The
generators are deterministic for the reported configuration. The feature
matrix, task order, splits, and labels are identical for stateful and
stateless runs.

Shared latent. In the shared latent task family, observed features are
noisy mixtures of latent factors. Each target depends on a different
subset of a shared causal pool. The ideal transfer is if the agent can
use the earlier tasks to identify useful representations in the presence
of correlated feature and high-variance nuisance factors.

The data generator samples 24 standard-normal latent factors. The six
potentially causal factors are scaled by 0.7 and the remaining nuisance
factors by 1.6 before projection through a $`24\times 150`$
standard-normal matrix; standard-normal feature noise is then added. For
generated task index $`t`$, let $`p=(t-1)\bmod 6`$, $`s=(t+1)\bmod 6`$,
and $`h=(t+2)\bmod 6`$. The score is
$`0.95z_{p}-0.75z_{s}+0.45\tanh(1.2z_{5})+0.32z_{h}z_{p}+0.22\sin(z_{s}z_{5})+\epsilon`$,
where $`\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.55^{2})`$.

Shared sparse. In the shared sparse task family, each target depends on
a changing subset of variables sampled from a small shared relevant
pool. The ideal transfer is if the agent can find an efficient procedure
for feature screening, regularization, and validation.

The base features are standard normal. Features
$`x_{020},\ldots,x_{029}`$ are noisy copies of
$`x_{000},\ldots,x_{009}`$, with Gaussian noise of standard deviation
0.2. Each task selects three features without replacement from the
shared pool $`x_{000},\ldots,x_{007}`$ and computes
$`1.7x_{a}-1.4x_{b}+0.9x_{c}^{2}+\epsilon`$, where
$`\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.35^{2})`$.

Shared regime. In the shared regime task family, every task contains the
same regime variable. However, its effect changes depending on the
target. The ideal transfer is if the agent can find earlier tasks that
motivate testing for regime-dependent interactions. Each new task would
then require the agent to re-estimate the interaction rather than reuse
on earlier rule.

Features $`x_{000},\ldots,x_{148}`$ are standard normal, and the visible
feature $`x_{149}`$ is the indicator
$`r=\mathbb{1}[x_{000}+0.6x_{001}-0.25x_{002}>0]`$. Odd tasks use
$`1.7x_{003}-x_{004}+0.5x_{005}`$ when $`r=0`$ and
$`-1.2x_{003}+2.2x_{006}-0.6x_{007}`$ when $`r=1`$. Even tasks use
$`1.1x_{004}+0.8x_{005}^{2}`$ when $`r=0`$ and
$`-1.8x_{005}+1.3x_{008}+0.4x_{009}`$ when $`r=1`$. Gaussian noise with
standard deviation 0.35 is added. Thus, the regime definition transfers
across tasks while its conditional prediction rule can change.

All three streams use a fixed five-task order. The agent receives
labeled training and development data and submits development
predictions, a serialized model, and an inference manifest. The private
evaluator loads the model to predict test features that are unavailable
to the agent. Binary tasks use private-test accuracy and multiclass
tasks use private-test weighted F1.

### A.3 Symbolic Regression

The symbolic regression setting tests whether agent are able to transfer
scientific search strategy. It contains five task in a
canonical-coordinates stream where the equations first appear
compliecated but become simpler after an appropriate change of
coordinates. Relevant transformations include centering an input,
rescaling variables, and combining multiple variables into a radial
coordinate. The variables, parameters, and final equation change across
tasks, so the agent cannot reuse a previously discovered expression.

#### Reported configuration.

Reported results use the canonical-coordinates family and the fixed task
order shown in
Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Reported configuration. ‣ A.3 Symbolic Regression ‣ Appendix A Detailed Setting Descriptions ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?").
Each task contains 384 training and 384 development rows, 2,000 private
interpolation rows, and 2,000 private extrapolation rows. The number of
generic features is 16. The data generator also randomly places the
relevant variables among these columns and samples every irrelevant
feature independently from $`\mathcal{U}[-2,2]`$.

Let $`v_{j}`$ denote the $`j`$-th relevant variable and
$`u_{j}=(v_{j}-b_{j})/s_{j}`$ its centered and scaled value. Unless a
smaller range is specified by the template, coefficient magnitudes are
sampled uniformly from $`[0.55,2.75]`$ with a random sign, centers from
$`[-1.25,1.25]`$, and scales from $`[0.65,2.5]`$. The modulation
coefficient in Tasks 2 and 3 uses magnitude $`[0.2,0.8]`$, and the cubic
coefficient in Task 4 uses $`[0.15,0.65]`$. Values are rounded to three
decimals and stored with the sampled relevant-feature indices in a
private ground-truth manifest. The resulting equation templates are:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| Task |  Equation template | Rel./all | Control |
| 1 |  $`c+au_{1}^{2}+qu_{1}\sin(u_{2})+r\cos(v_{3}+u_{1}u_{2})`$ | 3/9 | No |
| 2 |  $`c+a\exp(-u_{1}^{2})(1+q\cos(u_{2}))+r\sin(v_{3}u_{1})`$ | 3/10 | No |
| 3 |  $`c+a\exp[-(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}+u_{3}^{2})](1+q\cos(v_{4}))`$ | 4/12 | No |
| 4 |  $`c+au_{1}^{2}+ku_{1}^{3}+q\sin(u_{2})+r\cos(v_{3}v_{4})`$ | 4/14 | Yes |
| 5 |  $`c+a\cos(\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}+u_{3}^{2}})+q\sin(v_{4}v_{5})`$ | 5/16 | No |

Table 3: Construction of the canonical-coordinates symbolic-regression
stream. “Rel./all” gives the number of relevant and total features.

Training, development, and interpolation inputs use standardized radius
at most 1.5 for the centered variables. Extrapolation samples use radii
between 1.5 and 3.5 for Tasks 1, 2, and 4, and between 1.5 and 4.5 for
Tasks 3 and 5; the additional signed variables remain within their
training range. Training and development targets receive independent
Gaussian noise with standard deviation equal to 1% of the clean
training-target standard deviation. Both private target sets are
noise-free.

The agent submits an expression.json file containing one expression. The
restricted evaluator supports $`+,-,\times,/`$, \*\*, finite constants,
and sin, cos, exp, log, sqrt, and abs; it parses and evaluates the
expression without an LLM judge. Development $`R^{2}`$ is visible during
search. We use the arithmetic mean of interpolation and extrapolation
$`R^{2}`$. We also record each component $`R^{2}`$, normalized MSE,
expression-tree complexity, and whether symbolic simplification recovers
the generating structure. Task 4 is asymmetric because of its cubic term
and tests whether an agent verifies rather than blindly reuses an
even-symmetry assumption.

### A.4 Time-Series Forecasting

In the time series forecasting setting, we design three task family
streams with various degree of shared structure. All tasks require the
agent to submit an executable forecaster from the data we provide.

ETT cross-resolution. This stream contains, in order, ETTm1, ETTh1,
ETTh2, and ETTm2 \[[Zhou et al., 2021](#bib.bib10)\]. ETTh1 and ETTh2
use hourly resolution, while ETTm1 and ETTm2 use 15-minute resolution.
The datasets measure the same type of electricity-transformer system,
but differ in station and sampling frequency. This tests whether an
agent transfers insights about normalization, lag construction, and
periodicity while adapting context lengths and seasonal periods to the
new resolution.

PEMS traffic networks. This stream contains, in order, PEMS03, PEMS04,
PEMS07, and PEMS08 \[[Song et al., 2020](#bib.bib11)\]. Each dataset
represents traffic over a different sensor network and time period. The
components that are transferrable are for handling correlated channels,
daily and weekly seasonality, and chronological validation. The
different network prevents the agent from simply copying a fitted model.

Energy systems. This stream contains, in order, Electricity Hourly,
Australian Electricity Demand, Solar 10 Minutes, ETTh1, and ETTh2 \[[Lai
et al., 2018](#bib.bib12), [Godahewa et al., 2021](#bib.bib14), [Zhou et
al., 2021](#bib.bib10)\]. It is less homogeneous than the ETT stream.
However, it tests for forecasting practices such as periodicity
checking, seasonal baselines, scaling channels, and others.

All forecasting tasks are evaluated by RMSE on private chronological
test splits.

The three streams looks at different forms of transfer. ETT tests
adaptation within a closely related system, PEMS tests reuse across
related sensor networks, and the energy stream tests whether a
forecasting workflow survives broader domain variation.

### A.5 Realistic AutoML

The tabular AutoML setting contains two subsets of existing evaluation
benchmarks. We use a subset of SELA’s evaluation, textttcredit-g, kick,
Click_prediction_small, software-defects, and smoker-status \[[Chi et
al., 2024](#bib.bib8)\], and use a three task subset from MLE-Bench,
spaceship-titanic, tabular-playground-series-may-2022, and
tabular-playground-series-dec-2021 \[[Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)\].

In contrast to the other task families, the tasks don’t share a data
generator or a guaranteed predictive structure. For the SELA evaluation
subset, the agent controls preprocessing, feature engineering,
validation, and model selection before submitting an executable
predictor, which we evaluate using F1 on a private test set. The
MLE-Bench subset retains its competition-style prediction-submission
protocol: spaceship-titanic and the December 2021 task use accuracy,
while the May 2022 task uses ROC-AUC.

## Appendix C Agent Trace Case Studies

We present three transfer-eligible cases in which prior context visibly
changes a later search. Each figure contrasts the relevant conditions
using parsed agent messages and observable actions; hidden reasoning is
excluded. Hidden-test scores were computed after the run and were not
visible to the agent. The cases are selected for interpretability and
should not be read as a systematic or causal decomposition of the
aggregate results.

### C.1 Direct Code and Search-Policy Reuse

On the third ETT contract, stateful Sonnet recognized the same
forecasting structure as the preceding hourly task. It reused its
complete training pipeline but recalibrated ensemble weights and
rejected an overtrained iTransformer when visible development
performance worsened. The stateless agent instead built a seasonal model
from a clean start
(Figure [12](#A3.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ C.1 Direct Code and Search-Policy Reuse ‣ Appendix C Agent Trace Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

Stateful Sonnet reuses and adapts the pipeline

\[Task transition\] ‘‘Same structure as the last task. Let me compare
the actual data values to the previous task.’’ ‘‘Different data---same
structure, different values. I can reuse the exact same code.’’ \[Local
checks\] Reuse DLinear, NLinear, and iTransformer pipeline. Refit all
components, recalibrate ensemble weights, and drop the overtrained
iTransformer. \[Hidden evaluation\] First = final RMSE: 0.5319

Stateless Sonnet rebuilds from a clean start

\[Fresh session\] No earlier task transcript, code, fitted components,
or development results. \[Independent search\] Construct an
exponentially weighted same-hour seasonal model and tune its decay on
the current task. \[Hidden evaluation\] First RMSE: 0.6249 Final RMSE:
0.5962 The fresh search improves, but does not catch the stateful first
solution.

Figure 12: Direct reuse on a related ETT task. The stateful agent
explicitly reuses its earlier implementation, then recalibrates it using
current-task evidence. Its first valid solution already outperforms the
stateless agent’s final solution.

This case is direct evidence of code and search-policy reuse. It does
not show that Sonnet recovered a deeper relationship between the
underlying series; the transferable object was the working pipeline and
the procedure for adapting it.

### C.2 Matched Insights Change Initialization

For the Solar 10 Minutes task, fresh Codex sessions received either four
cards extracted from preceding tasks in the same stream or four cards
from unrelated forecasting streams. The matched cards supplied a useful
abstraction, but Codex still had to correct the task-specific period
(Figure [13](#A3.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ C.2 Matched Insights Change Initialization ‣ Appendix C Agent Trace Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

RMSE\] First: 0.5449  Final: 0.4125

\[Read 4 size-matched shuffled cards\] Cards describe unrelated

### C.3 Specific Memory Can Create Anchoring

On Energy Transformer Beta, matched Sonnet received 21 cumulative cards,

## Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies

To understand why, in certain cases, agents are not able to improve the
final results while showing signs of better search. We present different
cases that highlights different failures in search policy, model
selection, and operational adaptation.

### D.1 Dev Optimization Can Erase a Transfer Advantage

In one of Kimi K3 stateful canonical-coordinates task, it carried
forward a search procedure based on previous feature screening,
handcrafted basis construction, and greedy refinement. This policy
seemed to have helped early on by producing a useful expression early,
but subsequent optimization moved the selected expression away from the
candidate that ended generalizing the best under hidden extrapolation
(Figure [15](#A4.F15 "Figure 15 ‣ D.1 Dev Optimization Can Erase a Transfer Advantage ‣ Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

An earlier expression extrapolates well

\[Structure search\] ‘‘Breakthrough: $`\sin(x_{011}x_{012})`$ alone
gives dev 0.960!’’ \[Second valid expression\] Retain the sine-product
structure with a small set of corrections. Development $`R^{2}`$: 0.961
Hidden $`R^{2}`$: 0.593 Found after 7.3 minutes.

Dev polishing selects a fragile expression

\[Residual search\] Add quadratic, trigonometric, and absolute-value
corrections over several additional variables. ‘‘Dev R2 0.99979! The
full additive structure found. Promoting to final immediately.’’
\[Selected expression\] Development $`R^{2}`$: 0.99979 Hidden $`R^{2}`$:
$`-1.354`$ Selection regret: 41.3 reward points.

Figure 15: Within-task optimization destroys a strong transferred
initialization. Stateful Kimi finds its best hidden-generalizing
expression early, but selects a more elaborate expression because it
nearly saturates visible development $`R^{2}`$. The additional terms
fail under hidden extrapolation.

However, this failure was not due to Kimi not learning across tasks. In
fact, Kimi maintained a work log, reused its symbolic search tools, and
reached a very good solution quickly. However, what caused the failure
was the inductive bias it learned through that procedure. Small
development improvements favor flexible approximations even when the
task requires recovery of a compact generating structure. Also, the
agent overwrote a good candidate instead of using extrapolation
robustness as a separate selection objective. This example illustrate
that better agents should retain a portfolio of structurally distinct
candidates, penalize ungrounded complexity, and stress-test expressions
outside what is observed.

### D.2 Finding a Strong Candidate Is Not Enough

In one of GPT-5.6-Sol Energy Transformer Beta run, it explicitly reused
the previous task’s forecasting solution while refitting all parameters
to it. It was able to find a competitive model within five minutes and
continued to improve its model. However, the final selection generalized
much worse than both earlier attempts
(Figure [16](#A4.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ D.2 Finding a Strong Candidate Is Not Enough ‣ Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

Transfer accelerates candidate generation

\[Task transition\] ‘‘Same schema but a distinct Beta series, so I’ll
reuse only the proven modeling machinery---not its fitted state or
weights.’’ \[Task-local adaptation\] Refit every component, retune
analog neighbors, and remove a tree component that weakens the
chronological holdout. \[Second valid candidate\] Dev RMSE: 0.453 Hidden
RMSE: 0.545 Found after 4.5 minutes.

Dev selection promotes the wrong final model

\[Continued refinement\] Expand the ridge grid and repeatedly
recalibrate stacking weights. Each accepted change improves visible dev
RMSE. ‘‘The final adjacent grid also improves: official RMSE is now
0.417466. I’m promoting attempt 0010.’’ \[Hidden evaluation\] Selected
stateful RMSE: 1.049 Earlier stateful candidate: 0.545 Reset final:
0.523

Figure 16: Search succeeds, but final selection fails. Persistent state
helps GPT-5.6-Sol find a useful candidate much earlier, yielding a
$`+55.0`$-point time-convergence gain. Continued dev optimization
selects a fragile ensemble, however, producing a $`-16.9`$-point final
outcome gain.

We note that the agent did not blindly reuse the model from its previous
task. It refitted all components and validated a chronological holdout.
Yet, the holdout was insufficient to predict the hidden regime. The main
limitation for the agents is then selection robustness rather than
generating good candidates. However, a continual agent should preserve
its best simple incumbent, evaluate replacements across multiple time
splits and conditions. Thus, candidate generation and final selection
should be treated as separate research decisions.

### D.3 A Correct Operational Lesson Can Be Applied Too Strongly

In one of Opus 5 MLE-Bench run, it launched a large cross-validation run
before creating a valid artifact. At the end once the time budget was
over, there was only an empty final directory. The agent, however,
correctly diagnoses the problem and writes a persistent safe-first
policy. That policy prevents another null delivery, but its conservative
application reduces exploration and final quality on both subsequent
tasks
(Figure [17](#A4.F17 "Figure 17 ‣ D.3 A Correct Operational Lesson Can Be Applied Too Strongly ‣ Appendix D Agent Failure Case Studies ‣ From AutoResearch to Continual AutoResearch: Do Agents Learn Across Tasks?")).

The agent learns from a delivery failure

\[Task 1\] Launch a five-model, five-fold CV suite before writing a
final artifact. The process reaches the deadline; no valid submission is
produced. \[Postmortem\] ‘‘I launched a 5-model CV suite as my first
action and blocked waiting on it. The deadline passed with final
empty.’’ \[Persistent policy\] ‘‘Write a valid final within the first
$`\sim`$3 minutes, before any tuning.’’ Also cap threads and bound later
experiments.

The safe-first policy becomes too conservative

\[Task 2\] ‘‘Lesson applied: ship a valid final immediately, then
improve.’’ Train a bounded LightGBM on a 150k-row subset and produce
only two valid attempts. \[Reset comparison\] Engineer the same
character signal, train and rank-blend 11 MLPs across seeds and splits,
and produce eight valid submissions. \[Hidden ROC-AUC\] Stateful: 0.978
Reset: 0.998 Time-convergence gain: $`-85.4`$ points.

Figure 17: Operational learning improves validity but sacrifices
exploration. Opus converts a real failure into a reusable policy and
avoids another missing submission. Applied without an adaptive
exploration budget, however, the policy protects a weak fallback while
````
